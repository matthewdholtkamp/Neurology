# Coma — approach, workup & management

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: November 2026</span>

**One-liner:** an adult who cannot be roused — unresponsive to voice and to noxious
stimulation, eyes closed, no purposeful movement. For the on-call neuro / ED / ICU
provider: coma is a neurologic emergency, and the first hour decides the outcome —
stabilize, reverse what is reversible, localize with the exam, and image before the
cause declares itself.

!!! danger "🚩 Red flags — treat now, image as you go"
    | Pattern | Worry | Do now |
    |---|---|---|
    | Blown (unilateral fixed dilated) pupil, extensor posturing, Cushing response (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing), rostral–caudal decline | Uncal / transtentorial herniation, raised ICP | **Brain code:** HOB 30–45°, neck midline, hyperosmolar therapy, brief hyperventilation as bridge, neurosurgery — see Step 2 |
    | Abrupt coma + abnormal ocular motility, quadriparesis, or extensor posturing with a relatively unrevealing CT | Basilar artery occlusion | **CTA head/neck immediately** — time-critical, potentially reversible; do not wait for MRI |
    | Fever + meningismus / seizure / immunocompromise / petechiae | Bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis | Blood cultures, then **empiric antibiotics + dexamethasone (+ acyclovir if encephalitis possible) before CT or LP** |
    | Subtle twitching (face/eyelids/limbs), gaze deviation, nystagmus, fluctuating exam, or coma that persists after a convulsion stops | Nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) | **Urgent EEG / continuous EEG** — NCSE occurs in 8–30% of unexplained altered mental status |
    | Ataxic (Biot) breathing, loss of cough/gag, progressive loss of brainstem reflexes | Medullary failure / impending apnea | Airway now; ICU; do not leave the bedside |

## Diagnosis / classification

**Coma** is unarousable unresponsiveness: no eye opening, no command following, no
purposeful movement, no intelligible speech. It localizes to **either** bilateral
cerebral hemispheric dysfunction **or** dysfunction of the ascending reticular
activating system (ARAS) in the diencephalon / upper brainstem. That single fact
drives the whole exam: a focal or asymmetric finding points to a **structural**
lesion; a symmetric exam with preserved brainstem reflexes points to a
**toxic-metabolic** cause — with the exceptions listed in the pitfalls below.

**The single most useful bedside rule:** reactive pupils alongside otherwise absent
brainstem reflexes strongly favor a toxic-metabolic cause. Structural brainstem
damage usually takes out the pupillary reflex early.

### Score it — GCS and FOUR

| Scale | Components | Range | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GCS** | Eyes 1–4, Verbal 1–5, Motor 1–6 | 3–15 | Standard serial assessment, especially trauma. **Limitations:** does not test pupils, brainstem reflexes, eye movements, or respiratory drive; the verbal score is unusable once intubated. GCS 3–8 is the severe-impairment range that usually prompts airway and critical-care consideration. |
| **FOUR score** | Eye, Motor, Brainstem, Respiration — 0–4 each | 0–16 | Preferred in intubated and deeply comatose ICU patients and for brainstem evaluation; detects locked-in syndrome, which GCS misses. In ICU cohorts FOUR predicted mortality better than GCS; most patients with GCS 3 scored 1–8 on FOUR because brainstem function was preserved. |

**FOUR score in full** (document it — or the GCS subscores plus brainstem reflexes —
for every GCS 3T or deeply comatose patient):

| Domain | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Eye** | Eyelids open, tracking or blinking to command | Eyelids open but not tracking | Eyelids closed, open to loud voice | Eyelids closed, open to pain | Eyelids remain closed with pain |
| **Motor** (upper limbs) | Thumbs-up, fist, or peace sign | Localizing to pain | Flexion response to pain | Extension response to pain | No response, or generalized myoclonus status |
| **Brainstem** | Pupil and corneal reflexes present | One pupil wide and fixed | Pupil **or** corneal reflex absent | Pupil **and** corneal reflex absent | Absent pupil, corneal, **and** cough reflexes |
| **Respiration** | Not intubated, regular breathing | Not intubated, Cheyne–Stokes | Not intubated, irregular breathing | Breathes above ventilator rate | Breathes at ventilator rate, or apnea |

## Rapid approach

1. **ABCs before GCS** — hypoxia and hypotension lower the score. Assess airway
   patency, respiratory pattern, SpO₂, ventilation, secretions, cough/gag. **GCS ≤8
   triggers an immediate airway assessment but is not by itself an absolute indication
   for intubation** — intubate for inadequate oxygenation/ventilation, apnea or severe
   hypoventilation, loss of protective reflexes, inability to handle secretions, or
   progressive deterioration. A rapidly reversible cause (hypoglycemia, opioids) may
   make intubation avoidable. **Document the neuro exam — especially pupils, eye
   position/movements, corneals, motor response and laterality — before paralytics
   or sedatives when feasible.**
2. **Reverse the reversible, in parallel** — finger-stick glucose, then dextrose if
   low; thiamine if at risk; naloxone if opioid respiratory depression. Do not give
   the old universal "coma cocktail" to everyone — use each intervention per bedside
   findings and risk factors.
3. **Localize with the P-E-M-B exam** — Pupils → Eyes → Motor → Breathing/Brainstem
   (below). Pupils and eye movements carry the strongest localizing information; GCS
   quantifies severity, not anatomy.
4. **Image emergently** — noncontrast head CT (± CTA) once stable for transport.
5. **Work the four immediately treatable categories in parallel** — structural brain
   injury, nonconvulsive seizures/status, CNS infection/inflammation, toxic-metabolic
   causes — then escalate to EEG, MRI, and LP by the clock in the Workup section.

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Minutes 0–15: stabilize + reverse.** ABCs, monitor, two IVs, core temperature,
   finger-stick glucose → dextrose if <~70 mg/dL (Step 1). Brief neuro exam before
   sedation/paralysis. Collateral history immediately (EMS, family, witnesses,
   records): last known well, onset/course, preceding headache/seizure/trauma, meds
   (anticoagulants, insulin, sedatives), substance exposures, epilepsy, diabetes,
   renal/hepatic disease, malignancy.
2. **Minutes 15–60: CT + first labs + treat the threat.** Noncontrast head CT
   emergently; add CTA if basilar/large-vessel occlusion is plausible. Core labs and
   toxicology (below). Empiric antimicrobials immediately if infection is plausible
   — do not wait for CT or LP. Herniation signs → brain-code measures now.
3. **Hours 1–6: localize, then LP and EEG.** Re-examine (P-E-M-B) and trend the
   FOUR score. LP once CT excludes mass effect and coagulopathy is corrected, if
   CNS infection/inflammation is suspected. Start EEG/cEEG if the cause is still
   unclear or there are subtle seizure signs.
4. **Hours 24–48: MRI + second-line labs + re-examine.** MRI if still unexplained
   (sooner for suspected brainstem ischemia, encephalitis, PRES, diffuse axonal
   injury, or hypoxic injury). Do not label a persistently comatose patient
   "metabolic" or "postictal" without EEG + targeted MRI ± CSF.
5. **Day 3 and beyond: prognosticate multimodally, never from one exam.** After
   cardiac arrest, formal prognostic assessment at ≥72 h, off sedation, normothermic.
   Consider brain-death evaluation only when the prerequisites in Step 3 are met.

## Workup

### The clock

| Window | Do |
|---|---|
| **0–1 hour** | Core labs + toxicology; noncontrast CT (± CTA, ± C-spine imaging); empiric antimicrobials if infection possible |
| **1–6 hours** | LP once CT excludes mass effect and coagulopathy is corrected; start EEG if cause still unclear or subtle seizure signs |
| **24–48 hours** | MRI if still unexplained; second-line labs; re-examine. If CT is nondiagnostic, work a checklist: poisoning, infection, NCSE, endocrinopathy, and the rest of the toxic-metabolic panel |

### Imaging — CT vs MRI vs CTA

!!! orderset "Imaging — what, when, and why"
    - **Noncontrast head CT — obtain emergently.** First-line in unexplained coma
      after stabilization. Rapidly identifies hemorrhage, mass effect/herniation,
      hydrocephalus, large infarction/edema, and most traumatic lesions. The 2024 ACR
      Appropriateness Criteria rate it "Usually Appropriate" as initial imaging when
      intracranial pathology or a focal deficit is suspected. Also: chest X-ray,
      especially after intubation.
    - **CTA head/neck — add immediately** when basilar or other large-vessel occlusion
      is plausible (abrupt coma, abnormal ocular motility, quadriparesis, extensor
      posturing with a relatively unrevealing CT), or when arterial occlusion,
      aneurysmal SAH, dissection, or cerebral venous sinus thrombosis is suspected.
      Basilar occlusion is time-critical and potentially reversible — do not wait for
      MRI. Consider CTV for venous thrombosis.
    - **MRI brain — the key second-line test,** when CT does not explain the exam or
      the suspected lesion is in the posterior fossa/brainstem. In one ICU cohort,
      adding MRI changed management in 76% of patients with acute disorders of
      consciousness, including a revised diagnosis in 20%. Get it **within 24–48
      hours** for suspected stroke or encephalitis, as soon as the patient is stable;
      add gadolinium if infection, tumor, or inflammation is suspected. MRI first is
      reasonable in a stable patient with known malignancy, HIV, or endocarditis, or
      with suspected CNS infection/inflammation. After cardiac arrest, MRI is best on
      **days 3–5** — DWI can be normal in the first 1–2 days. MRI finds what CT
      misses: encephalitis (e.g., HSV temporal-lobe involvement), PRES, hypoxic-
      ischemic injury, diffuse axonal injury and microbleeds, Wernicke
      encephalopathy, osmotic demyelination, and subtle SAH.
    - **Do not delay treatment for imaging.** In suspected meningitis/encephalitis,
      draw blood cultures and give empiric therapy before CT.

### EEG — for nonconvulsive status epilepticus

!!! orderset "EEG — indications, duration, interpretation"
    - **Obtain urgent EEG / continuous EEG when:** coma is unexplained after initial
      CT and labs; consciousness stays impaired after a clinical seizure or
      convulsive status; subtle repetitive phenomena (eyelid/facial twitching,
      nystagmus, gaze deviation, rhythmic limb movements, episodic posturing,
      fluctuating consciousness); acute cortical injury associated with seizures
      (ICH, SAH, ischemic stroke, TBI, encephalitis, cardiac arrest); unexplained
      episodic autonomic events; or treatment with anesthetic infusions for
      refractory status. ACNS recommends continuous EEG for suspected NCSE, started
      as soon as feasible.
    - **Duration:** at least **24 hours** of monitoring is typical in comatose
      patients; consider **≥48 hours** when the patient remains comatose, is sedated,
      or has periodic/epileptiform discharges. About **80–95%** of patients ultimately
      found to have nonconvulsive seizures are detected in the first 24–48 hours,
      though seizures may appear later in comatose or sedated patients. If seizures
      are detected, continue cEEG until they have been controlled
      electrographically for at least **24 hours**. The 2HELPS2B score from the first
      hour can shorten monitoring when early risk is low.
    - **After cardiac arrest:** AHA / Neurocritical Care Society recommend starting
      EEG as early as possible and continuing **72–120 hours** if the patient does
      not wake. If continuous EEG is unavailable, do daily intermittent EEGs or
      transfer.
    - **Interpretation:** sedatives and antiseizure drugs affect the reading. EEG also
      aids diagnosis — triphasic waves suggest a metabolic cause; lateralized
      periodic discharges suggest a focal lesion. A 20–60-minute routine EEG can
      identify obvious NCSE, diffuse slowing, and triphasic morphology, but a
      negative routine EEG does not exclude NCSE.

### Lumbar puncture

!!! orderset "LP — indications and safety"
    - **Indications:** suspected meningitis or encephalitis (fever, meningismus,
      immunosuppression, or no other explanation); suspected SAH with a negative CT
      (>6 hours from onset); autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis;
      carcinomatous meningitis; unexplained coma with MRI or EEG suggestive of
      encephalitis. In immunosuppressed patients, lower the threshold and broaden
      the testing.
    - **Image before LP:** a comatose patient meets criteria for CT first. Triggers
      include altered consciousness (ESCMID: GCS <10; UK: GCS ≤12), focal deficits,
      new seizure, papilledema, or abnormal posturing. Contraindications: brain shift
      or mass effect on CT, coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia (correct first),
      anticoagulation, skin infection at the puncture site.
    - **Do not delay treatment for imaging:** draw blood cultures, then give empiric
      antibiotics plus dexamethasone — and acyclovir if encephalitis is possible —
      before CT. Waiting for imaging is linked to worse outcomes.
    - **CSF, at minimum:** opening pressure, WBC with differential, RBC, protein,
      CSF glucose with simultaneous serum glucose, Gram stain, bacterial culture.
      Then phenotype-directed: HSV-1/2 PCR, VZV PCR, enterovirus PCR, cryptococcal
      antigen, fungal/mycobacterial studies, meningitis/encephalitis multiplex PCR
      where appropriate, oligoclonal bands / IgG index, autoimmune encephalitis
      antibodies, cytology / flow cytometry, xanthochromia. **Save extra fluid frozen
      for later testing.** A very early negative HSV PCR does not completely exclude
      HSV encephalitis — if clinical/MRI suspicion remains high, repeat CSF testing.

### Laboratory testing — tiered

| Tier | Tests | Timing / trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **Immediate (all patients)** | Point-of-care glucose; ABG/VBG with lactate; co-oximetry (CO, methemoglobin); CBC; CMP (Na, Ca, Mg, phosphate, BUN/Cr, LFTs); coagulation studies; ECG; troponin; pregnancy test | On arrival |
| **Toxicology** | Serum ethanol, acetaminophen, salicylate, measured osmolality with osmolar gap, urine drug screen; levels of home medications (lithium, antiseizure drugs, baclofen) | On arrival. Urine drug screens miss synthetic opioids and many benzodiazepines, and give false results — do not rely on a negative screen alone |
| **Hepatic / endocrine** | Ammonia, TSH ± free T4, cortisol | Initial panel when the cause is unclear |
| **Infection** | Blood cultures, urinalysis + culture, chest X-ray; HIV, syphilis serology | Before antibiotics if possible |
| **Second-line** | Thiamine level (draw before treatment, but never delay treatment), B12, CK, autoimmune/paraneoplastic antibodies in serum and CSF, heavy metals, expanded toxicology (LC-MS) | When the first round of tests is unrevealing |

## Localization — the P-E-M-B exam

### Pupils

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Small, reactive | Metabolic cause (common); or bilateral pontine lesion |
| **Pinpoint** (1–2 mm), reactive | Pontine lesion, or opioid effect |
| Midposition, fixed | Midbrain lesion (or severe midbrain dysfunction) |
| Dilated, fixed, unilateral | Transtentorial (uncal) herniation or CN III lesion |
| Dilated, fixed, bilateral | Severe midbrain dysfunction or catastrophic anoxic injury |
| Dilated, reactive | Sympathetic response / mydriatic drug effect |
| **Symmetric, reactive pupils in deep coma** | Diffuse hemispheric dysfunction or toxic-metabolic cause |
| **Asymmetry or early loss of pupillary reactivity** | Structural (focal/brainstem) lesion |
| Unequal, with ptosis and eye turned "down and out" | CN III lesion — consider uncal herniation if acute |
| Horner syndrome (miosis + ptosis + anhidrosis) | Ipsilateral sympathetic interruption (hypothalamus → brainstem → cord) |
| Light–near dissociation (reacts to near but not light) | Dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) lesion |
| Fixed midposition pupils with loss of all other brainstem reflexes | Often **toxic-metabolic, with a better prognosis** — do not assume structural catastrophe from this finding alone |

**Pupil rule:** reactive pupils + otherwise absent brainstem reflexes strongly favor
a toxic-metabolic cause; asymmetric or early loss of pupillary reactivity favors
structural disease. A single-point exam is not enough — trend the pupils with the
FOUR score.

### Eye movements

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| **Roving eye movements** (slow, wandering, conjugate) | Hemispheres impaired, brainstem intact — good sign in coma |
| **Ping-pong gaze** (rapid horizontal oscillation) | Bilateral hemispheric disease with intact brainstem |
| **Ocular bobbing** (rapid downward, slow upward) | Pontine lesion |
| **Ocular dipping** (slow downward, rapid upward) | Anoxic encephalopathy / diffuse cortical dysfunction |
| **Conjugate gaze toward the lesion side** (eyes look toward the lesion) | Acute frontal lobe lesion ("looks toward the lesion"); **overcome by doll's-eyes** (oculocephalic) maneuver |
| **Conjugate gaze away from the lesion** | Pontine lesion ("looks away"); **cannot** be overcome by doll's-eyes |
| **"Wrong-way" eyes** (conjugate deviation away from the side of weakness) | Thalamic hemorrhage |
| **Oculocephalic reflex (doll's eyes) intact** | Brainstem and vestibular pathways intact — a good prognostic sign |
| **Skew deviation** (vertical misalignment) | Brainstem lesion (commonly pontine) or cerebellar disease |
| **Internuclear ophthalmoplegia** (adduction failure) | MLF lesion (brainstem) |
| **Oculovestibular reflex (cold caloric) absent** | Vestibular nerve/brainstem pathway or midbrain/pons dysfunction |
| **Ocular movements absent** | Brainstem dysfunction — a marker for brain-death evaluation |

### Motor responses

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Symmetric withdrawal / localizing | Intact motor pathways — a diffuse/toxic-metabolic cause |
| **Decorticate posturing** (flexion) | Lesion above the midbrain |
| **Decerebrate posturing** (extension) | Midbrain / upper pontine lesion — worse |
| **Asymmetric motor response** (one side localizes, the other postures or is flaccid) | **Focal structural lesion** — a hemispheric mass or unilateral injury |
| **Flaccid, no motor response** | Severe bilateral hemispheric, brainstem, or spinal cord injury; or the expected effect of sedatives/paralytics |
| **Extensor posturing that changes with stimulation** | Uncal herniation or acute structural shift |
| **Acute flaccid quadriparesis + coma** | Brainstem lesion, or high cervical cord injury |
| **Unilateral flaccid limb** | Focal lesion or peripheral process |
| **Generalized myoclonus** (especially post-arrest) | Consider status myoclonus, or myoclonic status epilepticus |

### Breathing patterns

| Pattern | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Cheyne–Stokes (crescendo–decrescendo) | Bilateral hemispheric / diencephalic dysfunction, or congestive heart failure |
| Central neurogenic hyperventilation (>40/min sustained) | Midbrain / upper pontine lesion |
| Apneustic breathing (prolonged inspiration) | Pontine lesion |
| Cluster breathing | Lower pontine / upper medullary lesion |
| **Ataxic (Biot) breathing** (irregular, gasping) | **Medullary lesion — ominous, impending apnea** |
| Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid) | Metabolic acidosis (DKA, uremia, sepsis) |
| Slow, shallow breathing | Opioid / sedative effect, or medullary failure |

### Herniation — rostral-to-caudal progression

In transtentorial herniation the sequence is: unilateral pupil dilation → loss of
consciousness and eye movements → extensor posturing → midposition fixed pupils
with loss of oculocephalic reflex → irregular breathing. In downward herniation,
the pupil changes first (third nerve compression); in central herniation the
respiratory pattern deteriorates first. **Central herniation:** early confusion →
pinpoint pupils → apneusis → ataxic breathing. **Tonsillar herniation:** bradycardia
and respiratory arrest can occur with relatively preserved pupils.

## Management

!!! orderset "Step 1 — First minutes: stabilize and reverse (every patient, immediately)"
    - **Airway / breathing:** assess and secure the airway (intubate if indicated
      above). Document the neuro exam before paralytics or sedatives when feasible.
      If trauma is possible, use rapid-sequence induction with in-line cervical-spine
      stabilization. Target normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg) unless herniation is
      suspected — then briefly hyperventilate to **PaCO₂ 30–35 mmHg** as a bridge.
    - **Circulation:** establish two IV lines. Assess for hemorrhage, shock, or sepsis.
      Maintain perfusion; correct hypotension promptly (avoid both hypo- and
      hyperperfusion).
    - **Glucose (check immediately, in every comatose patient):** point-of-care glucose.
      If **<~70 mg/dL** or symptomatic: **D50 25 g IV** (50 mL) **or D10 in 100 mL
      boluses**; repeat until the patient wakes or glucose normalizes. Recheck within
      **15 minutes**. If no IV access: **glucagon 1 mg IM**. For sulfonylurea-induced
      hypoglycemia: dextrose infusion + **octreotide 50–100 mcg SC/IV q6–8h**, observe
      ≥24 hours (prolonged hypoglycemia risk). Do not delay dextrose to wait for
      thiamine.
    - **Thiamine (if at risk — malnutrition, alcohol use, possible Wernicke):**
      **thiamine 100 mg IV** (up to **500 mg IV** if Wernicke encephalopathy is
      suspected) — give before or with dextrose.
    - **Naloxone (if opioid toxicity with respiratory depression):** **naloxone
      0.04–0.4 mg IV** titrated to adequate breathing, up to **2 mg**; repeat doses
      as needed. Goal is breathing, not arousal. Fentanyl analogues may need larger
      doses. Xylazine does not respond. Naloxone wears off before long-acting
      opioids — monitor for recurrent respiratory depression and consider an
      infusion at ~two-thirds of the effective reversal dose per hour.
    - **Seizure (if convulsing):** **lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV** (max 4 mg/dose, may
      repeat once) **or midazolam 10 mg IM** (>40 kg) **or diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV**
      (max 10 mg). Treat hypoglycemia and hyponatremia first.
    - **What NOT to do in Step 1:** do not give **flumazenil** empirically (seizure
      risk in chronic benzodiazepine users and co-ingestants); do not give the
      universal "coma cocktail" to every patient; do not assume ethanol explains
      the coma until structural injury and other toxic-metabolic causes are
      excluded; do not delay CT for a full laboratory panel.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — Cause-directed management (the four categories, in parallel)"
    - **Herniation / acute intracranial hypertension (brain code):** head of bed
      **30–45°**, head/neck midline, adequate analgesia/sedation, ventilator
      synchrony. **Hyperosmolar therapy: mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg IV or hypertonic saline
      23.4% 30 mL central / 3% 250–500 mL** (hypertonic saline preferred first —
      see the [ICP page](increased-icp.md)). Limits: serum Na ≥155 mEq/L,
      osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg, osmolar gap ≥20. Target CPP 60–70 mmHg. Treat
      hyperthermia; maintain euvolemia. **Dexamethasone only for vasogenic edema
      from tumor or abscess** — harmful in TBI. Evacuate mass lesions; EVD for
      obstructive hydrocephalus. For refractory ICP: barbiturate coma to burst
      suppression, mild hypothermia 35–36 °C, or decompressive craniectomy.
    - **Status epilepticus:** first-line benzodiazepine (above). Second-line at
      ~20 min — ESETT showed equivalence, so choose by patient factors: **levetiracetam
      60 mg/kg IV max 4,500 mg**; **fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg IV max 1,500 mg PE** at
      ≤150 mg PE/min; **valproate 40 mg/kg IV max 3,000 mg** (avoid in liver disease,
      POLG, pregnancy, hyperammonemia). Refractory = intubate + **midazolam, propofol,
      or ketamine infusion** (pentobarbital if those fail) to seizure or burst
      suppression. Super-refractory ≥24 h → consider immunotherapy if autoimmune
      etiology is suspected. **When convulsions stop but consciousness doesn't return
      as expected, obtain urgent cEEG — NCSE may be continuing.**
    - **Hypoglycemia (severe, recurrent, or drug-induced):** dextrose as in Step 1;
      identify the cause (sulfonylurea, insulin, sepsis, liver failure, endogenous
      hyperinsulinism). Sulfonylurea: dextrose infusion + octreotide (above),
      observe ≥24 h. Rapid correction of a reversible toxic-metabolic cause is one
      of the strongest predictors of a good outcome.
    - **Toxic / metabolic:** supportive ICU care first, specific antidote when
      indicated, early Poison Center / medical toxicology involvement for serious or
      uncertain poisoning. **TCA with wide QRS:** sodium bicarbonate **1–2 mEq/kg
      IV boluses**. **CO poisoning:** 100% oxygen ± hyperbaric oxygen; obtain
      co-oximetry (standard pulse oximetry may appear normal). **Toxic alcohols:**
      fomepizole ± hemodialysis. **Lithium, salicylate, valproate, barbiturates:**
      hemodialysis. **Acetaminophen:** N-acetylcysteine. **Organophosphates:** atropine
      + pralidoxime. **Adrenal crisis:** hydrocortisone **100 mg IV**. **Myxedema:**
      IV levothyroxine + hydrocortisone first. **DKA/HHS:** fluids + insulin, slow
      osmolality correction. **Uremia:** dialysis. **Hypercapnia:** ventilation.
      **Hypothermia:** rewarming. **Sepsis:** source control + early antibiotics.
      **Hepatic encephalopathy:** treat precipitants + hyperammonemia; acute liver
      failure with cerebral edema is a distinct neurocritical emergency.
      **Hypernatremia/hyperosmolar states:** restore circulating volume and correct
      the water deficit at a controlled rate while treating the underlying cause.
    - **Infection (meningitis/encephalitis):** blood cultures, then empiric antibiotics
      plus dexamethasone — and acyclovir if encephalitis is possible — **before CT or
      LP** (doses per local meningitis/encephalitis protocol; not pinned in this run's
      research — see footer). Never delay antimicrobials for imaging or LP.
    - **Structural (hemorrhage, mass, hydrocephalus):** correct coagulopathy, reverse
      anticoagulants, neurosurgery consultation early. Suspected basilar occlusion →
      CTA immediately, stroke team, thrombectomy consideration — see the
      [stroke page](../stroke/index.md).

!!! orderset "Step 3 — Escalate: ICU, cEEG, MRI, LP, and prognostication"
    - **ICU admission** for coma that persists after initial stabilization, for
      herniation/ICP management, refractory seizures, or any patient requiring airway
      support or frequent neuro checks.
    - **Continuous EEG** if the cause is unclear after initial workup, after convulsive
      status, or with subtle seizure signs (above). Continue until seizures are
      controlled electrographically for ≥24 h.
    - **MRI** if CT is unrevealing or the cause is still unclear at 24–48 h (above).
    - **LP** once CT excludes mass effect and coagulopathy is corrected (above).
    - **Prognosis — multimodal, never from a single exam:** etiology is the strongest
      determinant. Toxic/metabolic causes (including early-treated hypoglycemia) carry
      a good prognosis even in deep coma with absent brainstem reflexes. **Avoid the
      self-fulfilling prophecy.** After cardiac arrest, formal prognostic assessment at
      **≥72 h**, off sedation, normothermic; poor outcome is likely with **≥2** of:
      bilaterally absent pupillary + corneal reflexes at ≥72 h; bilaterally absent N20
      SSEP; highly malignant EEG after 24 h; status myoclonus within 72 h; NSE
      **>60 µg/L** at 48 and/or 72 h; diffuse anoxic injury on CT/MRI. 0–1 predictors
      = indeterminate (~50%) — continue observation. IMPACT is population-level only.
      Features favoring recovery: rapid correction of a reversible cause; preserved
      pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic/vestibular and cough reflexes; improving motor
      responses and spontaneous eye opening; reactive/continuous EEG background.
    - **Brain-death evaluation (BD/DNC):** consider **only** with coma + absent
      brainstem reflexes + no respiratory drive **and** prerequisites met per the
      2023 AAN/AAP/CNS/SCCM guideline: known catastrophic permanent mechanism with
      consistent imaging; observation **≥24 h** after hypoxic-ischemic injury
      (≥24 h after rewarming to ≥36 °C if hypothermia ≤35.5 °C); core temp **≥36 °C**;
      SBP **≥100 mmHg**; no severe metabolic/endocrine derangement; sedatives cleared
      (~5 half-lives or levels); neuromuscular blockade reversed (train-of-four).
      Do not evaluate if there is any consciousness, preserved brainstem reflex,
      brain-mediated movement, or spontaneous breathing. Ancillary blood-flow studies
      (nuclear perfusion, catheter angiography, transcranial Doppler) only when the
      exam or apnea test cannot be completed or confounders cannot be excluded. Full
      exam and apnea-test details: see the [brain death page](brain-death.md).

!!! warning "Avoid"
    - Do **not** intubate reflexively for GCS ≤8 without assessing oxygenation,
      ventilation, and protective reflexes — but do not delay when the airway is
      failing.
    - Do **not** give flumazenil empirically, and do **not** use the universal coma
      cocktail as a reflex.
    - Do **not** label a comatose patient "metabolic" or "postictal" without EEG and
      targeted MRI ± CSF.
    - Do **not** delay empiric antimicrobials for CT or LP in suspected CNS infection.
    - Do **not** evaluate for brain death before the prerequisites are met, and do
      **not** use a single early exam, a single biomarker, or a low GCS alone to
      prognosticate — especially after cardiac arrest.
    - Do **not** attribute coma to ethanol (or to any single cause) until structural
      injury and other toxic-metabolic causes are excluded.
    - Do **not** wait for MRI to treat suspected basilar occlusion or herniation.

!!! tip "TRICARE — coma workup and acute ICU drugs"
    - The acute agents on this page are generic inpatient/ED drugs — dextrose,
      thiamine, naloxone, lorazepam, levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, valproate,
      mannitol, hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate, hydrocortisone — typically
      formulary without prior authorization as inpatient agents. Maintenance
      antiseizure drugs (levetiracetam, valproate) are generic formulary options
      for outpatient continuation; newer or branded agents are the ones that
      typically require PA or step therapy.
    - The TRICARE tier status of these acute IV agents was **not individually
      re-verified against the current formulary in this run** — verify at
      [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst/#/).
    - For service members, acute inpatient care and ICU drugs are covered under the
      military treatment facility / TRICARE system; the practical bottleneck is
      usually not the drug — it is getting the right exam, EEG, and MRI in time.

## Disposition

- **ICU admission** for persistent coma, herniation/ICP management, refractory
  seizures, or airway support. Continuous EEG, frequent neuro checks (FOUR score
  trending), ICP management as needed.
- **Step-down / ward** only for rapidly reversing causes (hypoglycemia, opioid
  toxicity) with return to baseline, normal imaging, and a clear plan for the
  underlying cause.
- **Goals-of-care discussion early** when the cause is catastrophic, the patient is
  elderly or has advanced directives, or prognostication is poor — but never
  prognosticate from a single early exam, and never withdraw support based on a
  single finding.
- **Follow-up:** after discharge, address the underlying cause — epilepsy
  management, substance-use treatment, metabolic workup, driving restrictions
  after seizures, and rehabilitation for any residual deficits.

!!! military "Military medicine — coma, retention, and deployability"
    - **Profile:** coma from any cause requires a temporary profile during recovery;
      persistent deficits, cognitive impairment, or seizures after recovery generally
      require a permanent profile and a Medical Evaluation Board (MEB) referral.
    - **Retention:** retention standards (AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03) address seizures
      and cognitive impairment as the usual disqualifying sequelae; specific paragraph
      citations were not verified in this run — verify against the current regulation
      before counseling a service member.
    - **Deployability:** prolonged coma, anoxic injury, or persistent neurologic
      deficits are generally non-deployable; a service member who recovers fully
      from a reversible toxic-metabolic cause (e.g., hypoglycemia) without sequelae
      is typically returnable to duty once the underlying cause is treated.
    - **Fitness for duty:** after anoxic brain injury or prolonged coma, fitness
      determination is usually via the Integrated Disability Evaluation System
      (IDES) once recovery has plateaued (DoDI 1332.18).
    - **Aeromedical:** any history of coma with neurologic sequelae or seizures is
      generally disqualifying for flight duties — verify against current aeromedical
      policy before counseling.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

**What this page covers:** the first-hour approach to an adult in coma — stabilize,
reverse the reversible, localize with the P-E-M-B exam, image emergently, then work
the four immediately treatable categories (structural, seizures, infection,
toxic-metabolic) with EEG, MRI, and LP by the clock.

**What the evidence says:**

- The **2024 ACR Appropriateness Criteria** (Soares et al., JACR 2024) rate noncontrast
  head CT as "Usually Appropriate" as the first imaging test in unexplained coma, with
  CTA added for suspected basilar or large-vessel occlusion — a finding mirrored in the
  NCS ENLS coma pathway.
- **EEG is not optional in unexplained coma.** NCSE occurs in 8–30% of unclear altered
  mental status; about 80–95% of nonconvulsive seizures are detected within the first
  24–48 hours of continuous EEG. Both sources in this run agreed that a persistently
  comatose patient should not be labeled "metabolic" or "postictal" without EEG.
- **Status epilepticus dosing is settled.** The ESETT trial showed levetiracetam,
  fosphenytoin, and valproate are equivalent as second-line agents — choice should be
  by comorbidity, not by a false efficacy hierarchy. First-line benzodiazepine dosing
  (lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg, midazolam 10 mg IM, diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg) is consistent
  across the ACEP and AES guidelines.
- **Herniation management is a bundle, not a single drug:** HOB 30–45°, midline neck,
  CPP 60–70 mmHg, hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg or hypertonic saline),
  normocapnia (PaCO₂ 30–35 only as a brief bridge), and dexamethasone only for
  vasogenic edema from tumor or abscess. See the [ICP page](increased-icp.md) for the
  full tiered approach.
- **Prognostication after cardiac arrest is multimodal and time-gated.** The ERC-ESICM
  2021 and NCS 2023 guidance requires ≥72 hours off sedation and normothermic, with
  ≥2 of six predictors (absent pupillary + corneal reflexes at ≥72 h, absent N20 SSEP,
  highly malignant EEG after 24 h, status myoclonus within 72 h, NSE >60 µg/L, diffuse
  anoxic injury on imaging) before a poor outcome is considered likely — and 0–1
  predictors means indeterminate, not good.
- **Brain death is a clinical diagnosis with strict prerequisites** under the 2023
  AAN/AAP/CNS/SCCM consensus guideline — known catastrophic mechanism, ≥24 h
  observation after hypoxic-ischemic injury, core temp ≥36 °C, SBP ≥100 mmHg, sedatives
  cleared (~5 half-lives), neuromuscular blockade reversed. One point of disagreement
  between this run's two sources: the MAP threshold (OpenEvidence: SBP ≥100 or MAP
  ≥60; ChatGPT: SBP ≥100 and MAP ≥75) — the page states SBP ≥100 mmHg, which both
  sources agree on, and defers the MAP target to local protocol.

**What remains uncertain:** empiric meningitis antibiotic and acyclovir dosing was not
pinned in this run's research (doses per local protocol, flagged above); the MAP
threshold for BD/DNC prerequisites differs between sources (see above); and the
specific AR 40-501 paragraph citations for retention after coma were not verified
(flagged in the military box).
