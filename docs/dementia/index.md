# Approach to cognitive impairment

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: August 2026</span>

**One-liner:** cognitive complaints are common and most of them are **not** neurodegenerative
disease — so the job is to **separate the treatable from the degenerative, name the syndrome before
you name the disease, and only then decide whether a biomarker changes anything.**

**Who this is for:** the provider handed "memory problems" in clinic, and the one who has to decide
whether this is depression, drugs, sleep apnoea, a rapidly progressive dementia that needs admitting
tonight, or Alzheimer's disease. In a military setting the same complaint usually arrives as
**post-concussive cognitive symptoms in a young service member** — that is a different problem and
lives on the [TBI cognitive page](../tbi/cognitive.md).

!!! danger "🚩 Red flags — do not work this up as slow neurodegeneration"
    | Pattern | Worry | Do now |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Progression over weeks to months**, not years | **Rapidly progressive dementia** — CJD, autoimmune encephalitis, malignancy, infection | **Admit or expedite.** Go to [rapidly progressive dementia](rapidly-progressive.md) |
    | **Age <65**, especially <60 | Young-onset — FTD, genetic, autoimmune, metabolic, HIV, neurosyphilis | Broader work-up; refer to behavioural neurology |
    | **Early falls, parkinsonism, or visual hallucinations** | **[Dementia with Lewy bodies](lewy-body.md)** | **Do not give a typical antipsychotic** — see the sensitivity warning |
    | **Personality or conduct change dominating memory** | **[Frontotemporal dementia](frontotemporal.md)** | Collateral history is the test; imaging supports |
    | **Gait disturbance early, urinary incontinence, "magnetic" gait** | NPH, [vascular](vascular.md), B12 deficiency, cord pathology | Imaging + B12/copper; consider gait-responsive causes |
    | **Fluctuating consciousness, inattention, acute onset** | **Delirium, not dementia** | Find the cause; do not diagnose dementia in a delirious patient |
    | **Headache, seizure, focal deficit, papilloedema** | Mass, subdural, vasculitis | **Image before anything else** |

    **Two rules that prevent most diagnostic errors:** **never diagnose dementia during a
    delirium**, and **never diagnose Alzheimer's disease in a patient you have not screened for
    depression, drugs, sleep apnoea and thyroid or B12 deficiency.**

## Delirium, MCI, dementia — get the level right first

| | Onset | Attention | Course | Consciousness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Delirium** | Hours–days | **Impaired — the defining feature** | Fluctuating | Altered |
| **Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)** | Months–years | Usually intact | Slowly progressive | Normal |
| **Dementia (major neurocognitive disorder)** | Years | Intact until late | Progressive | Normal |

- **MCI = objective cognitive decline with preserved independence** in instrumental activities.
  **Dementia = cognitive decline that interferes with independence.** The line is functional, not a
  test score, and **the collateral historian draws it, not the patient.**
- **MCI is not a disease** — it is a stage with many causes. A meaningful proportion reverts to
  normal, which is exactly why the treatable-cause screen comes before the biomarker.
- **Subjective cognitive decline** (complaint without objective deficit) carries a modestly
  increased risk but is not a diagnosis. Follow it; do not work it up as disease.

## Name the syndrome before you name the disease

The single most useful diagnostic move is to ask **which cognitive domain went first**, and take
that answer from someone who lives with the patient.

| Leading domain | Think first | Page |
|---|---|---|
| **Episodic memory** (repeats questions, loses the thread of recent events) | Alzheimer's disease | [AD](alzheimers.md) |
| **Visuospatial, attention that fluctuates, visual hallucinations** | Dementia with Lewy bodies | [DLB/PDD](lewy-body.md) |
| **Behaviour, personality, disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy** | Behavioural-variant FTD | [FTD](frontotemporal.md) |
| **Language** — effortful speech, or loss of word meaning | Primary progressive aphasia | [FTD](frontotemporal.md) |
| **Processing speed and executive function, stepwise or with vascular risk** | Vascular cognitive impairment | [Vascular](vascular.md) |
| **Any of the above, but over weeks to months** | **Rapidly progressive dementia** | [RPD](rapidly-progressive.md) |

**Mixed pathology is the norm, not the exception**, particularly over 80 — vascular disease and
Alzheimer's pathology coexist far more often than either occurs alone. Say "predominantly X" rather
than pretending to a single diagnosis you cannot support.

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Rule out delirium.** Acute, fluctuating, inattentive → find the cause. Stop here.
2. **Take a collateral history.** Non-negotiable. What went first, when, and what can they no longer
   do? A patient's own account of their memory is the least reliable data in the encounter.
3. **Screen objectively** — MoCA or MMSE, plus a functional scale. Record the raw score *and* the
   education adjustment.
4. **Screen the treatable contributors before anything expensive:** depression, medications, sleep
   apnoea, alcohol, thyroid, B12. Most "reversible dementia" is one of these.
5. **Lab panel + structural imaging** (MRI preferred) — see Workup.
6. **Decide the syndrome** from the leading domain, and route to the disease page.
7. **Only then ask whether a biomarker changes management.** It usually does not, unless
   anti-amyloid therapy is genuinely on the table.
8. **Address safety and capacity from the first visit** — driving, firearms, finances, medications,
   and the caregiver.
9. **Military disposition** — and note that **dementia is disqualifying upon diagnosis**, which is
   unlike almost everything else in this reference.

## Workup

!!! orderset "Every patient — the baseline set"
    - **Labs:** CBC, CMP, **TSH**, **vitamin B12** (add methylmalonic acid if borderline), HbA1c,
      lipids. **Add: HIV and RPR/treponemal testing** where risk or young onset.
    - **Structural imaging — MRI brain without contrast** is preferred (CT only if MRI is
      contraindicated or unavailable). You are looking for hippocampal and lobar atrophy patterns,
      **vascular burden**, subdural collections, hydrocephalus, and mass lesions.
    - **Medication review** — every visit. See the Avoid box.
    - **Depression screen** (PHQ-9 or GDS) and a **sleep apnoea screen** (STOP-BANG).
    - **Objective cognitive testing** — MoCA is more sensitive than MMSE for MCI and for executive
      and visuospatial deficits; MMSE is more familiar and better validated longitudinally. Use one
      consistently and record the version.

!!! orderset "Add when the picture demands it"
    - **Young onset (<65), atypical, or rapid** → expand: autoimmune and paraneoplastic panels,
      heavy metals, copper/ceruloplasmin, ammonia, EEG, CSF. See [RPD](rapidly-progressive.md).
    - **Neuropsychological testing** — when the syndrome is unclear, when the deficit is mild and
      the stakes are high (capacity, occupation, a service member's fitness), or to establish a
      baseline before treatment.
    - **EEG** — suspected seizures, fluctuation, or RPD.
    - **DaT-SPECT** — supports [DLB](lewy-body.md) when parkinsonism is equivocal.
    - **Polysomnography** — REM sleep behaviour disorder, or suspected OSA.

!!! warning "Reversible and contributing causes — screen before you diagnose"
    Truly *reversible* dementia is uncommon, but **treatable contributors are extremely common and
    routinely missed.** Every one of these is worth more than a biomarker in an unselected patient:

    - **Depression** — the classic mimic. Prominent complaint with relatively preserved objective
      performance, poor effort, "I don't know" answers. Treat and reassess.
    - **Medications** — anticholinergics (oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics),
      **benzodiazepines**, opioids, sedating antihistamines, high anticholinergic burden overall.
    - **Obstructive sleep apnoea** and chronic sleep deprivation.
    - **Alcohol** and other substances.
    - **B12 deficiency**, hypothyroidism.
    - **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** — gait first, then urinary, then cognition. Gait must lead.
    - **Subdural haematoma** — especially in an anticoagulated or fall-prone patient.
    - **Neurosyphilis, HIV**, and in the right setting **CNS Whipple, Lyme**.
    - **Hearing and vision loss** — under-treated, and they masquerade as cognitive decline.

## Biomarkers — what they answer, and when to ask

**The question a biomarker answers is narrow: "is Alzheimer's pathology present?"** It does not tell
you the syndrome, it does not exclude co-pathology, and in most patients it does not change what you
do. Order it when the answer would change management.

| Test | What it shows | Practical note |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma p-tau217** | Alzheimer's pathology, with the best performance of the blood markers | The most consequential recent change in this field — see Discover. **Performance falls in unselected primary-care populations**; use it in the right pretest setting |
| **CSF Aβ42/40 ratio + p-tau181 or p-tau217** | Amyloid and tau pathology | Long-established; requires LP |
| **Amyloid PET** | Fibrillar amyloid burden | Confirms pathology; **required or expected before anti-amyloid therapy** |
| **Tau PET** | Tau distribution and stage | Correlates with symptoms better than amyloid does |
| **FDG-PET** | Regional hypometabolism pattern | Helps separate **AD from FTD** when the clinical picture is ambiguous |
| **DaT-SPECT** | Presynaptic dopaminergic deficit | Supports **DLB**; does not diagnose AD |

!!! warning "A positive amyloid biomarker is not a diagnosis of dementia"
    Amyloid pathology is present in a substantial minority of **cognitively normal older adults**.
    A positive test in a patient without objective impairment tells you about risk, not disease —
    and it cannot be un-told. **Do not order amyloid biomarkers in a patient you would not treat**,
    and counsel before you test, not after.

## Management — the frame

Disease-specific treatment lives on the disease pages: **[Alzheimer's](alzheimers.md)** (including
anti-amyloid therapy), **[DLB/PDD](lewy-body.md)**, **[FTD](frontotemporal.md)**,
**[vascular](vascular.md)**. What follows applies to everyone.

!!! orderset "Step 1 — the things that work regardless of diagnosis"
    - **Stop or reduce the cognition-toxic drugs.** Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, sedating
      antihistamines, opioids where possible. This is the highest-yield prescribing act on the page.
    - **Treat the contributors** — depression, sleep apnoea, pain, hearing and vision loss.
      **Hearing aids matter**; under-treated hearing loss is a modifiable risk factor.
    - **Vascular risk factor control** — BP, lipids, diabetes, smoking. Relevant in *every* dementia,
      not just [vascular](vascular.md), because mixed pathology is the norm.
    - **Exercise, social engagement, and structured routine.**
    - **Caregiver support and education from the first visit** — caregiver burden predicts
      institutionalization more reliably than the patient's test score.

!!! warning "Avoid — and this list is as important as anything you prescribe"
    - **Benzodiazepines** — worsen cognition, increase falls, and are hard to stop.
    - **Anticholinergics** — oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics, older antipsychotics.
      Cumulative anticholinergic burden is a modifiable risk.
    - **Typical antipsychotics in suspected [DLB](lewy-body.md)** — severe sensitivity reactions.
    - **Antipsychotics generally for behavioural symptoms** — all carry a **boxed warning for
      increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis.** Non-pharmacological
      management first, every time.
    - **Do not treat an amyloid biomarker.** Treat a patient with a syndrome.

## Safety, capacity and the conversation

- **Driving** — ask at every visit, and ask the collateral historian separately. Dementia diagnosis
  alone is not automatic revocation, but **moderate dementia is incompatible with driving.** Know
  your state reporting requirements.
- **Firearms** — in a military and veteran population this must be asked directly and early, framed
  as storage planning while the patient can still participate in the decision.
- **Finances** — vulnerability to fraud and financial mismanagement often precedes the diagnosis.
- **Advance planning and capacity** — the window for the patient to express their own wishes closes.
  **Raise durable power of attorney and advance directives at the first visit, not the fifth.**
- **Say the diagnosis.** Patients and families overwhelmingly want to be told. Vague euphemism
  transfers the burden of uncertainty onto the family without sparing anyone.

!!! tip "TRICARE — cognitive impairment work-up"
    - **The symptomatic drugs are generic and formulary** — donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine,
      memantine. Start there; nothing on this page requires a prior authorization.
    - **The expensive decisions are diagnostic, not therapeutic.** **Amyloid PET, tau PET and
      plasma biomarker assays** are where coverage questions arise, and coverage has moved quickly —
      **check current policy before you order, not after.**
    - **Anti-amyloid monoclonals are a different order of magnitude** — high-cost specialty
      infusions with their own coverage, site-of-care and MRI-monitoring requirements. Those are
      covered on the [Alzheimer's page](alzheimers.md).
    - **Neuropsychological testing** frequently needs authorization; the referral question and the
      clinical justification matter more than the CPT code.
    - **Verify current coverage at the [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst)
      and with the regional contractor before counselling — DoD P&T revises criteria without
      notice.**

!!! military "Military medicine — cognitive impairment and dementia"
    *Framework — verify against current **AR 40-501**, **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 and Vol 2**,
    **DoDI 1332.18**, and service aeromedical policy. See
    [Profiles, MEB/MAR2 & deployability](../military/deployability.md).*

    - **Get the population right first.** True neurodegenerative dementia is **rare on active
      duty**. In an MTF the overwhelming majority of dementia care is for **retirees and
      dependents**, where the questions are diagnostic, caregiver and coverage questions rather
      than fitness ones. **In a young service member, cognitive complaints are far more likely to
      be post-concussive, sleep, mood, substance or medication-related** — start at
      [TBI — cognitive](../tbi/cognitive.md), not here.
    - **Retention — dementia is one of only two conditions in the neurology standard that act
      *upon diagnosis*.** **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.26.d** carries the sentence *"Paragraph
      5.26.a. does not apply"* — the same carve-out as **epilepsy (5.26.k)** and nothing else in
      5.26. **There is no "persists despite appropriate treatment" prong to argue.** The Army
      counterpart is the catch-all at **AR 40-501 3-31k**, closing on the **para 3-1** definition.
      *This is the opposite posture from [MG](../nmj/index.md), [LEMS](../nmj/lems.md) and
      [botulism](../nmj/botulism.md), where the 5.26.a gate is the whole argument — do not import
      that reasoning here.*
    - **Because it acts on diagnosis, the diagnosis itself must be solid.** A referral built on a
      screening score and a worried spouse will not survive review, and the cost of being wrong is
      a career. **Document the collateral history, objective neuropsychological testing, structural
      imaging, and the exclusion of the treatable contributors** — depression, medications, sleep
      apnoea, substances, endocrine and nutritional causes — *before* the word dementia enters the
      record.
    - **Profile (DA Form 3349).** Profiles on the **S** factor where the impairment is
      psychiatric/cognitive in character, and on **P** where it reflects a neurologic condition —
      state which and why. **Temporary** while the work-up and the treatable-cause trial run;
      **permanent** once the diagnosis is established. **A permanent 3 or 4 is itself the DES
      referral** (AR 40-501 3-4). Section 4 limits should address **weapons access, driving,
      independent duty, and safety-sensitive tasks** explicitly.
    - **EPTS / LOD.** Onset dating is genuinely hard here — decline is insidious and often first
      visible in performance records rather than in medicine. **Review NCOERs/OERs, ACFT trend,
      counselling statements and prior PHAs**, and date the first attributable functional change.
      In young-onset cases ask specifically about **family history**, since several dementias are
      autosomal dominant and that raises pre-service questions.
    - **MEB / DES.** Refer on diagnosis — there is no MRDP argument to wait out, because the
      condition is disqualifying on diagnosis. Document **objective testing, functional impact
      corroborated by the chain of command, and safety concerns.** **Whether the Soldier is found
      fit is the PEB's decision — write the standard and the evidence, not the outcome.**
    - **Deployability: non-deployable.** Beyond the cognitive impairment itself — impaired judgment
      under stress, weapons access, sleep deprivation and heat as decompensating factors, and the
      absence of any capacity for independent duty.
    - **Aeromedical: disqualifying.** The accession standards imported by **AR 40-501 4-27** reach
      this directly, and any cognitive diagnosis ends flying-duty qualification pending **USAAMA**
      adjudication. The same applies to **ATC and UAS** duties.
    - **The TBI overlap needs saying plainly.** Service members with repetitive head impact exposure
      ask whether they are developing **CTE**. **Traumatic encephalopathy syndrome (TES) is a
      research construct, CTE is a post-mortem neuropathological diagnosis, and neither can be
      diagnosed in a living patient.** Counsel honestly, treat the treatable contributors, and see
      [TBI — work-up and return to duty](../tbi/index.md). Do not let that conversation become an
      undocumented dementia diagnosis.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

**The field changed at the point of diagnosis before it changed at the point of treatment.** For
thirty years Alzheimer's disease was a clinical diagnosis confirmed at autopsy. Now **amyloid and
tau pathology can be detected in life** — first with PET, then CSF, and now with **blood-based
markers led by plasma p-tau217**, which performs closest to the CSF standard. That has two
consequences the clinic feels immediately: the diagnosis can be biologically confirmed rather than
inferred, and it can be confirmed in people whose symptoms do not warrant treatment. **A test that
is easy to order is easy to over-order**, and the pretest probability in front of you determines
whether the result means anything.

**Anti-amyloid therapy is real, modest, and demanding.** Lecanemab and donanemab genuinely remove
amyloid and genuinely slow decline — by margins that are statistically robust and clinically
modest — at the cost of **ARIA**, infusion or injection burden, **ApoE ε4 genotyping**, and serial
MRI. Whether that trade is worth it is a conversation, not a protocol, and it belongs to the patient.
The details, including the **ARIA monitoring schedule and the interaction with cerebral amyloid
angiopathy**, are on the [Alzheimer's page](alzheimers.md) and the
[CAA page](../stroke/cerebral-amyloid-angiopathy.md).

**Meanwhile the highest-yield interventions remain unglamorous.** Deprescribing anticholinergics and
benzodiazepines, treating sleep apnoea, correcting hearing loss, managing vascular risk, and
supporting the caregiver do more for more patients than anything in the biomarker column — and they
work regardless of which protein is misfolding.

**Key sources:** NIA-AA diagnostic framework; DLB consensus criteria; FTD and PPA consensus
criteria; VASCOG/AHA vascular cognitive impairment criteria; FDA labelling for the anti-amyloid
monoclonals and the symptomatic agents.
