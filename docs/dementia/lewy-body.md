# Dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease dementia

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: August 2026</span>

*Part of [Approach to cognitive impairment](index.md).*

**One-liner:** an **α-synucleinopathy** producing **fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual
hallucinations, parkinsonism and REM sleep behaviour disorder** — commonly misdiagnosed as
Alzheimer's disease, and the one dementia where **giving the wrong drug can kill the patient.**

**Who this is for:** anyone about to prescribe an antipsychotic to an older adult with dementia,
anyone puzzled by a patient whose cognition swings between visits, and anyone who has been told a
patient "has Alzheimer's but sees things."

!!! danger "🚩 Neuroleptic sensitivity — the reason this page exists"
    **Up to about half of patients with DLB have a severe sensitivity reaction to antipsychotics**,
    and it can be **irreversible or fatal.** The reaction is acute onset or worsening of
    **parkinsonism, rigidity, impaired consciousness, autonomic instability** — a
    **neuroleptic-malignant-like syndrome.**

    - **Never give haloperidol or another typical antipsychotic to a patient with suspected DLB.**
    - **Atypicals are safer, not safe.** The risk is reduced, not abolished — quetiapine and
      clozapine are the least-worst options, and **olanzapine and risperidone are not.**
    - **This is a bedside emergency-department problem, not just a clinic one.** The commonest way
      the harm happens is an agitated older patient in an ED or on a ward getting IM haloperidol
      before anyone takes a Parkinson's or hallucination history.
    - **Ask the two questions before any antipsychotic in an older adult:** *do they have
      parkinsonism?* and *do they see things that are not there?*

## Diagnosis

**Core features** — fluctuating cognition with pronounced variation in attention and alertness;
**recurrent, well-formed, detailed visual hallucinations**; **REM sleep behaviour disorder**, which
may precede cognitive decline by years; and **spontaneous parkinsonism.**

**Supportive and biomarker findings** include severe neuroleptic sensitivity, autonomic dysfunction
(orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary problems), repeated falls, syncope, hyposmia,
depression, apathy and systematised delusions; **reduced dopamine transporter uptake on DaT-SPECT**;
**abnormal MIBG cardiac scintigraphy**; and **polysomnographic confirmation of REM sleep without
atonia.**

!!! note "DLB vs Parkinson's disease dementia — the same biology, split by a clock"
    The distinction is conventional, not biological: the **"1-year rule."** If dementia begins
    **before or within 1 year of** parkinsonism, call it **DLB**; if parkinsonism has been
    established for **more than a year** before dementia, call it **Parkinson's disease dementia**.

    **Treat them essentially the same.** The rule exists for research consistency and matters
    clinically mostly because it shapes what the family has already been told.

**What gets it missed:**

- **Fluctuation is mistaken for delirium** — or for a patient "having a good day."
- **Memory is often relatively preserved early**, so an amnestic screen looks reassuring.
  **Visuospatial and attentional testing is where DLB shows up** — pentagon or cube copy, clock
  draw, and sustained attention tasks.
- **Hallucinations are under-reported** because patients with insight find them frightening to
  disclose. Ask directly, and ask the collateral historian.
- **RBD is dismissed as "vivid dreams."** Ask whether the patient acts out dreams, and whether the
  bed partner has moved to another room or been injured.
- **Mixed AD and Lewy pathology is common** — a positive amyloid biomarker does not exclude DLB.

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Ask the two safety questions** — parkinsonism? visual hallucinations? — and **flag neuroleptic
   sensitivity prominently in the record** before anything else happens.
2. **Take the collateral history**, specifically for fluctuation, hallucinations, and dream
   enactment.
3. **Test visuospatial and attentional domains**, not just memory.
4. **Screen for and treat orthostatic hypotension** — it drives falls, and it is treatable.
5. **Deprescribe** — anticholinergics, dopamine blockers (including antiemetics), benzodiazepines.
6. **Start a cholinesterase inhibitor.** This class works better here than in Alzheimer's disease.
7. **Treat the symptom that most impairs function**, one at a time: hallucinations, RBD,
   parkinsonism, orthostasis, or sleepiness.
8. **Address falls, driving and caregiver support.**
9. **Military disposition** — dementia is disqualifying upon diagnosis; see the military box.

## Management

!!! orderset "Step 1 — deprescribe, then treat orthostasis"
    - **Stop dopamine-blocking antiemetics** — **metoclopramide and prochlorperazine are common,
      avoidable causes of acute deterioration.** Use **ondansetron** instead.
    - **Stop anticholinergics** — oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics. They worsen cognition and
      hallucinations in a disease already defined by cholinergic deficit.
    - **Stop or minimise benzodiazepines and sedating agents** — falls, confusion.
    - **Treat orthostatic hypotension** — liberalise salt and fluid unless contraindicated,
      compression, elevate the head of the bed, and **review antihypertensives, which are often no
      longer needed.** Orthostasis is a major and reversible contributor to falls here.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — cholinesterase inhibitor (the cornerstone in DLB/PDD)"
    The cholinergic deficit in Lewy body disease is more profound than in Alzheimer's, and the
    response — particularly for **hallucinations, apathy and fluctuation** — is often better.

    - **Rivastigmine** — the agent with an FDA indication for **mild-to-moderate dementia associated
      with Parkinson's disease.** **Oral: 1.5 mg PO BID**, increasing by 1.5 mg BID at **minimum
      2-week** intervals to a maximum of **6 mg BID** as tolerated.
        - **Patch: 4.6 mg/24 h**, increasing after **≥4 weeks** to **9.5 mg/24 h (the minimum
          effective dose)** and after a further ≥4 weeks to **13.3 mg/24 h.** The patch is often
          better tolerated and is the pragmatic first choice.
    - **Donepezil — 5 mg PO once daily**, increasing to **10 mg daily** after 4–6 weeks. Widely used
      in DLB, though its labelled indication is Alzheimer's-type dementia — **off-label here, and
      worth saying so.**
    - **Watch the pulse.** Cholinergic bradycardia matters more in a population already prone to
      syncope and falls.

!!! orderset "Step 3 — treat the dominant symptom"
    === "Visual hallucinations"

        - **First, remove the cause** — anticholinergics, dopaminergic drugs, infection,
          dehydration, poor lighting, visual impairment. **Correct the glasses and turn a light on**
          before reaching for a drug.
        - **Optimise the cholinesterase inhibitor** — often sufficient.
        - **If hallucinations are distressing or dangerous and non-drug measures fail:**
          **quetiapine** at a low starting dose, or **clozapine**, which has the better evidence in
          Parkinson's disease psychosis but requires **absolute neutrophil count monitoring.**
          **Both are off-label for DLB.** Start low, go slow, review to stop.
        - **Pimavanserin (Nuplazid) — 34 mg PO once daily, no titration.** **Its FDA indication is
          hallucinations and delusions associated with *Parkinson's disease* psychosis** — using it
          in DLB is **off-label**, and the page should not pretend otherwise.
        - **All antipsychotics carry a boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly patients
          with dementia-related psychosis**, and pimavanserin is included.
        - **Not every hallucination needs treating.** A patient with insight who is not distressed
          by benign, non-threatening imagery may be better served by explanation than by a drug.

    === "REM sleep behaviour disorder"

        - **Make the bedroom safe first** — remove hard and sharp objects, pad or lower the bed,
          consider separate beds. This is the intervention that prevents injury.
        - **Melatonin** is the usual first pharmacological choice, and is preferred over
          clonazepam in this population because of falls, sedation and cognitive effects.
        - *A benzodiazepine (clonazepam) works but is the wrong drug class in a falls-prone,
          cognitively impaired patient* — last resort only, lowest possible dose, explicit review
          date. **Neither melatonin nor clonazepam is FDA-labelled for RBD; doses are deliberately
          not quoted here pending review.**

    === "Parkinsonism"

        - *Carbidopa/levodopa is the treatment*, but the response is typically **less robust than
          in Parkinson's disease** and it can **worsen hallucinations and confusion.** **Dosing is
          deliberately not quoted here** — it is titrated to motor response in a population where
          the psychiatric ceiling, not the motor benefit, sets the dose.
        - **Treat only if the motor symptoms are functionally limiting**, use the lowest effective
          dose, and titrate slowly with an explicit warning to the family about psychiatric
          worsening.
        - **Avoid dopamine agonists, amantadine and anticholinergic antiparkinsonian drugs** — all
          worse for cognition and hallucinations than levodopa.

    === "Excessive daytime sleepiness"

        - Screen for and treat **sleep apnoea**, and review sedating medications first.
        - Sleepiness is intrinsic to the disease and often the symptom families find hardest;
          scheduled activity and light exposure help more than stimulants.

!!! warning "Avoid"
    - **Haloperidol and all typical antipsychotics** — severe sensitivity reactions, potentially
      fatal. **Flag this in the allergy field, not the progress note.**
    - **Olanzapine and risperidone** — worse tolerated here than quetiapine or clozapine.
    - **Metoclopramide and prochlorperazine** — dopamine blockers hiding in the antiemetic order set.
    - **Anticholinergics of any kind**, including for bladder symptoms.
    - **Benzodiazepines** — except a deliberate, reviewed clonazepam trial for injurious RBD.
    - **Dopamine agonists, amantadine, anticholinergic antiparkinsonian agents.**
    - **Surgery without a plan** — anaesthesia, opioids and antiemetics all conspire here.
      **Tell the surgical and anaesthetic teams the diagnosis in advance.**

!!! tip "TRICARE — DLB/PDD prescribing"
    - **The workhorses are generic and formulary** — rivastigmine (capsule and patch), donepezil,
      memantine, carbidopa/levodopa, melatonin. **Nothing in the first two steps should require a
      prior authorization.**
    - **Quetiapine and clozapine are generic**; clozapine's constraint is the **ANC monitoring and
      registry requirement**, not the formulary.
    - **Pimavanserin is a branded specialty agent** — assume prior authorization, and note that a
      PA written for **DLB rather than Parkinson's disease psychosis is off-label** and should be
      argued as such, with the literature and the clinical rationale, rather than filed as a
      formulary request.
    - **Verify current status at the
      [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst)
      before counselling.** *TRICARE-specific criteria were not independently verified for this page.*

!!! military "Military medicine — DLB and PDD"
    *Framework — verify against current **AR 40-501**, **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 and Vol 2**,
    **DoDI 1332.18**, and service aeromedical policy. See
    [Profiles, MEB/MAR2 & deployability](../military/deployability.md).*

    - **Almost entirely a beneficiary population.** DLB and PDD are diseases of later life; active
      duty cases would be extraordinary. **The military value of this page is safety, not
      disposition** — specifically, stopping an MTF or deployed provider from giving haloperidol to
      an agitated older beneficiary with undisclosed parkinsonism.
    - **Retention, if it ever arises: disqualifying upon diagnosis** — dementia is
      **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.26.d**, one of only two paragraphs where *"Paragraph 5.26.a. does
      not apply"*. Army hook **AR 40-501 3-31k** closing on **para 3-1**. **Parkinsonism engages the
      movement-disorder standard independently** — cite both, and see
      [deployability](../military/deployability.md).
    - **Profile:** **P** factor for the neurologic condition, **S** where the cognitive and
      psychiatric burden dominates; state which. Section 4 limits must address **driving, weapons
      access, falls risk and independent duty.**
    - **EPTS / LOD:** RBD and hyposmia can precede the diagnosis by **years**, so a retrospective
      onset argument is genuinely available here in a way it is not for most conditions — date the
      first attributable feature, including dream enactment, and say what you reviewed.
    - **MEB / DES:** refer on diagnosis; no MRDP to wait out. Document objective testing, falls,
      autonomic burden and functional impact.
    - **Deployability: non-deployable.** Fluctuating cognition, orthostasis and falls, medication
      dependence, and a hallucinatory disorder are each independently disqualifying for the
      operational environment.
    - **Aeromedical: disqualifying** pending **USAAMA** adjudication; likewise **ATC and UAS**.
    - **The practical military-medicine point** is the pre-operative and pre-procedural one:
      beneficiaries with DLB coming to an MTF for surgery need the diagnosis **communicated to
      anaesthesia in advance**, because the standard perioperative antiemetic and sedative order
      set is a hazard here.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

**Two diseases, one protein, and a rule invented for tidiness.** DLB and Parkinson's disease dementia
share α-synuclein pathology, cholinergic depletion and clinical phenomenology; the **1-year rule**
that separates them is a research convention, not a biological boundary. It survives because it lets
trials enrol consistent populations — but at the bedside the treatment is the same, and pretending
otherwise mostly confuses families who have already been given one label.

**The cholinergic deficit is deeper here than in Alzheimer's, and it shows.** Cholinesterase
inhibitors, whose effect in AD is genuinely modest, often produce visible improvement in DLB —
particularly in hallucinations, apathy and the fluctuation itself. That is the closest thing to a
mechanistically satisfying treatment response in the dementias, and it argues for starting one early
rather than reserving it.

**And the most important fact on the page is a prohibition.** Neuroleptic sensitivity in DLB is not a
side effect to weigh against benefit — it is a potentially fatal, sometimes irreversible reaction
affecting a large minority of patients. Most of the harm is preventable by two questions asked
before an antipsychotic is written for any older adult. **This is the rare instance where the
diagnosis matters most to the clinician who has never heard of the patient.**

**Key sources:** DLB consortium consensus criteria; FDA labelling for rivastigmine and pimavanserin;
the antipsychotic class boxed warning for dementia-related psychosis.
