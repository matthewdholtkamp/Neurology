# Alzheimer's disease

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: August 2026</span>

*Part of [Approach to cognitive impairment](index.md).*

**One-liner:** the commonest cause of dementia — an **amyloid and tau proteinopathy** that
classically presents as **progressive episodic memory loss** and is now, for the first time, both
**biologically confirmable in life** and **modifiably treatable**, at a cost in monitoring, money and
risk that has to be discussed rather than assumed.

**Who this is for:** the provider deciding whether this is Alzheimer's disease at all, what to start,
and — the genuinely new question — **whether this specific patient should be offered an anti-amyloid
antibody**, which is a decision about eligibility, risk tolerance and logistics as much as diagnosis.

!!! danger "🚩 Before you call it Alzheimer's"
    | Pattern | Worry | Do |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Weeks-to-months** progression | [Rapidly progressive dementia](rapidly-progressive.md) | Expedite; do not start a cholinesterase inhibitor and follow up in 6 months |
    | **Visual hallucinations, fluctuation, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder** | [Dementia with Lewy bodies](lewy-body.md) | **Antipsychotic sensitivity** — get this right before anyone prescribes |
    | **Behaviour or language first, memory relatively spared** | [FTD](frontotemporal.md) | Collateral history; FDG-PET can help |
    | **Age <65** | Young-onset AD, genetic, or a non-AD cause | Broader work-up; genetic counselling if familial pattern |
    | **On an anti-amyloid antibody, now with new focal deficit or headache** | **ARIA** | **Urgent MRI. ARIA-E mimics ischaemic stroke — consider it before thrombolysis** |

## Diagnosis

**The framework changed in 2024, and it is contested.** The **Alzheimer's Association Revised
Criteria (2024)** replace the 2018 NIA-AA research framework and the 2011 NIA-AA clinical
guidelines — NIA now holds an advisory role only. The change is not cosmetic:

- **AD is now defined biologically.** An abnormal **Core 1** biomarker — amyloid PET, an approved
  CSF assay, or **plasma p-tau217** — is **sufficient for diagnosis without symptoms.**
- **Core 2** biomarkers (tau PET, biofluid tau) provide **prognostic staging.**
- The **"N"** (neurodegeneration) was **removed** from the core criteria.
- The **clinical stage** (asymptomatic, MCI, mild/moderate/severe) is recorded **separately** from
  the biology.

!!! warning "This is genuinely controversial, and the page takes a position"
    Defining a disease by a biomarker in a person without symptoms means **a substantial number of
    cognitively normal older adults meet criteria for "Alzheimer's disease"** — see the amyloid
    prevalence figures on the [hub](index.md#biomarkers-what-they-answer-and-when-to-ask). Several
    expert groups argue the 2024 criteria should stay in the **research domain** for now.

    **Practical position for this page:** use the biological criteria to *characterise* a
    symptomatic patient, not to *diagnose* an asymptomatic one. **Do not tell a person without
    cognitive impairment that they have Alzheimer's disease on the strength of a blood test.**

**The classic clinical syndrome:**

- **Episodic memory first** — repeating questions, losing recent conversations, misplacing items,
  relying increasingly on notes and on a spouse. **Impaired *encoding*, not just retrieval**: cueing
  and recognition do not rescue the memory the way they do in depression or in subcortical disease.
- Then **word-finding difficulty, visuospatial disorientation** (getting lost on familiar routes),
  and **executive decline.**
- **Insight is often reduced early**, which is why the collateral history is the diagnostic
  instrument.

**Atypical presentations exist and are missed:** **posterior cortical atrophy** (visual complaints
with normal eyes), **logopenic primary progressive aphasia**, a **dysexecutive/frontal variant**, and
a **corticobasal syndrome** that is sometimes AD pathology. Young onset skews atypical.

!!! note "What a biomarker adds — and what it does not"
    A positive amyloid biomarker tells you **AD pathology is present**. It does **not** tell you
    the patient's symptoms are caused by it, and it does not exclude co-pathology — **mixed vascular
    and Lewy pathology is common**, especially over 80.

    **The order of operations matters:** establish the syndrome and exclude the treatable
    contributors *first* (see the [hub](index.md#workup)); test biology only when the answer changes
    management. **The clearest indication to test is a genuine intention to offer anti-amyloid
    therapy**, which requires confirmed amyloid pathology before initiation.

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Confirm it is dementia or MCI, not delirium or depression**, and that the treatable
   contributors have been screened. See the [hub](index.md).
2. **Confirm the syndrome is amnestic-predominant** — or recognise one of the atypical variants.
3. **Structural imaging** — MRI to support the pattern and, critically, **to establish the vascular
   and microhaemorrhage baseline** that governs anti-amyloid eligibility.
4. **Decide whether anti-amyloid therapy is genuinely on the table** — stage, comorbidity,
   anticoagulation, MRI findings, logistics, and the patient's own values. **If it is not, do not
   order amyloid biomarkers.**
5. **If it is:** confirm amyloid pathology, **test ApoE ε4 genotype**, baseline MRI, and counsel on
   ARIA before the first dose.
6. **Start symptomatic therapy** — a cholinesterase inhibitor, adding memantine as disease advances.
   This is independent of, and additive to, any decision about anti-amyloid therapy.
7. **Treat the modifiable contributors and vascular risk** — they matter here as much as anywhere.
8. **Address safety, capacity, driving and caregiver support** from the first visit.
9. **Military disposition** — dementia is **disqualifying upon diagnosis**; see the military box.

## Management

### Symptomatic therapy — start here for essentially everyone

!!! orderset "Step 1 — cholinesterase inhibitor"
    Modest, real, symptomatic benefit. Not disease-modifying. Choose on tolerability and route;
    there is no persuasive efficacy difference between them.

    - **Donepezil — 5 mg PO once daily in the evening.** After 4–6 weeks, increase to **10 mg
      daily** (the usual maintenance). In **moderate-to-severe** disease the label permits **23 mg
      daily** after at least 3 months at 10 mg — **the added benefit is marginal and the cholinergic
      side effects are not**; most patients should stay at 10.
    - **Rivastigmine — oral 1.5 mg PO BID**, increasing by 1.5 mg BID at **minimum 2-week**
      intervals to 3, 4.5 and up to **6 mg BID** as tolerated.
        - **Patch: start 4.6 mg/24 h**, increase after **≥4 weeks** to **9.5 mg/24 h — the minimum
          effective dose** — and after a further ≥4 weeks to a maximum of **13.3 mg/24 h.** Rotate
          sites; the patch has less GI intolerance than the capsule and is the pragmatic choice for
          many patients.
        - *Rivastigmine also carries a **Parkinson's disease dementia** indication — relevant on the
          [DLB/PDD page](lewy-body.md).*
    - **Galantamine ER — 8 mg PO daily in the morning**, increasing after **≥4 weeks** to **16
      mg/day**, and after a further ≥4 weeks to a maximum of **24 mg/day.** **Take with food and
      maintain fluid intake.** **Do not exceed 16 mg/day in moderate hepatic impairment.**
    - **Counsel on the side effects that cause discontinuation** — nausea, diarrhoea, anorexia and
      weight loss, vivid dreams, bradycardia and syncope, and increased urinary urgency. **Titrate
      slowly; most intolerance is a titration problem.**
    - **Check the pulse before and during treatment.** These drugs are cholinergic — bradycardia,
      heart block and syncope are real, and a patient who falls after starting one should have the
      drug reconsidered before the fall is attributed to the dementia.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — add memantine as disease advances"
    - **Memantine — 5 mg PO once daily**, increasing in **5 mg increments at intervals of at least
      one week** to 10, 15 and a target of **20 mg/day (10 mg BID)**. Labelled for **moderate to
      severe** Alzheimer's disease.
    - **Reduce the dose in severe renal impairment.**
    - **Combination with a cholinesterase inhibitor is standard** in moderate-to-severe disease and
      is generally well tolerated — memantine's side-effect profile is much lighter than the
      cholinergic agents'.

### Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies — the decision, then the protocol

!!! warning "This is a shared decision, not a reflex — and eligibility is narrow"
    Both approved agents are indicated only for patients in the **MCI or mild dementia stage** —
    the population studied. **They were not studied in moderate or severe disease and should not be
    started there.**

    **Confirm before anyone commits:**

    - **Amyloid pathology is confirmed** (PET or CSF) — this is a label requirement, not a nicety.
    - **ApoE ε4 genotype is known.** **Homozygotes — roughly 15% of AD patients — have a
      substantially higher incidence of ARIA**, including symptomatic, serious and severe
      radiographic ARIA. **Test before initiating, and counsel before testing**, including what a
      result means for blood relatives.
    - **A recent baseline MRI has been reviewed**, and the exclusions are numeric: **more than 4
      microhaemorrhages, or any single macrohaemorrhage, excludes treatment.** Pre-existing
      microhaemorrhage or superficial siderosis raises ARIA risk. This is where
      [cerebral amyloid angiopathy](../stroke/cerebral-amyloid-angiopathy.md) and this page meet;
      read that page's ARIA section before your first patient.
    - **Anticoagulation is a relative contraindication** — increased ARIA-H risk, and intracerebral
      haemorrhages **>1 cm, some fatal**, have occurred in this drug class.
    - **Cognitive staging is documented** — MCI or mild dementia, typically MMSE ~22–30.
    - **The infrastructure exists** — infusion or injection capacity, and **MRI access on the
      required schedule**. Starting without the monitoring is worse than not starting.

!!! orderset "Lecanemab (Leqembi) — dosing and monitoring"
    - **Intravenous: 10 mg/kg over approximately 1 hour every 2 weeks.**
    - **Subcutaneous (LEQEMBI IQLIK autoinjector): 500 mg once weekly.**
    - **After 18 months**, either continue the starting regimen or transition to maintenance:
      **IV 10 mg/kg every 4 weeks**, or **SC 360 mg once weekly.**
    - **MRI schedule: baseline, then after 1, 2, 3 and 6 months of treatment.**
    - **Enhanced clinical vigilance for ARIA during the first 14 weeks.**
    - **Infusion-related reactions** — slow or stop the infusion; consider premedication with
      antihistamine, NSAID or corticosteroid at subsequent doses.

!!! orderset "Donanemab (Kisunla) — dosing and monitoring"
    - **Intravenous over approximately 30 minutes every 4 weeks, titrated:**
      **infusion 1 — 350 mg; infusion 2 — 700 mg; infusion 3 — 1,050 mg; infusion 4 and beyond —
      1,400 mg.**
    - **That titration is not arbitrary — it is a safety intervention.** TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 6 showed
      the modified schedule cut **ARIA-E from 24.2% to 15.6%** and **symptomatic ARIA-E from 4.8%
      to 2.8%**, while still clearing amyloid. Do not compress it.
    - **Consider stopping** once amyloid plaques are reduced to minimal levels on amyloid PET —
      a genuinely different treatment philosophy from lecanemab's open-ended maintenance, and worth
      explaining to patients as a potential **finite course.**
    - **Confirm amyloid pathology before initiating; baseline MRI and serial monitoring as
      labelled.**

!!! orderset "If ARIA occurs — what to actually do"
    Management is driven by **severity and whether the patient has symptoms.** Radiographic ARIA
    is common; most of it is asymptomatic and does not end treatment.

    | Situation | Action |
    |---|---|
    | **Asymptomatic, mild** | **Continue treatment** with MRI surveillance |
    | **Asymptomatic, moderate** | **Suspend**; resume by shared decision after radiographic resolution |
    | **Symptomatic, mild or moderate** | **Suspend.** Consider **IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3–5 days**, then an oral prednisone taper. **Monthly MRI until resolution.** **Avoid anticoagulants and thrombolytics** |
    | **Severe, or recurrent ARIA** | **Permanently discontinue.** Corticosteroids; inpatient management |

    - **Recurrence after resumption runs 13.8–25.6%** — factor that into the shared decision rather
      than treating resumption as a return to baseline.
    - **Incidence for orientation:** ARIA-E in **12.6%** with lecanemab and **24.0%** with
      donanemab at standard dosing; **roughly 70% of ARIA is asymptomatic.** ApoE ε4 homozygotes
      run far higher — up to **55%** in high-dose aducanumab trials.

!!! danger "ARIA — boxed warning for the whole class"
    **Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities: ARIA-E (oedema, sulcal effusion) and ARIA-H
    (microhaemorrhage, superficial siderosis).**

    - **Usually early, usually asymptomatic — but serious and life-threatening events occur, and
      ARIA can be fatal.** Intracerebral haemorrhages **>1 cm, some fatal**, have been observed.
    - **ApoE ε4 homozygotes are at higher risk** across every severity category.
    - **Pre-existing microhaemorrhage or superficial siderosis raises the risk** of both ARIA-E and
      ARIA-H.
    - **The cross-specialty point that matters most: ARIA-E causes focal neurologic deficits that
      mimic ischaemic stroke.** **Before giving thrombolysis to any patient on this drug class,
      consider whether the deficit is ARIA-E.** Make sure your stroke service knows which of your
      patients are on these agents — see [acute ischaemic stroke](../stroke/index.md).
    - **Any new neurologic symptom on treatment gets an MRI**, and management is dictated by ARIA
      type, severity and whether symptoms are present.

### Behavioural and psychological symptoms

!!! orderset "Non-pharmacological first — every time"
    Identify the **trigger**: pain, constipation, urinary retention, infection, hunger, boredom,
    overstimulation, fear, a changed routine, or a caregiver approach that is escalating rather than
    de-escalating. **Most agitation has a cause that is not psychiatric**, and the medication that
    works best is usually the one you stop.

    - Structure, routine, daylight, activity, and caregiver coaching.
    - **Review every medication** — anticholinergics and benzodiazepines make this worse.
    - **Treat pain empirically** in a patient who cannot report it. Scheduled paracetamol is an
      underused agitation intervention.

!!! orderset "If medication is genuinely necessary — there are now TWO approved options"
    - **Dextromethorphan/bupropion (Auvelity)** — **FDA indication for agitation associated with
      dementia due to Alzheimer's disease added in 2026.** **The first non-antipsychotic approved
      for this indication**, which means it does **not** carry the antipsychotic class mortality
      warning — the main reason to consider it first in a patient where that warning is the
      sticking point. *Dosing for the agitation indication was not label-verified for this page —
      check the current label before prescribing.*
    - **Brexpiprazole (Rexulti)** — approved May 2023. **0.5 mg PO once daily on days 1–7; increase
      to 1 mg once daily on days 8–14; then 2 mg once daily from day 15 — the recommended target.**
      May increase to a **maximum of 3 mg once daily after at least 14 days** on clinical response.
      **Maximum 2 mg/day in moderate-to-severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh ≥7).**
      **Not indicated for as-needed ("prn") use** — do not write it that way.
        - **Carries the antipsychotic class boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly
          patients with dementia-related psychosis.** Document the discussion, use the lowest
          effective dose, and set a review date to stop.
        - Trial effect was roughly a **5-point greater reduction on the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation
          Inventory** versus placebo over 12 weeks — real, and worth stating plainly to a family
          deciding whether the mortality warning is acceptable.
    - **Depression is common and treatable** — an SSRI is often more useful than an antipsychotic.
      **Citalopram has the best agitation trial evidence (CitAD, at 30 mg/day) — but that dose
      exceeds the FDA maximum of 20 mg/day in patients over 60** because of QT prolongation. That
      is an awkward gap between the evidence and the label; **sertraline is the conservative
      default**, and escitalopram is under study.
    - **Avoid benzodiazepines.** They worsen cognition, increase falls, and are difficult to stop.

!!! warning "Avoid"
    - **Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs** — cognition, falls, dependence.
    - **Anticholinergics** — oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics. **Reducing anticholinergic
      burden is one of the few genuinely disease-relevant prescribing acts available.**
    - **Typical antipsychotics**, and any antipsychotic in suspected [DLB](lewy-body.md).
    - **Anti-amyloid antibodies in moderate or severe dementia** — outside the studied population.
    - **Starting an anti-amyloid antibody without MRI capacity** for the required schedule.
    - **Ordering an amyloid biomarker in a patient you would not treat.**

!!! tip "TRICARE — Alzheimer's prescribing"
    - **The symptomatic agents are generic and formulary** — donepezil, rivastigmine (oral and
      patch), galantamine, memantine. Start here; expect no prior authorization.
    - **Anti-amyloid monoclonals are high-cost specialty biologics** and should be assumed
      **prior-authorization-gated**. Expect requirements mirroring the label: **confirmed amyloid
      pathology, MCI or mild dementia stage, ApoE ε4 genotyping, baseline MRI, and a documented
      monitoring plan.** They are **infused agents and run through the medical benefit**, so the
      authorization question includes **site of care** as well as the drug.
    - **The MRI schedule is part of the cost.** Baseline plus scans at 1, 2, 3 and 6 months is
      four to five studies in the first half-year — build that into the authorization request
      rather than discovering it at the second scan.
    - **Brexpiprazole** is a branded specialty agent; expect PA and expect the criteria to require a
      documented **AD** diagnosis and failed non-pharmacological management.
    - **Verify current status and criteria at the
      [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst)
      before counselling — DoD P&T revises tiers and criteria without notice.**
      *TRICARE-specific coverage for anti-amyloid therapy and for amyloid PET was not independently
      verified for this page.*

      emergency department — and any remote or deployed facility they might present to — needs to
      know it before the thrombolysis decision.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

**Amyloid is necessary and insufficient — and the drugs prove both halves.** For decades the amyloid
hypothesis was argued from genetics and pathology while every therapeutic test of it failed.
Lecanemab and donanemab settled the mechanistic question: **remove amyloid and decline slows.** They
also settled the magnitude question less comfortably. **Lecanemab slowed CDR-SB decline by 0.45
points over 18 months — about 27%, or roughly 4–5 months of delayed progression.** **Donanemab
managed 0.67 points (36%) in the low/medium-tau population and 0.70 (29%) overall at 76 weeks**,
and reduced the risk of progressing from MCI to mild dementia by **33%**, and mild to moderate by
**50%**. Those are real, reproducible, and small. Whether they are worth biweekly infusions, serial
MRI, genotyping and a boxed warning is not a question medicine can answer on the patient's behalf —
but it is a question that deserves the actual numbers rather than an adjective.

**And the class has already lost a member.** **Aducanumab was withdrawn from the US market in
November 2024** — a commercial decision rather than a safety or efficacy one, but a reminder that
approval is not permanence. **Anti-tau therapies have produced no positive phase 3 to date.**

**The honest framing for the clinic is a trade, not a breakthrough.** These drugs slow a trajectory;
they do not restore function or stop the disease. The patients most likely to benefit — early,
biologically confirmed, without significant vascular or microhaemorrhage burden, with the support to
attend monitoring — are a **minority of the people who will ask about them.** Saying so clearly is
part of the job, and so is not letting a patient outside that group feel they have been denied
something.

**Meanwhile the diagnostic revolution may matter more than the therapeutic one.** **Plasma p-tau217**
brings biological confirmation within reach of a blood draw. That is transformative for specialist
triage and dangerous as a screening test — amyloid pathology exists in cognitively normal older
adults, and a positive result cannot be withdrawn once given. The discipline the field needs is
about **who gets tested**, not about what the assay can detect.

**And the unglamorous interventions keep outperforming.** Deprescribing anticholinergics and
benzodiazepines, treating sleep apnoea and hearing loss, controlling vascular risk, and supporting
caregivers still deliver more benefit to more patients than anything in the biologics column.

**Key sources:** FDA labelling for lecanemab and donanemab; NIA-AA diagnostic and staging framework;
FDA labelling for the cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine and brexpiprazole.
