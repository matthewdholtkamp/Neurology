# Myasthenia gravis

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: August 2026</span>

**One-liner:** an antibody-mediated disorder of the **postsynaptic** neuromuscular junction —
**fatigable, fluctuating weakness** that worsens with use and through the day and recovers with
rest. Ocular, bulbar, limb, and — the one that kills — respiratory muscles.

**Who this is for:** the provider seeing fluctuating diplopia/ptosis or dysphagia in clinic, and
the one facing a patient in **myasthenic crisis** in the ED. MG is a real active-duty diagnosis and
a named referral condition under AR 40-501 3-31c — but the ocular-only carve-out means the
disposition is not automatic.

!!! danger "🚩 Myasthenic crisis — respiratory failure is the emergency"
    **The threat is the diaphragm, and pulse oximetry will reassure you right up until the patient
    crashes.** Hypoxia is a late sign; hypercapnic fatigue comes first.

    **Assess ventilation directly, not with the pulse ox — the "20/30/40 rule":**

    | Measure | Intubation-range value |
    |---|---|
    | **Forced vital capacity (FVC)** | **<20 mL/kg** (or <1 L) |
    | **Negative inspiratory force (NIF/MIP)** | **worse than −30 cmH₂O** |
    | **Maximal expiratory pressure (MEP)** | **<40 cmH₂O** |

    **None of these thresholds is prospectively validated, and MG patients can crash before any of
    them trends down.** FVC and NIF together predict better than either alone. **Trajectory beats
    any single number** — a patient whose FVC has fallen 30% in six hours is intubated tonight even
    if tonight's number looks acceptable. A **fall in MEP of ≥30%** marks high risk. A **supine-vs-
    seated drop in VC** flags diaphragm weakness.

    - **Single-breath count** — after a deep breath in, sitting upright, count out loud at
      **~2 counts per second**. **Reaching ~30 correlates with adequate pulmonary function; ≤20
      indicates significant inspiratory muscle weakness** (~116 mL of VC per count). A 2023
      systematic review puts the normal threshold at ≥25. **This is a correlate of VC/NIF, not a
      validated independent intubation trigger** — use it to trigger spirometry, and where
      spirometry is unavailable.
    - **A normal PaCO₂ is not reassuring.** A rise from the patient's own baseline (30 → 42) means
      the diaphragm is failing. Do not wait for hypercapnia by lab reference range.
    - **Bulbar failure** — can't handle secretions, weak cough, nasal voice — is an independent
      reason to secure the airway.
    - **Low-risk / step-down:** VC >20 mL/kg, MEP >40 cmH₂O, NIF better than −40 cmH₂O.

    **BiPAP can buy time early but fails fast** in true respiratory-muscle fatigue. **Check a blood
    gas before you commit: PaCO₂ >45 mmHg at BiPAP initiation was the only predictor of BiPAP
    failure** in a 60-episode cohort, and a trial started *before* hypercapnia developed prevented
    intubation and shortened ventilation. A 2023 review cites **PaCO₂ >50**, pneumonia, and older
    age as NIV-failure predictors; **failure rates reach ~38%.** Trial BiPAP in the non-hypercapnic
    patient — **hypercapnia already present argues for intubation.** Intubate early in patients with
    high pre-crisis MGFA class, severe dysphagia, or absent protective reflexes.
    **If you are asking whether to intubate, intubate.**

    !!! warning "Two intubation traps specific to MG"
        - **The two paralytic classes go in OPPOSITE directions — do not treat them as one rule.**
            - **Succinylcholine: MG patients are RESISTANT** (fewer endplate receptors; ED95 ≈2.6×
              normal). If you use it, **dose UP — 1.5–2 mg/kg — not down.** An under-dosed RSI in a
              patient with no respiratory reserve is the disaster. Pyridostigmine also inhibits
              plasma cholinesterase, so block may be prolonged and phase-II block more likely on
              repeat dosing.
            - **Non-depolarizing blockers: MG patients are exquisitely SENSITIVE** — 10–50% of the
              normal dose, titrated to twitch monitoring.
            - **Preferred where available: rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg with sugammadex immediately at
              hand.** Tell anesthesia the patient has MG *before* induction, every time.
        - **Do not give IV magnesium** to a crashing MG patient (e.g. for a coincident indication)
          — it blocks neuromuscular transmission and can precipitate or deepen crisis. See the
          pregnancy/eclampsia caveat in the drug list below.

## Rapid approach

1. **Recognize the pattern** — fatigable, fluctuating, fatigues with use, worse later in the day,
   spares sensation and reflexes.
2. **If the patient is in or near crisis, stop working down this list.** Protect the airway, then go
   straight to [myasthenic crisis](#myasthenic-crisis-acute-management). **Rapid immunotherapy is
   started on clinical grounds — do not wait for serology, electrodiagnostics, or chest imaging.**
   Send the serology on the way past; it will not be back for days and it does not change tonight's
   management.
3. **Send serology** — AChR, then MuSK if negative (see below).
4. **Confirm** with the pattern + serology; use EDx (RNS, SFEMG) when serology is negative.
5. **Image the mediastinum** (CT/MRI chest) — **every MG patient needs a thymoma screen.**
6. **Treat** — symptomatic (pyridostigmine) + immunotherapy; crisis gets IVIG or PLEX.
7. **Screen for precipitants** — infection, and the long list of drugs that worsen MG.
8. **Start the military disposition** — profile, and the ocular-vs-generalized determination.

## Diagnosis

**The clinical signature is fatigability.** Sustained upgaze reproduces ptosis; repeated
abduction fatigues an eye; proximal limbs weaken on repetition. **Ptosis and diplopia** are the
commonest presenting features. **Sensation, reflexes, and pupils are normal** — a fixed pupil or
sensory loss argues against MG and for a mimic.

### Serology — send in this order

| Antibody | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **AChR (acetylcholine receptor)** | ~**85%** of generalized MG | First test. Highly specific. Titre does not track severity |
| **MuSK (muscle-specific kinase)** | ~**5–8%** | Send if AChR-negative. **Distinct phenotype** — prominent bulbar/facial/neck/respiratory weakness, and **different treatment** (see below) |
| **LRP4** | ~1–3% | Send in double-seronegative patients |
| **Seronegative** | remainder | Especially **ocular-only** MG. A negative panel does not exclude MG — lean on EDx, and see the caveat below before calling anyone seronegative |

!!! warning "\"Seronegative\" is often an assay limitation, not an absence of antibody"
    **A standard radioimmunoassay misses antibodies that a clustered-AChR cell-based assay (CBA)
    detects** — and those patients respond to antibody-directed therapy like anyone else.
    **Send a clustered-AChR CBA before you label a patient triple-seronegative**, because that
    label changes both the treatment route and, downstream, what a payer will approve.

!!! tip "When serology is negative — electrodiagnostics"
    - **Repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS)** — a **decremental response**: *"a 10% decrement in
      amplitude from the first to fourth or fifth intravolley waveform while stimulating at
      2–5 Hz"* (3 Hz is the usual standard). **Hold anticholinesterases ≥12 h and keep the limb
      warm** — cooling masks decrement. The preferred initial electrodiagnostic test, but
      **abnormal in only ~17% of ocular MG vs ~85% of generalized MG.**
    - **Single-fiber EMG (SFEMG)** — **increased jitter or impulse blocking**. The most sensitive
      test for MG — **~92% overall, 95–99% in generalized disease with an appropriate muscle, but
      only ~79% in purely ocular MG** — and less specific and operator-dependent. Sensitivity rises
      with severity and with muscle selection (orbicularis oculi/frontalis for ocular disease).
      A normal SFEMG in a well-chosen muscle argues strongly against MG **only in generalized
      disease.**
    - The **ice-pack test** (ptosis improves ≥2 mm after 2 min of ice) is a useful, cheap bedside
      adjunct for ptosis — not a substitute for confirmation.

!!! warning "Every MG patient needs a chest CT/MRI"
    **10–15% of MG patients have a thymoma**, and its presence changes management independently of
    the MG — a thymoma is a surgical indication **regardless of MG severity or serostatus.**
    Conversely, thymoma essentially never accompanies **MuSK** disease. Image the mediastinum in
    everyone at diagnosis.

### Ocular vs generalized — the distinction that drives everything

- **Ocular MG** — weakness confined to lids and extraocular muscles. Often seronegative.
- **How many generalize depends on whether they were treated** — quote the right number for the
  question you are answering:
    - **50–80%** in older long-term studies of largely **untreated** patients.
    - **~28%** in contemporary **treated** cohorts (mean 87-month follow-up; mean time to
      generalization 24 months).
    - **~39%** pooled across 31 studies (95% CI 32–47%), which blends both.
  Generalization is concentrated in the **first 1–2 years**; after 2 years of purely ocular disease
  the risk falls substantially. Risk factors — later onset age, female sex, symptom severity,
  abnormal RNS, AChR positivity, thymic hyperplasia — are inconsistently replicated.
- This split is the hinge of both **treatment intensity** and the **military disposition** — the
  regulations disqualify generalized MG but retain disease *confined to the extraocular muscles.*

### Stage it — MGFA classification and the treatment target

The trials this page cites are written in this language, so the page uses it too.

| Class | Definition |
|---|---|
| **I** | Any ocular muscle weakness; may have weakness of eye closure. Strength elsewhere normal |
| **II** | **Mild** generalized weakness |
| **III** | **Moderate** generalized weakness |
| **IV** | **Severe** generalized weakness |
| **V** | **Intubation**, or NIV to avoid intubation |

Subclass **a** = limb/axial predominant; **b** = oropharyngeal/respiratory predominant.
*MGTX enrolled Class II–IV.*

- **Treatment goal (MGFA consensus):** post-intervention status of **minimal manifestation (MM) or
  better** — *"no symptoms or functional limitations from MG but some weakness on examination of
  some muscles"* — **with no more than grade 1 medication side effects.** Aim at this explicitly;
  it is the endpoint the trials report.
- **Refractory MG** (the gate to biologics, and it has a definition): *"unchanged or worse after
  corticosteroids and at least 2 other immunosuppressant agents, used in adequate doses for an
  adequate duration, with persistent symptoms or side effects that limit functioning."*
  **Refer refractory patients to a clinician or centre with MG expertise.**

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Is the airway safe?** Bulbar or respiratory weakness → assess ventilation directly, admit,
   treat as crisis. Nothing below matters until this is answered. **If the answer is no, jump to
   [crisis](#myasthenic-crisis-acute-management) and start rapid immunotherapy on clinical grounds
   — steps 2 and 3 do not gate treatment.**
2. **Confirm the diagnosis** — pattern + serology, EDx if seronegative. **Send a clustered-AChR CBA
   before accepting a seronegative label.**
3. **Screen the mediastinum** — chest CT/MRI for thymoma.
4. **Start symptomatic control** — pyridostigmine (less useful in MuSK disease). If a tolerated dose
   in the 300–480 mg/day range leaves the patient symptomatic, they are **inadequately controlled,
   not under-dosed** — move to immunotherapy rather than pushing higher.
5. **Start immunotherapy** — most generalized patients need it; steroids + a steroid-sparing agent.
   **Measure respiratory function before the first steroid dose** (see the early-worsening box).
6. **Discuss thymectomy** — a separate oncologic indication for any thymoma; for non-thymomatous
   **AChR-positive generalized** MG *clinicians should discuss it* (AAN Level B), with the best
   randomized evidence in **ages 18–50**. **Not** for MuSK or LRP4 disease.
7. **Escalate to targeted biologics** when refractory (defined above) or steroid-dependent — FcRn,
   complement, or anti-CD19. **Serostatus decides which are available to you.**
8. **Hand the patient a precipitant-drug list** and address the military disposition.

## Myasthenic crisis — acute management

!!! orderset "Step 1 — airway and monitoring"
    - **Admit to an ICU/step-down** anyone with a crisis or impending crisis. Manifest crisis
      warrants ICU or step-down; impending crisis warrants admission somewhere with ICU capability.
    - **Serial FVC, NIF and single-breath count** — every few hours; **trend beats snapshot.**
    - **Intubate** on the 20/30/40 thresholds above, on bulbar failure with secretions, on visible
      accessory-muscle fatigue, or on a **falling trend regardless of absolute number.**
      **Do not wait for hypoxia.**
    - **Hold pyridostigmine in intubated crisis patients** — the marginal benefit is outweighed by
      secretions and mucus plugging, and it confounds assessment of the response to PLEX/IVIG.
      **Restart it before weaning.**
    - **Do not use IV pyridostigmine** (1–2 mg/h; 1 mg IV ≈ 30 mg PO) — discouraged because of
      **fatal arrhythmia risk.**
    - **Find and treat the precipitant** — infection is the commonest; also review every new drug
      (see the avoid list). **Do not withhold antibiotics from a septic MG patient** — see the
      alternatives in that box.

!!! danger "Before you increase pyridostigmine in a weakening patient, ask whether the drug is the cause"
    **Cholinergic crisis is now exceedingly rare** — and the modern position is that you no longer
    differentiate it by giving edrophonium or neostigmine. But excess cholinesterase inhibitor
    **cannot be fully excluded** as a cause of worsening, and these drugs increase airway secretions
    either way. So it is a consideration to rule *through*, not a coin-flip.

    **What points to cholinergic toxicity:**

    - **Muscarinic excess** — hypersalivation, bronchorrhea, lacrimation, diaphoresis, miosis,
      bradycardia, abdominal cramps, diarrhea — plus **fasciculations and cramps** (nicotinic).
    - **Timing.** Weakness peaking **30–60 minutes after a dose** rather than at trough. This and
      the response to a **pyridostigmine holiday** are the most usable discriminators now.
    - **Dose.** Suspicion rises **above ~450 mg/day**, where depolarizing block itself causes
      weakness — and at lower doses in **renal impairment**, since pyridostigmine is renally
      cleared. Note that cholinergic overdose is *often but not always* accompanied by muscarinic
      signs.

    **What points to myasthenic crisis:** fatigable weakness worsening with repetitive effort,
    normal reflexes, normal sensation, and **absence** of autonomic/cholinergic features.

    !!! warning "Anticholinergic cover hides exactly the warning signs you need"
        **Glycopyrrolate and hyoscyamine suppress the muscarinic signs without touching the
        nicotinic component** (weakness, fasciculations, cramps). The pyridostigmine label states
        that using atropine to blunt muscarinic side effects, **by masking the signs of overdosage,
        can lead to inadvertent induction of cholinergic crisis.** A patient on scheduled
        glycopyrrolate can be walked into depolarizing block with a dry mouth.

    **When it is unclear, holding pyridostigmine is the safe move** — it is symptomatic only, and
    nothing is lost by stopping it while you secure the airway.

!!! warning "No ICU, no spirometer, no IVIG — the far-forward version"
    Everything above assumes a hospital. If you are in an aid station, afloat, or forward:

    - **Single-breath count is your FVC.** Count it hourly and write down every value — the trend
      is the whole signal.
    - **Request urgent MEDEVAC** the moment the count is ≤20, the patient cannot manage secretions,
      or **the count has fallen at all between checks.** Do not wait for a number, and do not wait
      for daylight.
    - **Intubate before transport, not during.** A patient who is marginal on the ground is
      unmanageable in an aircraft or a small boat.
    - **State the destination requirement in the evacuation request:** the receiving facility needs
      a **ventilator AND access to IVIG or plasma exchange.**
    - **While waiting:** sit them upright, suction, NPO, stop every drug on the avoid list, treat
      the precipitating infection with a non-avoid-list agent, **hold pyridostigmine if there are
      any muscarinic signs**, and **do not start high-dose steroids in an unmonitored setting.**

!!! orderset "Step 2 — rapid immunotherapy: PLEX preferred in crisis, IVIG if PLEX is unavailable"
    **~70% of patients with severe weakness improve with either.** But read that number carefully:
    the randomized equivalence data come from **exacerbation, not intubated crisis** (Gajdos 1997,
    n=87, no difference at day 15; Barth 2011, n=84 with QMG >10.5, **69% IVIG vs 65% PLEX**).
    **In crisis specifically, the non-randomized evidence favours PLEX** — superior severity at
    7 days, ventilation status at 2 weeks and 1-month function (Qureshi, n=54); earlier improvement
    and shorter ICU stay (Wang, n=40); and in a 250-case multicentre cohort, first-line
    PLEX/immunoadsorption meant **significantly less need for invasive ventilation.** A 2025
    Cochrane review found IVIg **may be less beneficial** than PLEX with possibly longer
    hospitalization (very low certainty).

    - **Plasma exchange — 5 sessions over 7–10 days**, 1.0–1.5 plasma volumes (40–60 mL/kg) each.
      **First-line in crisis absent a contraindication**, and particularly effective in **MuSK
      disease.** Daily vs alternate-day showed no efficacy difference. Improvement is usually
      evident **by the 2nd–3rd session.** Unusable in sepsis; carries haemodynamic and access risk.
    - **IVIG 2 g/kg total, divided over 2–5 days.** Easier logistically, no vascular access issue.
      Improvement usually **by the 4th–5th day**; effect lasts ~4–8 weeks. *Off-label — no immune
      globulin product carries an FDA myasthenia indication.* Contraindicated in hypercoagulable
      states, renal failure, and IgG hypersensitivity.
    - **Start or intensify chronic immunotherapy in parallel** — IVIG/PLEX is a bridge, not a
      maintenance plan. Steroids are usually begun or increased now (watch the early-worsening
      window, below).

!!! danger "IVIG boxed warning — thrombosis and acute renal failure"
    - **Thrombosis** can occur without known risk factors. Risk is higher with
      **estrogen-containing contraception**, prolonged immobilization, indwelling central lines,
      hypercoagulable states, hyperviscosity, and advanced age — several of which describe the
      hospitalized crisis patient.
    - **Renal dysfunction and acute renal failure**, more so with sucrose-containing products.
    - **Check baseline renal function, ensure adequate hydration, and use the lowest practicable
      dose and infusion rate** in at-risk patients. Watch for aseptic meningitis and haemolysis.

!!! warning "If a patient on a C5 inhibitor gets PLEX or IVIG, you owe them a supplemental dose"
    This page's own crisis pathway creates exactly this sequence. **PLEX, plasmapheresis and IVIG
    strip circulating drug and abolish complement blockade.** Both labels require make-up dosing:

    - **Eculizumab** — after each PLEX/plasmapheresis session: **300 mg** if the last dose was
      300 mg, **600 mg** if the last dose was ≥600 mg, given **within 60 minutes after** the
      session. With scheduled IVIG cycles: **300–600 mg** supplemental depending on last dose and
      IVIG frequency (**no supplement for one-off acute rescue IVIG**).
    - **Ravulizumab** — after **each** PLEX/plasmapheresis: **1,200–1,500 mg** by weight band;
      after **completion of an IVIG cycle**: **600–1,800 mg** by weight band and last dose — both
      **within 4 hours.**

!!! warning "Steroid early-worsening in crisis"
    High-dose corticosteroids cause transient worsening in **up to 50%** of patients, on average
    **about 5 days after initiation** and lasting **1–7 days** (occasionally up to 3 weeks) — a
    real hazard in a patient already near respiratory failure. **In severe disease, give PLEX or
    IVIG first and wait several days for benefit before starting corticosteroids.** If worsening
    does occur in a monitored setting, **generally maintain the dose until the patient improves
    rather than withdrawing it.** The full prescribing rules are in
    [Chronic treatment](#immunotherapy-tiers) — read them before you write the first dose.

## Chronic treatment

### Symptomatic

!!! orderset "Pyridostigmine — symptom control, not disease modification"
    - **Pyridostigmine 30–60 mg PO every 4–6 h**, titrated to symptoms. **Practical ceiling
      ~360–480 mg/day.**
        - *The FDA label describes an average dose of 600 mg/day and severe cases up to 1,500 mg/day
          — that reflects **pre-immunotherapy-era practice and is not a modern target.*** The
          operative threshold is **>450 mg/day**, above which depolarizing block itself causes
          weakness. Lower still in **renal impairment** — pyridostigmine is excreted essentially
          unchanged by the kidney.
    - **Extended-release 180 mg at bedtime** for overnight and on-waking weakness.
    - **Still symptomatic on a tolerated dose in the 300–480 mg/day range? That patient is
      inadequately controlled, not under-titrated — escalate to immunotherapy, do not push the
      dose higher.** Cholinesterase inhibitors rarely give complete or sustained relief and do not
      modify disease; immunosuppression is indicated whenever symptomatic treatment alone fails to
      give a fully satisfactory functional result.
    - Muscarinic side effects — cramping, diarrhea, secretions — are often covered with
      **glycopyrrolate 1 mg PO TID (max 8 mg/day)** or hyoscyamine. **Both labels list myasthenia
      gravis as a contraindication**; this is accepted off-label practice, so use the lowest
      effective dose, document the rationale — and read the cholinergic-crisis box above, because
      **this cover masks the warning signs of overdose.**
    - **Less effective, and often poorly tolerated, in MuSK disease** — do not lean on it there.
    - **Now supported by Class I evidence.** The first placebo-controlled randomized trial
      (IMPACT-MG, 2026) confirmed benefit across MGII, QMG and MG-ADL. The same trial found that
      **adding amifampridine on top of pyridostigmine did not help** and caused more adverse
      events — the [LEMS](lems.md) drug does not transfer to MG.

### Immunotherapy tiers

=== "First-line immunotherapy"

    !!! danger "Measure respiratory function before the first prednisone dose"
        Steroid-induced worsening hits **up to 50%** of patients, on average **~5 days** after
        initiation, lasting **1–7 days** — and is predicted by **bulbar symptoms, older age, and
        generalized disease.**

        - **Before the first dose:** single-breath count and FVC.
        - **Do not start high-dose prednisone as an outpatient** in anyone with bulbar or
          respiratory weakness — those patients are started **inpatient**, or covered with
          PLEX/IVIG first, or both. Pulse high-dose methylprednisolone should only rarely be used
          in MG.
        - **Give explicit return precautions:** *"if your swallowing, speech or breathing gets
          worse in the next two weeks, come back the same day — do not wait for your follow-up."*
        - **If worsening occurs in a monitored setting, maintain the dose until improvement rather
          than withdrawing it.**

    - **Corticosteroids (prednisone)** — the workhorse. Pick a regimen and write it down:
        - **Start-low-go-slow (outpatient default, lowest worsening risk):** **10–20 mg daily**,
          increased by 5–10 mg every 5–7 days to a target of **~0.75–1 mg/kg/day (typically
          40–60 mg daily)** or until control. The MGTX protocol used **10 mg on alternate days,
          increased in 10 mg increments to a maximum of 100 mg alternate-day or 1.5 mg/kg,
          whichever is lower.**
        - **Initiate-and-maintain:** **20 mg/day**, effective in the mycophenolate trials, with
          early addition of a steroid-sparing agent.
        - **High-dose induction** (0.75–1.0 mg/kg/day, or 60 mg/day) achieves pharmacological
          remission in ~28% and marked improvement in a further 53% — **but carries the worsening
          risk, so not outpatient in bulbar or respiratory disease.**
        - **Then:** hold ~1–3 months and taper slowly to the lowest effective dose or alternate-day
          dosing. **Avoid alternate-day dosing in diabetes** (glucose swings).
        - **Monitor:** glucose/HbA1c, BP, weight, K⁺, bone density with vitamin D and calcium,
          cataract and IOP screening on prolonged use. **Contraindicated in systemic fungal
          infection.** Do not stop abruptly after prolonged use — adrenal suppression.
        - *Labelled systemic range is 5–60 mg/day; the MG titration schedules above are consensus
          practice, not label.*
    - **Steroid-sparing oral agents** — started early to get patients off steroids:
        - **Azathioprine** — best-established. **Start 50 mg PO daily (~1 mg/kg/day); after 6–8
          weeks increase by 0.5 mg/kg/day at 4-week intervals to a maximum of 2.5 mg/kg/day.**
          Onset is slow — allow a **12-week minimum trial** before calling it a failure. Boxed
          warning: **malignancy.**
            - **Check TPMT *and* NUDT15 before starting** — the current label covers both.
              Homozygous deficiency of either → use a different agent; heterozygous → reduce the
              dose; heterozygous for both → reduce further.
            - **CBC with platelets weekly × 1 month, twice monthly for months 2–3, then monthly.**
              Add LFTs periodically. Delayed marrow suppression occurs.
            - **Allopurinol → reduce azathioprine to one-third to one-quarter of the usual dose.
              Febuxostat is not recommended.** Lower doses in renal impairment.
        - **Mycophenolate mofetil** — **1,000 mg PO BID** typical in MG (off-label); onset over
          months. Monitor CBC; reduce or interrupt for neutropenia.

            !!! danger "Mycophenolate — boxed warning: embryofetal toxicity. Read before prescribing to any woman of childbearing potential."
                - **First-trimester pregnancy loss and multiple congenital malformations.**
                  Consider **azathioprine instead** in anyone who may become pregnant. There is a
                  **REMS** and a pregnancy registry.
                - **Two pregnancy tests** (sensitivity ≥25 mIU/mL): immediately before the first
                  dose and again **8–10 days later**; repeat at routine follow-up.
                - **Contraception from 4 weeks before, throughout, and for 6 weeks after** the last
                  dose. **Mycophenolate lowers oral-contraceptive efficacy — a barrier method must
                  be added.** A woman on a COC alone is not protected.
                - Also boxed: **malignancy** (lymphoma, skin) and **serious infection**, including
                  HBV/HCV reactivation.

        - Others in selected patients, **all off-label in MG**:
            - **Methotrexate — 7.5–20 mg PO or SC ONCE WEEKLY** (never daily) **+ folic acid 1 mg
              daily.** Monitor CBC, LFTs, creatinine. **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy**
              for non-neoplastic disease (boxed warning); effective contraception in both sexes.
              **Daily-instead-of-weekly dosing errors have killed patients — write the day of the
              week on the prescription.**
            - **Tacrolimus — 3–5 mg PO daily**, trough-guided (~5–10 ng/mL); monitor levels, K⁺,
              creatinine, glucose, BP, QTc. Boxed: malignancy and serious infection.
            - **Cyclosporine — 3–5 mg/kg/day PO divided BID**, trough-guided; monitor creatinine
              and BP. Boxed: malignancy, serious infection, hypertension, nephrotoxicity.

=== "Refractory / steroid-dependent — targeted biologics"

    The class that reorganized MG care. Reserve for inadequate control on, or intolerance of,
    conventional immunotherapy — and note the **serostatus restrictions**, which are the practical
    decision driver.

    !!! note "No class has beaten another head-to-head"
        A 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis (27 randomized trials, n=2,318) found FcRn
        inhibitors, C5 inhibitors and CD19 depletion **statistically indistinguishable** on QMG and
        MG-ADL; a German real-world propensity-matched comparison of C5 vs efgartigimod (n=153)
        found the same, **with insufficient response in 20–49% of patients in both arms.**
        **Choose on serostatus, route, dosing architecture and cost — not on presumed potency.**
        Two practical differentiators: **cyclical FcRn dosing requires symptom worsening to trigger
        the next cycle**, which creates timing uncertainty, whereas nipocalimab and the complement
        agents run on fixed schedules; and **B-cell depletion accrues effect over time** — MINT's
        AChR-positive benefit widened between week 26 and week 52.

    **Age floors differ by agent:** eculizumab **≥6 y**, nipocalimab **≥12 y**; ravulizumab,
    zilucoplan, rozanolixizumab, efgartigimod and inebilizumab are **adults only.**

    **FcRn inhibitors** (lower total IgG, including pathogenic antibody):

    - **Efgartigimod (Vyvgart IV; Vyvgart Hytrulo SC** with hyaluronidase) — **the serostatus gate
      is gone: FDA broadened the gMG indication to ALL adult serotypes on 8 May 2026** (AChR+,
      MuSK+, LRP4+ and triple-seronegative), on the ADAPT SERON trial. **It is the only approved
      agent with no serostatus restriction, and the only approved option for the seronegative
      patient.** Hytrulo also carries a CIDP indication.
        - **IV: 10 mg/kg over 1 h once weekly × 4** (a "cycle"); cap at 1,200 mg/dose if ≥120 kg.
        - **SC (Hytrulo): 1,000 mg/10,000 U prefilled syringe over 20–30 s once weekly × 4.**
        - Subsequent cycles on clinical evaluation.
    - **Rozanolixizumab (Rystiggo, SC)** — **AChR+ or MuSK+.** **SC infusion once weekly × 6,
      weight-banded: <50 kg 420 mg; 50–<100 kg 560 mg; ≥100 kg 840 mg.** Do not start the next
      cycle sooner than **63 days** from the start of the previous one. **Watch for aseptic
      meningitis** — labelled and serious; headache, fever and meningism on treatment warrant
      workup, not reassurance. Angioedema and rash are also labelled.
    - **Nipocalimab (Imaavy)** — **FDA-approved 29 Apr 2025**, AChR+ or MuSK+, adults and
      **adolescents ≥12**. **30 mg/kg IV over ≥30 min once, then 15 mg/kg IV over ≥15 min two weeks
      later and every 2 weeks thereafter. IV only — no SC formulation is approved.**
    - *Not prescribable, and worth knowing why:* batoclimab's phase 3 was **positive**, but the
      sponsor **is not filing for approval in MG** — development moved to the next-generation FcRn
      blocker IMVT-1402. **Do not expect a batoclimab label.**
    - **All FcRn agents:** evaluate and update age-appropriate vaccines **before each new cycle**;
      live vaccines are not recommended during treatment, and non-live vaccine responses are
      blunted — time them to the end of a cycle.

    **Complement C5 inhibitors** (block terminal complement — **AChR+ only**, since complement
    damage at the endplate is the AChR mechanism; **ineffective in MuSK** disease):

    - **Eculizumab (Soliris)** — **900 mg IV weekly × 4, then 1,200 mg IV at week 5, then 1,200 mg
      IV every 2 weeks.** *Do not start at q2wk — that skips the entire induction month.*
      **Now also approved in children ≥6 (AChR+, Feb 2025)** — the only MG biologic approved in
      children. **Biosimilars are marketed and carry the same gMG indication** (BKEMV,
      interchangeable; EPYSQLI) — expect step-therapy through one.
    - **Ravulizumab (Ultomiris)** — **weight-based load, then q8wk maintenance starting 2 weeks
      after the load** (gMG, ≥40 kg): 40–<60 kg **2,400 → 3,000 mg**; 60–<100 kg **2,700 → 3,300
      mg**; ≥100 kg **3,000 → 3,600 mg.**
    - **Zilucoplan (Zilbrysq)** — **SC once daily, weight-banded: <56 kg 16.6 mg; 56–<77 kg 23 mg;
      ≥77 kg 32.4 mg**, self-injected after training. **Obtain baseline amylase and lipase before
      starting** — pancreatitis and pancreatic cysts are labelled; stop for suspected pancreatitis.
    - **The prescriber must enrol in a REMS** for all three. This gates access — start it before
      the patient needs the drug.

    !!! danger "C5 inhibitors — boxed warning: life-threatening and fatal meningococcal infection"
        - **Vaccinate against serogroups A, C, W, Y *and* B — that is two separate vaccines
          (MenACWY + MenB) — ≥2 weeks before the first dose**, per current ACIP schedules for
          complement-inhibitor recipients, which differ from the package insert. *MenACWY alone
          leaves the patient exposed to the serogroup that classically kills them.*
        - **If therapy is urgent and the patient is not up to date, do not delay two weeks.** The
          labels are explicit: give **antibacterial prophylaxis** and vaccinate as soon as
          possible. The risk of delaying therapy can outweigh the infection risk.
        - **Vaccination does not eliminate the risk** — breakthrough meningococcal disease occurs
          in vaccinated patients. Counsel on symptoms and evaluate immediately if suspected.
        - Same rules as in [NMOSD](../ms/nmosd.md).

    **B-cell depletion:**

    - **Inebilizumab (Uplizna)** — anti-CD19. **FDA-approved for gMG 11 Dec 2025** (AChR+ **or**
      MuSK+, MINT trial); its third indication after NMOSD and IgG4-RD. **300 mg IV, repeat 300 mg
      IV at 2 weeks, then 300 mg IV every 6 months** (infuse over ~90 min; observe ≥1 h after).
      *Note the MuSK-positive QMG difference in MINT was not statistically significant.*
        - **Before the first dose: HBsAg/anti-HBc, quantitative serum IgG, and TB screening** —
          **active hepatitis B and active or untreated latent TB are contraindications.**
        - **Premedicate every infusion** — IV methylprednisolone 80–125 mg + PO diphenhydramine
          25–50 mg + PO acetaminophen 500–650 mg.
        - **Live vaccines ≥4 weeks before starting**, and none afterwards until B-cell repletion.
          **Contraception during and for 6 months after** the last dose.
    - **Rituximab** (anti-CD20) — **off-label; no FDA myasthenia indication exists.** State the
      regimen you mean: commonly **375 mg/m² IV weekly × 4**, or **1 g IV × 2 doses two weeks
      apart.** **Screen HBsAg and anti-HBc before the first dose** (boxed warning); the IV product
      also carries a boxed warning for **fatal infusion-related reactions.**
        - **Particularly effective in MuSK disease**, where consensus supports it as an *early*
          option rather than a last resort. **In AChR-positive generalized MG the consensus
          position is weaker** — an option after failure or intolerance of other agents — **but
          randomized evidence now also supports early rituximab within 1 year of generalized
          onset**, a position the 2025 ABN guidelines have adopted.

### Serostatus is the practical routing tool

| Situation | Favoured approach |
|---|---|
| **AChR+ generalized** | Pyridostigmine + steroids/steroid-sparing; **discuss thymectomy** if non-thymomatous; escalate to **FcRn, complement, or inebilizumab** — the only group with all three classes available |
| **MuSK+** | Pyridostigmine often poorly tolerated; **rituximab early**; **FcRn (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab, nipocalimab)** or inebilizumab; **complement inhibitors do not work**; **thymectomy does not help** |
| **Seronegative generalized** | **Send a clustered-AChR CBA first — most "seronegative" patients are assay-negative, not antibody-negative.** If truly triple-seronegative: pyridostigmine + steroids/steroid-sparing (not serostatus-restricted), then **efgartigimod — the only FDA-approved biologic for this group** (all-serotype label, May 2026). Complement inhibitors, rozanolixizumab, nipocalimab and inebilizumab all remain serostatus-restricted. IVIG/PLEX and off-label rituximab are the fallbacks. *Efficacy data in true triple-seronegative disease are small and largely uncontrolled — say so to the patient.* |
| **Any thymoma** | **Surgical resection regardless** of severity or serostatus — but see the exceptions and the timing rule below |
| **Ocular-only** | Pyridostigmine first; **add corticosteroids (~15 mg/day is often enough) if symptoms are functionally limiting or troublesome** — this is a positive recommendation, not a coin-flip. Whether it *prevents generalization* is supported by consistent observational data awaiting randomized confirmation |
| **Crisis / refractory** | **PLEX first-line in crisis** (IVIG if PLEX unavailable); escalate chronic therapy in parallel |

## Thymectomy

**Thymoma present → surgical resection**, as oncologic surgery: *"with rare exceptions, all
patients with MG with thymoma should undergo surgery to remove the tumor."* Two caveats the word
"always" hides:

- **Resection is done to remove the tumour and may not improve the MG.** All thymic tissue is
  removed with it. Incompletely resected thymoma is managed with post-operative
  radiotherapy/chemotherapy.
- **Exceptions exist:** in elderly or multimorbid patients palliative radiotherapy can be
  considered, and **small thymomas may be followed** unless enlarging or symptomatic.
- **Timing — thymectomy is elective.** It *"should be performed when the patient is stable and
  deemed safe to undergo a procedure where postoperative pain and mechanical factors can limit
  respiratory function."* **Do not operate on an unstable myasthenic.**

**No thymoma → discuss thymectomy in AChR-positive generalized MG.** The AAN puts this at
**Level B — clinicians *should discuss* it**, not "do it." **MGTX** randomized thymectomy plus
prednisone against prednisone alone and found it superior — better time-weighted QMG, **lower
steroid requirement**, less azathioprine and IVIG use, **fewer hospitalizations for exacerbation**
— **sustained through 5 years**, with an absolute increase in the chance of reaching minimal
manifestation status at 36 months of **20% (95% CI 1.6–37%).**

- **Age — the number that is usually quoted wrong.** MGTX *enrolled* patients under 65 with disease
  duration <5 years, **but in post hoc analysis neither prednisone dose nor QMG differed between
  arms in patients ≥50.** So: **best randomized evidence is ages 18–50**, and several consensus
  documents restrict the recommendation there.
    - **Above 50, the randomized evidence does not demonstrate benefit — but observational data
      do.** A multicentre propensity-matched study in late-onset AChR-positive non-thymomatous MG
      found thymectomy conferred a **2.36-fold higher chance of remission or minimal manifestation
      status (48.9% vs 23.8% at 24 months; 59.5% vs 28.9% at 36 months)**, with adverse-event rates
      comparable to younger patients. A second cohort found HR 3.25 (95% CI 1.31–8.1). Both call
      for a randomized trial. Some experts consider thymectomy in the 50–65 group especially when
      thymic hyperplasia is present or biomarkers resemble early-onset disease.
- **Surgical route — counsel the uncertainty, don't resolve it.** **MGTX used extended transsternal
  thymectomy.** The AAN (Level B) directs clinicians to counsel patients that **it is uncertain
  whether the benefit of extended transsternal thymectomy is also attained by minimally invasive
  approaches.** This is contested — some authorities hold that video- and robot-assisted methods
  give the same benefit **provided all thymic tissue including mediastinal fat is removed.**
  Present both positions.
- **MuSK, LRP4, and agrin antibodies** — *"current evidence does not support an indication for
  thymectomy."*
- **Purely ocular disease** — not routine, and the data are limited, retrospective and largely
  negative (47 thymectomized ocular patients vs 67 who declined: no difference in stable remission
  at 100–116 months; another series, remission in 2 of 11). The consensus carve-out is narrow:
  **AChR-positive ocular MG patients who fail anticholinesterases and who prefer not to take,
  cannot take, or are refractory to immunosuppression may be *offered* thymectomy.**
- **Seronegative generalized MG** — thymectomy *"may be considered"* if immunosuppression fails or
  is not tolerated.

## Prescribing for a patient with MG — read before you write

!!! warning "The precipitant list — written for the covering provider, not just the neurologist"
    Give every patient a copy. But this box is aimed at **you** — the ED doc reaching for cipro,
    the anesthesiologist, the OB starting magnesium — who may not know this patient has MG.

    **Avoid — highest priority:**

    - **Fluoroquinolones** (cipro/levo/moxi/ofloxacin) — FDA boxed warning in MG. Guidance grades
      them *"use cautiously, if at all"* — in practice, avoid if any alternative exists.
    - **D-penicillamine** — *causes* an MG-like syndrome. Avoid.
    - **Botulinum toxin** — presynaptic NMJ blocker. Avoid.
    - **IV magnesium — treat as contraindicated.** See the pregnancy box below.
    - **Telithromycin** — caused severe, often fatal worsening and was contraindicated; **withdrawn
      from the US market since 2016.** Listed for completeness only.

    **Use only if unavoidable, and watch closely:**

    - **Aminoglycosides**, **macrolides** (azithro/clarithro/erythromycin), **clindamycin**,
      **colistin/polymyxins**, **lincomycin**.
    - **Beta-blockers**, **procainamide**, **quinine/quinidine**.
    - **Statins** — can worsen or unmask MG.
    - **Hydroxychloroquine/chloroquine.**
    - **Live-attenuated vaccines** (MMR, varicella-zoster, intranasal influenza, oral polio,
      rotavirus, oral typhoid, smallpox, yellow fever) — these do not affect MG itself but are
      **contraindicated on immunosuppressive treatment.** **Give age-appropriate vaccines *before*
      starting an FcRn blocker, complement inhibitor, or inebilizumab — not after.**
    - **Iodinated radiologic contrast** (older reports; modern agents appear safer) and
      **desferrioxamine.**

    **What to give instead — and the mirror error to avoid:**

    **Reasonable antibiotic alternatives:** penicillins and cephalosporins, doxycycline,
    vancomycin, metronidazole, TMP-SMX. **But do not let this list stop you treating a serious
    infection** — untreated sepsis is the commonest cause of myasthenic crisis and a bigger threat
    than any drug here. If no alternative exists, **give the drug, tell the patient and the team
    why, and monitor single-breath count and FVC every 4 hours for 48 hours** with an airway plan
    in place.

    **Contraindications on the labels of the drugs this page recommends:**

    - **Pyridostigmine** — mechanical intestinal or urinary obstruction; caution in bronchial asthma.
    - **Eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan** — do not initiate in **unresolved serious
      meningococcal infection.**
    - **Inebilizumab** — **active hepatitis B**; **active or untreated latent TB.**
    - **Methotrexate** — **pregnancy** (absolute, for non-neoplastic disease).
    - **Prednisone** — systemic fungal infection.
    - **Glycopyrrolate / hyoscyamine** — labelled contraindicated **in myasthenia gravis** itself.

!!! danger "Checkpoint-inhibitor myasthenia is a different disease — and the patient dies of the heart"
    Not a cautionary drug interaction. A distinct, fulminant entity that a covering provider meets
    in an oncology patient with **no MG history.**

    - **Median onset ~3 weeks** after the first dose (usually within 6 weeks).
    - **It overlaps with myocarditis and myositis** — the "triple-M" syndrome — in a large
      minority, with **in-hospital mortality around 38%.**
    - **In any patient on pembrolizumab, nivolumab or ipilimumab with new ptosis, diplopia, bulbar
      or proximal weakness: send troponin, CK and an ECG, and put them on a monitor.**
    - **AChR antibody is often negative or low-titre — a negative panel does not exclude it.**
    - **Stop the ICI and start high-dose corticosteroids on suspicion**, without waiting for
      confirmation; add IVIG/PLEX for bulbar or respiratory involvement. Escalate to cardiology and
      oncology the same night.

!!! danger "IV magnesium, eclampsia, and pregnancy"
    **Treat IV magnesium as contraindicated in MG** — it blocks neuromuscular transmission and can
    precipitate or deepen crisis. The situation where a provider feels forced to give it is
    **eclampsia or pre-eclampsia prophylaxis.**

    - **Discuss alternative seizure prophylaxis (e.g. levetiracetam) with OB and neurology before
      giving magnesium.**
    - If magnesium is judged unavoidable, give it **only with an airway plan, continuous
      respiratory monitoring, IV calcium gluconate immediately available, and reduced dosing with
      serial magnesium levels.**
    - Oral magnesium-containing antacids and laxatives are also best avoided.
    - **Pregnancy and the first postpartum months are a high-risk exacerbation window.** MG in
      pregnancy is a co-managed, hospital-delivery, no-surprises situation — and remember that
      **mycophenolate and methotrexate are contraindicated**, so review the regimen *before*
      conception.

!!! tip "TRICARE — MG prescribing"
    Pyridostigmine, prednisone, azathioprine, and mycophenolate are **generic/formulary.** The
    **targeted biologics** (all FcRn inhibitors, all complement inhibitors, inebilizumab) are
    **high-cost specialty drugs, PA-gated.** Expect a **specialist-prescriber or
    neurologist-consultation requirement** and documentation of serostatus and failed conventional
    therapy.

    - **Start with the generics and document them.** Pyridostigmine, prednisone, and azathioprine
      or mycophenolate are the conventional trials every biologic PA will ask you to have failed —
      **record drug, dose, and duration for each from the first visit**, not retrospectively.
    - **Serostatus drives coverage as well as efficacy.** Complement C5 inhibitors are FDA-labeled
      for **AChR-antibody-positive** gMG only, and payer criteria generally track the label —
      expect an AChR+ requirement on any complement request and route the MuSK patient accordingly.
      **Efgartigimod is now the exception: its all-serotype label (May 2026) removes the serostatus
      argument entirely**, which matters most for MuSK and seronegative patients. Published
      criteria for this class typically also require **MGFA class II–IV** and failure of or
      contraindication to **≥2 conventional therapies**, and bar concurrent use of two gMG
      biologics.
    - **Rituximab is off-label in MG — argue it as off-label, not as formulary.** TRICARE evaluates
      off-label use on **medical necessity plus support from medical literature and national
      organizations**, not on formulary tier. For MuSK disease that argument is available: cite the
      2020 International Consensus Guidance, the MuSK-specific response data, and the mechanistic
      reason complement inhibitors are unavailable.
    - **Benefit split decides where the drug is given and who bills.** Infused agents (eculizumab,
      ravulizumab, inebilizumab, **IV nipocalimab**, IV efgartigimod) typically run through the
      **medical benefit**; self-administered SC agents (zilucoplan, SC efgartigimod,
      rozanolixizumab) through **specialty pharmacy**, which sets the copay tier.
    - **Eculizumab is a biosimilar market now** (BKEMV, interchangeable; EPYSQLI) — both carry the
      adult AChR+ gMG indication and cost substantially less, so expect step-therapy through one.
      The **meningococcal boxed warning and REMS apply identically** to all three.
    - **IVIG — split the two settings.** **Inpatient crisis IVIG runs under the inpatient benefit;
      do not delay it for an authorization.** **Outpatient maintenance or bridging IVIG** is a
      medical-benefit authorization — open it the day you decide the patient will need it, because
      the lag is unprotected time.
    - **Verify every agent and its criteria at the [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst)
      before counseling — the DoD P&T Committee revises tiers and PA criteria without notice.**
      *The specifics above are the general payer pattern; TRICARE-specific criteria were not
      independently verified for this page.*

!!! military "Military medicine — myasthenia gravis"
    *Framework — verify against current **AR 40-501**, **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2**, **DoDI 1332.18**, and
    service aeromedical policy. See [Profiles, MEB/MAR2 & deployability](../military/deployability.md).*

    - **Retention — and the carve-out that decides the case.** **AR 40-501 para 3-31c** lists
      **myasthenia gravis, *"Unless clinically restricted to extraocular muscles."*** The DoD floor
      is **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.26.l**, *"myasthenia gravis, unless only involving extraocular
      muscles."* Both retain purely ocular disease and disqualify generalized disease — but they
      are **not structurally identical**, and the difference matters:
        - **MG is *not* an "upon diagnosis" condition.** Unlike epilepsy (5.26.k) and dementia
          (5.26.d), which carry *"Paragraph 5.26.a. does not apply,"* MG **stays gated by 5.26.a**:
          the condition must *persist despite appropriate treatment* **and** *preclude satisfactory
          performance of required duties.* **Document both prongs explicitly** — a board memo that
          skips them is the mis-cited document.
        - Add the **AR 40-501 3-1** criteria generalized MG most readily meets: **3-1b**
          (medication requiring frequent physician monitoring) and **3-1d** (dependence on frequent
          special treatments or clinical monitoring).
    - **The ocular carve-out does not end the retention analysis.** Check and document three more
      standards before concluding "retains": **AR 40-501 3-7b / DoDI 6130.03 V2 5.4.e** (binocular
      diplopia "not correctable by surgery, that is severe, constant, and in a zone less than 20
      degrees from primary position" — **in MG the fluctuating character usually defeats
      "constant," so say so rather than leaving it unaddressed**); **3-31k**, residual symptoms
      after adequate treatment meeting the para 3-1 definition; and **3-1b/3-1d**, which chronic
      immunotherapy or scheduled infusion can meet **even in ocular-only disease.**
    - **Deployability: generalized MG is non-deployable**, on multiple independent grounds:
        - **Crisis risk** — a respiratory emergency that needs IVIG/PLEX and an ICU is
          incompatible with an austere or afloat environment.
        - **Infusion/monitoring-dependent therapy** and **immunosuppression.**
        - **Cold chain is the hard constraint.** Every approved biologic here — and IVIG — requires
          continuous **2–8 °C** storage. That fails before the infusion schedule does.
        - **Live vaccines vs immunosuppression.** Theater-entry immunization requirements commonly
          include live vaccines that are contraindicated on immunosuppressive therapy — a
          theater-entry blocker independent of how well the MG is controlled.
        - **Complement inhibitors create a standing meningococcal risk** (boxed warning, REMS,
          revaccination schedule) that is hard to manage forward and dangerous in a crowded field
          environment.
        - **Nerve-agent pretreatment conflict.** A Soldier already on therapeutic pyridostigmine
          cannot take standard **pyridostigmine bromide** pretreatment and is hypersensitive to
          cholinesterase inhibition generally — see [nerve agent / organophosphate](nerve-agent.md).
          Raise it before a CBRN-threat assignment.
        - **Heat, exertion, and sleep deprivation all worsen fatigable weakness** — the operational
          environment is a direct provocation.
        - A long list of **common field/formulary drugs are contraindicated** (fluoroquinolones and
          aminoglycosides especially) — a real constraint on deployed medical support.
        - *If retention is being contemplated, **zilucoplan** is the only self-administered daily SC
          regimen and the only one with any field plausibility — and it still needs the cold chain.*
    - **Profile (DA Form 3349).** MG profiles on the **P** factor (DA Pam 40-502 4-3c(1) — nervous
      system falls under *physical capacity/stamina*); add **E** where there is visual impairment.
        - **Temporary** during induction, titration, or post-thymectomy recovery — ≤90 days per
          instrument, and **link every extension** so the days-on-profile count is real.
        - Convert to **permanent** at clinical MRDP or the **12-month administrative MRDP**,
          whichever comes first. Generalized disease → **permanent P-3** (a permanent 3 or 4 is
          itself the DES referral, AR 40-501 3-4). Jumping straight to permanent invites a
          premature referral.
        - Section 4 limits should be functional and specific: no sustained overhead or repetitive
          exertion; no sustained load-carriage; no duty requiring uninterrupted alertness or
          reliable speech/swallow; no high-heat exposure; **no sustained wear of a protective mask**
          where respiratory reserve is reduced.
    - **Ocular-only: retain on a documented surveillance plan, not on an assumption.** Roughly
      **4 in 10 generalize** (fewer in treated cohorts), most **within 1–2 years.** Carry the
      Soldier on a **linked temporary profile** through that window at a stated interval —
      **q3 months for 24 months**, matching the DA Pam 40-502 3-month functional-reassessment
      requirement — recording at each visit **baseline and serial FVC and single-breath count**, a
      bulbar screen (speech, swallow, chew, neck flexion), limb fatigability, and repeat serology
      if initially seronegative. **Conversion triggers** — any bulbar or respiratory symptom, any
      limb fatigability, a falling FVC, or a crisis — end the carve-out: the disease is no longer
      "clinically restricted to extraocular muscles," 3-31c applies, and you write the permanent
      **P-3.**
    - **EPTS / LOD — and the 6-month fork.** MG symptoms often predate diagnosis by years; date the
      **first attributable symptom** (fluctuating ptosis/diplopia, chewing fatigue, exertional
      dyspnea) and review pre-accession records. **AR 40-501 3-2, Note:** for conditions predating
      enlistment, **accession standards govern the first 6 months of AD** (separation via
      **EPSBD**); after 6 months Chapter 3 retention standards govern. **This matters more in MG
      than almost anywhere else, because the two standards do not match** — DoDI 6130.03 **Vol 1
      6.26.q** disqualifies a *history* of MG with **no extraocular exception**, while AR 40-501
      3-31c retains ocular-only disease. Also check **3-3c**: a condition known and **waived at
      accession**, essentially unchanged in severity and not meeting para 3-1, is **not** a DES
      referral. If the diagnosis was made in service, state LOD affirmatively and say what you
      reviewed.
    - **MEB/DES:** refer generalized MG. Document serostatus, crisis history, respiratory function
      (FVC), medication trials, and duty impact. **A first crisis is a sentinel event** — crisis is
      respiratory or bulbar **by definition**, so the disease is no longer "clinically restricted to
      extraocular muscles" and the 3-31c carve-out is unavailable as a matter of text. **Whether the
      Soldier is ultimately found fit is the PEB's decision, not yours — write the standard and the
      evidence, not the outcome.**
    - **Aeromedical: disqualifying for flying duty — and the retention carve-out does not carry
      over.** **AR 40-501 Chapter 4 never names myasthenia gravis.** The chain is **4-27
      (Neurological)** → *"the causes in the accession standards plus the following"* → the
      accession standard **DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 para 6.26.q**, which disqualifies a **history** of
      myasthenia gravis with **no extraocular exception.** **So a Soldier retained under the 3-31c
      ocular carve-out is still aeromedically disqualified — on the history alone, with or without
      current diplopia.** **4-24** (systemic) and **4-24a** (therapeutic medications; see the
      medication APLs) apply independently, as do the ocular-motility standards at **4-4g**.
      Disposition and any waiver run through **USAAMA** and the applicable **Aeromedical Policy
      Letters** (4-1c), not through Chapter 3. Non-Army Services follow their own aeromedical
      policy — confirm before counseling.
    - **Vaccination collides with every drug on this page.** Get **all live vaccines done before
      starting** — inebilizumab requires them **≥4 weeks** ahead and none afterwards until B-cell
      repletion; FcRn agents and rituximab exclude live vaccines during treatment. For complement
      inhibitors the requirement runs the other way: **MenACWY + MenB ≥2 weeks before the first
      dose.** Non-live vaccine responses are blunted on FcRn blockade — time them to the end of a
      cycle, and re-evaluate vaccine status before each new cycle.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

**The lesion is the endplate.** In AChR-positive MG, antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor
drive **complement-mediated destruction of the postsynaptic membrane** — the folds flatten, receptor
density falls, and the safety factor of neuromuscular transmission collapses, so repeated firing
fails progressively. That complement mechanism is exactly why **C5 inhibitors work in AChR disease.**
**MuSK** antibodies are IgG4 and act differently — they disrupt the MuSK–LRP4 clustering pathway
that maintains the receptor array **without prominent complement activation**, which is precisely
why **complement inhibitors fail in MuSK disease** while **B-cell depletion (rituximab) works so
well.** The serostatus is not a label; it is the mechanism, and it predicts which drug will work.

**A decade that transformed the refractory patient.** For most of MG's history, the patient who
failed steroids and azathioprine had few options. Since 2017, three mechanistic classes have
arrived: **complement inhibition** (eculizumab, then ravulizumab and zilucoplan), **FcRn blockade**
(efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab, and nipocalimab, which recycles less pathogenic IgG), and now
**anti-CD19 depletion** (inebilizumab, approved for gMG in December 2025). Refractory MG went from
a management dead-end to a menu — the harder question now is sequencing and cost, not whether an
option exists.

**And in 2026 the last serostatus wall came down.** Every targeted agent had been gated by antibody
status, which left the **triple-seronegative** patient — perhaps 10–15% of generalized MG — with
nothing approved. **On 8 May 2026 the FDA removed efgartigimod's AChR-positive restriction**, on the
strength of **ADAPT SERON**, making it the only approved therapy covering MuSK-positive,
LRP4-positive and seronegative disease alike. Before reaching for it, though, send a
**clustered-AChR cell-based assay**: most "seronegative" patients are assay-negative rather than
antibody-negative, and finding the antibody reopens every other option.

**Not every immune target works at the endplate.** The winners are easy to list; the failures are
the more useful lesson. Since 2024, **IL-6 blockade** (satralizumab — statistically positive but
clinically marginal, development halted), **oral factor D inhibition** (vemircopan — missed every
endpoint, terminated early), and **BTK inhibition** (tolebrutinib — phase 3 discontinued) have all
fallen out. **Batoclimab** succeeded in phase 3 and was then abandoned commercially in favour of a
next-generation molecule. The classes that work are the ones that **remove IgG or block terminal
complement.**

**No class has beaten another head-to-head.** A 2026 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 27
randomized trials (n=2,318) found FcRn inhibitors, C5 inhibitors and CD19 depletion **comparable**
on QMG and MG-ADL, and a real-world propensity-matched comparison agreed — **while noting
insufficient response in 20–49% of patients in both arms.** Sequencing is therefore driven by
serostatus, route, dosing rhythm and cost. Two more complement agents are at the FDA door, and both
would change the logistics rather than the efficacy: **cemdisiran**, an siRNA silencing hepatic C5
dosed **subcutaneously every 12 weeks** (FDA action date November 2026), and **gefurulimab**, a
self-administered SC nanobody. Neither is approved.

**Thymectomy earned its place by trial.** MGTX was the randomized evidence that settled a
decades-old practice: in non-thymomatous AChR-positive generalized MG, **thymectomy plus prednisone
beat prednisone alone**, and the benefit — better disease control, less steroid, fewer
exacerbations — **held through 5 years.**

**Key evidence:**

- **MGTX (NEJM 2016; Lancet Neurology 2019 extension)** — thymectomy + prednisone superior to
  prednisone alone in non-thymomatous AChR+ generalized MG; benefit sustained to 5 years. **Post
  hoc: no difference in patients ≥50.**
- **REGAIN** — eculizumab in refractory AChR+ gMG.
- **CHAMPION-MG** — ravulizumab; **RAISE** — zilucoplan.
- **ADAPT** — efgartigimod, the only pivotal gMG trial to enroll regardless of antibody status;
  **ADAPT SERON** — basis of the May 2026 all-serotype expansion.
- **MycarinG** — rozanolixizumab; **Vivacity-MG3** — nipocalimab.
- **MINT** — inebilizumab in AChR+ or MuSK+ gMG (basis of the Dec 2025 approval); AChR+ benefit
  widened between weeks 26 and 52.
- **IMPACT-MG (2026)** — first placebo-controlled trial of pyridostigmine, Class I evidence; and
  the negative arm showing **amifampridine adds nothing** on top of it.
- **Barth 2011** — the IVIG-vs-PLEX randomized head-to-head (69% vs 65%), in *moderate-to-severe
  worsening MG, not intubated crisis.*
- **Menon 2024 (Neurology)** — 154-patient propensity-matched ocular MG cohort; immunosuppression
  associated with roughly halved generalization risk.
- **International Consensus Guidance for Management of MG, 2020 update** (Narayanaswami et al.,
  *Neurology* 2021) — **still current, AAN-affirmed 14 Jul 2022. It has been supplemented, not
  replaced, by national guidance — and it predates nipocalimab, zilucoplan, rozanolixizumab and
  inebilizumab**, so the biologic sequencing on this page reflects labels and trials, not guideline
  text.

## Related neuromuscular-junction disorders

MG is the postsynaptic, autoimmune prototype — but fatigable or acute weakness at the NMJ has
other causes that are treated completely differently:

- **[Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)](lems.md)** — *presynaptic* antibody disease,
  often paraneoplastic. The MG mimic that is really a lung-cancer search.
- **[Botulism](botulism.md)** — *presynaptic toxin*; descending paralysis, an antitoxin emergency.
- **[Nerve agent / organophosphate toxicity](nerve-agent.md)** — poisoning of the synaptic enzyme;
  a CBRN and pesticide emergency treated with atropine and pralidoxime.
