# Neurology in pregnancy and postpartum

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: November 2026</span>

**One-liner:** In pregnancy or after delivery, check blood pressure and ask what changed about the headache, seizure, or deficit. Start obstetric and neurologic evaluation in parallel.

!!! danger "Call obstetrics and the appropriate emergency team early"
    | Presentation | Immediate action |
    |---|---|
    | New severe headache, visual symptoms, confusion or seizure in the second half of pregnancy or within six weeks postpartum | Consider preeclampsia/eclampsia and PRES. Repeat BP promptly. Stabilize, check glucose and activate the obstetric emergency pathway. Assess other causes in parallel. |
    | Acute BP **≥160 systolic or ≥110 diastolic** persisting **15 minutes** | Activate the severe hypertension bundle. Aim for treatment within **30–60 minutes of confirmed severe hypertension**. Coordinate targets with stroke and obstetrics if acute stroke is also suspected. |
    | Thunderclap, focal deficit, progressive headache with papilledema, or seizure with atypical course | Activate the appropriate stroke or emergency pathway. Consider hemorrhage, CVST, RCVS, dissection or pituitary apoplexy. Check last known well and image promptly. |
    | Headache after neuraxial anesthesia | Ask about posture and call obstetric anesthesia. A nonpostural or changing headache, seizure, fever, hypertension or focal finding warrants evaluation for other causes. |

## What to do in order

!!! orderset "Step 1 — Stabilize and choose imaging for the actual question"
    - Record gestational age or postpartum day, BP trend, last known well, recent neuraxial procedure, anticoagulant exposure and baseline neurologic deficits. Check point-of-care glucose in seizure or altered consciousness.
    - If preeclampsia is possible, obtain platelets, creatinine, liver enzymes and urine protein assessment per obstetric protocol. Avoid waiting for proteinuria before acting on severe symptoms or severe BP.
    - **Suspected stroke:** get emergent brain imaging using the local stroke pathway. CT and CTA when indicated should proceed if they are the fastest way to answer the acute question. MRI without contrast is an option when immediately available and appropriate. Use MRV or CTV when CVST is suspected. Reserve gadolinium in pregnancy for situations where it meaningfully changes management and the expected benefit justifies exposure.
    - **Potentially disabling ischemic stroke:** discuss IV thrombolysis and thrombectomy promptly with stroke and obstetrics. Consider bleeding risk, delivery timing and recent procedures. Pregnancy alone should not delay activation or exclude otherwise appropriate reperfusion.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — Treat eclampsia or an ongoing seizure"
    - Protect the airway and follow the obstetric emergency order set for suspected eclampsia. **Magnesium sulfate IV, 4–6 g over 20–30 minutes then 1–2 g/hour**, is a common ACOG regimen. Monitor respiration, reflexes and urine output. Renal dysfunction requires modified dosing and monitoring. Myasthenia gravis is a contraindication and needs an immediate alternative plan with obstetrics, anesthesia and neurology.
    - For persistent or recurrent convulsions, call for airway support and follow obstetric and [status epilepticus](../seizure/index.md) protocols with specialist input. Evaluate hemorrhage, CVST, metabolic causes and other etiologies. In a patient with established epilepsy, check adherence, vomiting and whether levels or dose requirements have changed.

!!! orderset "Step 3 — Make the outpatient plan before the next trimester"
    - **Established epilepsy:** avoid abrupt discontinuation or switching of an effective antiseizure medication during pregnancy. For preconception selection, consider lamotrigine, levetiracetam or oxcarbazepine when appropriate for the syndrome. Avoid valproate when clinically feasible, with specialist counseling about seizure control and fetal risks.
    - **Folic acid:** prescribe **at least 0.4 mg PO daily** before conception and during pregnancy for patients taking an antiseizure medication. Coordinate the dose with obstetrics. For medicines with changing levels, use a preconception level when available, monitor during pregnancy and plan postpartum dose reassessment to avoid toxicity.
    - **Typical primary headache after secondary causes have been addressed:** discuss acetaminophen as a first option. Review gestational age, liver risk and combination products. Persistent migraine needs an individualized obstetric medication plan. Check trimester-specific NSAID limits and lactation separately. Avoid routine use of drugs with insufficient pregnancy safety data.

## Disposition

Admit or transfer suspected eclampsia, stroke, CVST, PRES, ongoing seizure, or progressive unexplained deficits. For outpatient care, leave one written plan for emergency return, medication and level monitoring, obstetric/neurology follow-up, and postpartum review. Include sleep and feeding logistics when counseling a patient with epilepsy.

!!! tip "TRICARE — medication and postpartum follow-up"
    Check product, formulation and benefit in the [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst) and [authorization guide](../military/tricare.md). When a payer-preferred option is clinically unsuitable, document the specific maternal or fetal safety concern.

!!! military "Military medicine"
    Document symptoms, recurrence risk, onset and duty context for EPTS/LOD. Assess duty restrictions through obstetrics. [Army Directive 2025-02](https://h2f.army.mil/Portals/141/pdfs/ARN43152-ARMY_DIR_2025-02-000-WEB-1.pdf) provides a 365-day operational deferment after birth for Soldiers who give birth, including deployment. Assess ongoing neurologic restrictions through the [profile, retention and MEB/IDES guide](../military/deployability.md) and [destination and medication screen](../military/deployment-medications.md).

## Discover

The 2026 AHA maternal stroke statement emphasizes prompt emergency imaging and treatment. The AAN/AES/SMFM medication guideline weighs seizure control alongside fetal risk. Its preconception preferred options do not justify a reflex switch after pregnancy has begun.
