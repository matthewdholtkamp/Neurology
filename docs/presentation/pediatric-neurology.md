# Pediatric neurology essentials

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: November 2026</span>

**One-liner:** Start with age, weight in kilograms, developmental baseline and return to baseline. Get the caregiver’s timeline and video if one is available.

!!! danger "Get pediatric emergency help early"
    - Ongoing convulsion at **five minutes**, recurrent convulsions without recovery, airway compromise, or persistent altered mental status: use the local pediatric status/PALS pathway. Check glucose and obtain an accurate weight or emergency estimate.
    - Fever with meningismus, toxic appearance, persistent encephalopathy, focal findings or a concerning infant presentation: assess for CNS infection urgently and treat per the pediatric protocol.
    - Repeated brief flexion or extension clusters in an infant, especially on waking or with loss of skills: suspect epileptic spasms. Contact pediatric neurology urgently, within 24 hours, for a sleep EEG and rapid treatment planning.
    - Sudden focal deficit, new inability to walk, suspected ingestion, rapidly progressive headache or papilledema: urgent pediatric assessment and targeted imaging or transfer.

## What to do in order

!!! orderset "Step 1 — Seizure with fever"
    1. Treat the ongoing seizure and assess the fever source. A **simple febrile seizure** in a neurologically healthy child **6–60 months** is generalized, lasts **under 15 minutes**, occurs once in **24 hours**, and is followed by recovery to baseline. Focal, prolonged or recurrent features require a different evaluation.
    2. In a well-appearing child who fits the simple pattern, focus testing on the fever source. Routine EEG, neuroimaging and blood tests solely for the seizure are generally unnecessary.
    3. Consider LP when the history or examination suggests CNS infection. The AAP guideline also permits consideration in a **6–12 month** old with incomplete or unknown Hib/pneumococcal vaccination, or after antibiotics that could mask meningitis. Complex features alone do not determine the LP or imaging decision.
    4. For a child ready for discharge, teach seizure first aid and when to call emergency services. Antipyretics help comfort. They should not be presented as reliable seizure prevention.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — First unprovoked seizure or suspected infantile epilepsy"
    - Ask whether there was a trigger, focal onset, injury, medication/ingestion, family history, developmental regression or a prolonged postictal state. Examine for focal deficits and use targeted testing. Consider ECG for a possible blackout or arrhythmic event.
    - Discuss urgent imaging and admission with pediatric neurology when there is persistent focal deficit, failure to return to baseline, trauma, signs of raised ICP or concern for a structural/infectious cause. Very young age, prolonged or recurrent events and an atypical exam lower the threshold for specialist involvement.
    - When the child returns to baseline and no urgent finding remains, arrange expedited pediatric neurology follow-up and EEG planning. MRI and chronic antiseizure treatment depend on the syndrome and recurrence risk. For suspected spasms, use the urgent infantile epilepsy pathway. Do not wait for a routine visit.
    - Rescue and loading drugs require the pediatric dose, maximum, indication and product concentration. Use a pediatric order set and read back the dose in both mg and mL when dispensing a liquid.

!!! orderset "Step 3 — Headache or new ataxia"
    - **Headache:** check BP, developmentally appropriate neurologic exam, gait, vision and fundus if feasible. Escalate sudden severe onset, progressive symptoms with vomiting, focal findings, papilledema, infection or altered mental status. A typical primary headache with normal exam and no red flags usually does not need imaging.
    - **Typical migraine:** treat early with hydration and an age-appropriate acute plan. **Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg PO once, maximum 400 mg for this regimen**, is a practical initial option for an eligible child. AAN/AHS supports the weight-based dose. Check age, prior doses, liquid concentration and contraindications, including significant dehydration or renal risk. If a triptan is needed, select by pediatric age, product label and contraindications with the treating clinician.
    - **Acute ataxia:** distinguish cerebellar incoordination from weakness, pain, vertigo and sensory loss. Ask about recent infection, medication/ingestion, trauma and headache. Persistent altered alertness, focal deficits, severe headache or progressive inability to walk needs urgent evaluation. MRI generally best characterizes suspected posterior fossa disease when feasible. Use CT or a stroke pathway when speed or suspected hemorrhage makes it appropriate.

## Disposition

Discharge only when baseline and safe function are established, the caregiver understands the plan, and follow-up is feasible. Give explicit instructions for prolonged or repeated seizure, poor recovery, new weakness, progressive headache or impaired walking.

!!! tip "TRICARE — pediatric formulations and referral"
    Check age, indication, exact device or liquid concentration, dose and requested quantity in the [TRICARE Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst). Route specialty care through the [applicable benefit](../military/tricare.md).

!!! military "Military families"
    For dependents, document developmental baseline, follow-up and school or childcare rescue plans. For a serving adolescent, use the applicable [profile, EPTS/LOD, retention and MEB/IDES guidance](../military/deployability.md) and [deployment screen](../military/deployment-medications.md) separately.

## Discover

The low-testing pathway for simple febrile seizures depends on matching the phenotype and examining for CNS infection. A first unprovoked seizure needs a different follow-up plan. For an infant with possible spasms, a caregiver’s phone video can make a brief cluster visible to the team that needs to see it.
