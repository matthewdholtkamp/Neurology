# Radiculopathy & Plexopathy

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: November 2026</span>

**One-liner:** arm or leg pain, weakness, and sensory loss that follow a nerve root (radiculopathy) or a plexus element (plexopathy) — for the on-call neuro or PCP seeing a patient with radiating pain and a focal deficit, the job is to exclude the dangerous cause, localize to root vs plexus vs nerve, and treat in a time-limited, stepped way that gets most patients better without surgery.

!!! danger "🚩 Red flags — image now, refer urgently"
    | Pattern | Worry | Do now |
    |---|---|---|
    | Urinary retention / overflow incontinence, saddle anesthesia, fecal incontinence or reduced anal tone, bilateral or multiroot leg deficits | Cauda equina / conus syndrome | **Emergent MRI + immediate spine/neurosurgical evaluation** — do not trial conservative care |
    | Rapidly progressive or severe motor weakness | Major nerve-root or cord compression | **Urgent MRI + spine referral** — no conservative trial first |
    | Hyperreflexia, clonus, Babinski/Hoffmann, gait dysfunction, hand clumsiness, long-tract signs | Cervical myelopathy / cord compression | **Urgent cervical MRI** |
    | Fever, IV drug use, indwelling catheter (LR+ 15.7), immunosuppression, recent infection, severe focal spinal pain | Discitis / osteomyelitis / epidural abscess | **Urgent MRI with contrast** + labs (ESR/CRP, CBC, blood cultures) |
    | History of cancer (LR+ 27.9 when combined with clinical suspicion), unexplained weight loss, age >50, failure to improve within ~1 month | Malignancy / metastasis | **MRI with contrast** + cancer workup |
    | Trauma, osteoporosis, long-term steroid use | Fracture | **Image the level** (CT for bony detail) |

## Diagnosis / classification

**Radiculopathy** is pain, dysesthesia, weakness, sensory loss, and reflex loss in a **dermatomal/myotomal pattern from a single root** — most often a disc herniation (lumbar: usually **L4–5 or L5–S1**) or cervical spondylosis/foraminal stenosis. **Plexopathy** is the same kind of deficit spread across **more than one peripheral nerve and more than one root level, but coherent within one plexus** (brachial or lumbosacral). The two are distinguished clinically by the pattern, and electrodiagnostically by the SNAP/paraspinal rule below — not by imaging alone.

### The exam that localizes

| Test / finding | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Spurling's test** (cervical) | Highly specific for cervical radiculopathy (**specificity 0.84–1.00**) — a positive test supports the diagnosis; a negative test does not exclude it |
| **Upper-limb neurodynamic test cluster** (cervical) | A **negative** cluster helps rule radiculopathy out (**sensitivity 0.97**) |
| **Straight-leg raise / crossed SLR** (lumbar) | Crossed SLR is specific (**0.90**); a positive ipsilateral SLR supports a lumbar root lesion |
| **Reduced reflexes** (especially triceps) and **arm pain worse than neck pain** (cervical) | Raise the likelihood of cervical radiculopathy |
| **Myelopathy screen** (cervical) | Hyperreflexia, gait change, loss of hand dexterity — if present, this is no longer isolated radiculopathy; image the cord urgently |
| **Mimics to exclude** | Shoulder disease, carpal tunnel, ulnar entrapment, and "double crush" (root + entrapment together) |

**Think in trunks and cords, not just dermatomes, for plexopathy:**

- **Upper trunk C5–6:** deltoid, biceps, supraspinatus/infraspinatus, brachioradialis weakness; reduced biceps/brachioradialis reflex; lateral arm/forearm sensory loss.
- **Middle trunk C7:** triceps, wrist and finger extensors; triceps reflex.
- **Lower trunk C8–T1:** intrinsic hand muscles, finger flexors; medial forearm/hand sensory loss.
- **Posterior cord:** deltoid + radial-innervated muscles. **Medial/lateral cords:** combinations of median/ulnar/musculocutaneous deficits.
- **Upper lumbar plexus L1–4:** hip flexion/adduction and knee extension weakness; reduced patellar reflex; anterior/medial thigh and medial leg sensory loss.
- **Sacral plexus L4–S4:** hip abduction/extension, hamstrings, ankle and toe muscles; Achilles reflex abnormalities; posterior thigh, leg and foot sensory loss.
- A plexopathy becomes particularly likely when the pattern **cannot be explained by one root or one terminal nerve** — e.g., simultaneous suprascapular and musculocutaneous abnormalities.

## Rapid approach

1. **Exclude the dangerous thing first** (red-flag screen above). Any red flag → urgent MRI and spine referral; no conservative trial.
2. **Localize: root vs plexus vs nerve.** Dermatomal/myotomal single-root pattern → radiculopathy. Multi-nerve, multi-root, plexus-coherent pattern → plexopathy. Discrete, stepwise deficits in individual named nerves, often in more than one limb → mononeuropathy multiplex (see the differential table).
3. **Route:** uncomplicated radiculopathy → conservative care first (Step 1). Suspected plexopathy → EMG/NCS + dedicated plexus MRI and an etiologic workup (Step 1 for plexopathy).
4. **Reassess on a clock:** radiculopathy not improving at **4–6 weeks** → MRI and consider ESI or surgical referral; progressive deficit at any point → escalate immediately.

## The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Red-flag screen + neuro exam** (motor, sensory, reflexes, gait, myelopathy signs, bladder/bowel, saddle sensation). Document the exact myotome/dermatome.
2. **If no red flags and the exam is stable: start conservative care now** — activity + NSAID + early PT (Step 1). Do not image routinely.
3. **If the pattern is plexus-coherent (not one root):** order EMG/NCS and dedicated plexus MRI, and start the etiologic workup — do not anchor on a spine MRI.
4. **Reassess at 2–6 weeks.** Improving → continue and taper. Not improving or worsening → MRI (if not already done), ESI for severe concordant radicular pain, spine referral.
5. **Surgery** only for the indications below — progressive deficit, emergent compression, or persistent disabling radicular pain with a concordant lesion after an adequate nonoperative trial (~6–12 weeks).
6. **Disposition + follow-up:** almost always outpatient for uncomplicated radiculopathy; admit for red flags, myelopathy, or cauda equina.

## Workup

**Uncomplicated radiculopathy needs no routine imaging or labs.** The ACR recommends **no routine imaging for acute cervical radiculopathy without trauma or red flags** — most cases resolve with conservative care, herniated discs shrink over time, and spondylotic changes are common in people without symptoms. Interpret any MRI only alongside the clinical findings, never on its own — false positives and false negatives are frequent.

### When to get MRI

| Situation | Imaging |
|---|---|
| **Urgent / emergent** — cauda equina, myelopathy/cord compression, suspected infection or cancer, severe or progressive deficit (including multi-level deficits) | **MRI now** (with contrast if infection or malignancy is suspected — MRI without and with IV contrast is the appropriate first study for suspected infection) |
| **Elective** — persistent symptoms after **4–6 weeks** of failed conservative care, if the patient is a candidate for intervention or the diagnosis remains uncertain | **MRI without contrast** (cervical: "may be appropriate" even for acute uncomplicated radiculopathy, "usually appropriate" for chronic radiculopathy per the 2024 ACR cervical criteria; lumbar: after ~6 weeks of optimal medical management in surgical/intervention candidates). ACOEM: MRI at **3–4 weeks** is reasonable if an epidural is planned |
| **Not recommended** | Routine early MRI (does not improve outcomes, increases downstream procedures); repeat MRI without clinical change; radiographs alone (rarely change acute management without red flags) |
| **MRI contraindicated or obscured by hardware** | **CT myelography** is the alternative; CT also shows bony anatomy, foraminal osteophytes, and ossification for surgical planning |

### Labs

Order **ESR/CRP and CBC only when infection or cancer is suspected**. For suspected plexopathy, the lab panel is broader — see the plexopathy workup below.

### Electrodiagnostics (EMG/NCS)

**Not needed when the diagnosis is clear.** Use EMG/NCS when clinical and imaging levels don't match, when surgery is being considered, or to exclude peripheral neuropathy/entrapment. The AANEM recommends **needle EMG plus at least one motor and one sensory nerve conduction study in the affected limb**. Two timing points that matter:

- **EMG may be falsely unrevealing very early in an axonal radiculopathy** — interpret with symptom duration in mind.
- For plexopathy, study at about **3–4 weeks after onset** (fibrillations take ~3 weeks to appear) and repeat at about **3–6 months** to track reinnervation.

!!! orderset "Workup — suspected plexopathy"
    - **EMG/NCS (extensive, compared with the other side):** SNAP amplitude is the most useful marker of axon-loss plexopathy and maps the element involved; motor NCS are insensitive for screening — side-to-side CMAP amplitude estimates the percentage of motor axon loss, which matters for prognosis. Needle EMG is the most sensitive test for motor axon loss and shows the proximal extent; MUAPs show continuity and early reinnervation.
    - **Dedicated plexus MRI / MR neurography with STIR and contrast** — the main imaging study (ACR: preferred initial imaging for nontraumatic plexopathy once localization is made). Brachial plexus MRI: **sensitivity 81%, specificity 91%, accuracy 88%** vs surgery/follow-up; added information beyond exam and EDx in **45%** of patients. Lumbosacral plexus MRI changed management in **71%** of patients in one series; abnormal nerve signal correlates with active denervation on EMG. MR neurography evaluates nerve caliber/enlargement, T2/STIR hyperintensity, loss of normal fascicular architecture, enhancement, and muscle denervation edema/atrophy.
    - **High-resolution ultrasound** complements MRI for superficial segments, dynamic and traumatic lesions, and fascicular detail (operator-dependent). For trauma, CT myelography or MRI can show root avulsion.
    - **Labs, tailored to the case:** **HbA1c, CBC, ESR/CRP**; **ANA, ANCA, SSA/SSB, RF/CCP, cryoglobulins, hepatitis B/C** (vasculitis); **SPEP/immunofixation + free light chains** (amyloid); **HIV, syphilis, Lyme**; **CSF (cells, protein, cytology/flow)** if infiltrative or inflammatory disease is suspected. If malignancy is suspected: **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET**.
    - **Genetics when the pattern fits:** **PMP22 deletion** for painless recurrent palsies (HNPP); **SEPT9** for familial recurrent painful episodes (hereditary neuralgic amyotrophy).
    - **Targeted fascicular nerve biopsy** only for unexplained progressive plexopathy — e.g., to find perineurial tumor spread, lymphoma, or vasculitis.
    - → Localization confirmed → treat the cause (see Management, Step 3 for plexopathy).

## Management

### Radiculopathy — conservative care first

The natural history is favorable: **75–90% of patients with chronic radiculopathy improve with nonoperative care**, and conservative care is appropriate for symptoms under about **6–8 weeks** with mild-to-moderate deficits and no progressive neurologic loss. Cervical evidence is much thinner than lumbar — most cervical recommendations are extrapolated from lumbar data. The approach is multimodal and time-limited: education + staying active, PT/exercise, and short courses of NSAIDs. Medications are short-term symptom control, not disease-modifying treatment.

!!! orderset "Step 1 — first-line: stay active + NSAID + early PT (start here for most)"
    - **Keep the patient active** and educate about the generally favorable natural history. Bed rest does not help.
    - **NSAID — ibuprofen 400–600 mg PO q6–8h** (max 2,400 mg/day Rx; 1,200 mg/day OTC) **or naproxen 500 mg PO BID** (max 1,000–1,250 mg/day), for **about 1–2 weeks, then reassess**. Lowest effective dose, shortest time. Screen for GI, renal, and cardiovascular risk; add a PPI if GI risk is high. **Avoid in CKD stage 4–5, with anticoagulants, or in uncontrolled heart failure.**
    - **Physical therapy / exercise — refer early:** directional-preference exercises and activity in the acute phase; add strengthening and neurodynamic mobilization in the subacute phase. In the Fritz trial, early PT for sciatica of <90 days improved disability and pain more than usual care. Cervical PT components: cervical/scapular stabilization and strengthening, postural and ergonomic training, neural mobilization when tolerated, selective manual therapy, traction in appropriately selected patients, progressive home exercise.
    - → No reliable improvement across **2–6 weeks**, or pain remains disabling → **Step 2**.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — persistent disabling radicular pain: selective steroid or ESI"
    - **Oral corticosteroid — only selectively, and know the split:** **prednisone 60 mg × 5 days, then 40 mg × 5 days, then 20 mg × 5 days** (Goldberg trial). Pain improves only ~**0.56/10 at <2 weeks**, with a small long-term function gain (7.4/100) and **no change in surgery rates**. **VA/DoD 2022 recommends against systemic steroids; NICE recommends against oral corticosteroids for sciatica.** Reserve for clear radicular findings (dermatomal pain/deficits, positive SLR) and counsel that the benefit is small and short-term and steroids do not reduce the need for surgery. Adverse effects: insomnia, nervousness, increased appetite; watch glucose in diabetes. Best dose is unknown — avoiding higher doses may reduce harm. **Do not use a Medrol Dosepak or prednisone taper routinely for lumbar radiculopathy.**
    - **Cervical exception:** the limited cervical RCT evidence is more favorable — **prednisolone 50 mg/day × 5 days, tapered over the subsequent 5 days** produced clinically important improvement in **75.8% vs 30% with placebo** (better pain and Neck Disability Index scores) — but this was a **single small trial of 59 patients**, so a short course is a selective option for severe cervical radiculopathy, not a default.
    - **Epidural steroid injection (ESI) — image-guided transforaminal or interlaminar,** usually in the subacute phase after **~4–6 weeks** of failed conservative care, or to bridge severe pain. Probably reduces short-term (~2–6 week) pain and disability (AAN 2025: probably effective short-term; NNT ~7 for pain, ~10 for disability — see footer for a conflicting NNT). A 2026 systematic review found even lower doses — **dexamethasone 4 mg, methylprednisolone 20 mg, triamcinolone 10 mg** — sometimes produced outcomes statistically indistinguishable from higher doses. **Do NOT prescribe a predetermined "series of three"** — repeat only on meaningful response to the first procedure. **Cervical transforaminal injections with particulate steroid carry rare but catastrophic neurovascular risk — nonparticulate dexamethasone is preferred.**
    - → Still disabling after an adequate trial with a concordant lesion → **Step 3 (surgical referral)**.

!!! orderset "Step 3 — surgical referral"
    **Do not require a completed conservative trial when there is:** progressive or substantial motor weakness; cervical myelopathy; cauda equina/conus syndrome; spinal instability; epidural abscess; tumor causing neurologic compression; or another compressive lesion threatening permanent neurologic injury.

    **Elective referral** (neurologically stable degenerative radiculopathy) becomes reasonable with **persistent disabling radicular pain or neurologic dysfunction despite an adequate nonoperative trial (~6–12 weeks) and imaging demonstrating a concordant compressive lesion**. Cervical: spine referral/interventional evaluation when symptoms fail to improve after roughly **4–8 weeks**; NASS-derived recommendations describe a **6–12 week** nonoperative period as reasonable in stable patients. Lumbar: ACR identifies patients with persistent or progressive symptoms after **~6 weeks** of optimal medical management who are candidates for surgery/intervention.

    **Results are better when leg or arm pain predominates over axial pain. Fusion is not indicated for axial low back pain alone.** Cervical caveat: most patients improve whatever the treatment — in RCTs of surgery vs standardized nonsurgical care, surgery gave only a small benefit for disc herniation and no significant benefit for spondylosis at 12 months.

!!! warning "Avoid"
    - **Routine gabapentinoids for lumbar sciatica** — pregabalin (PRECISE trial, titrated up to 300 mg/day) was **no better than placebo** for acute or chronic sciatica, with more adverse effects and misuse potential. NICE recommends against them. Reconsider or avoid; use only for another compelling neuropathic indication.
    - **Opioids** for radicular pain — poor efficacy, dependence risk, and they blunt the response to specific treatment.
    - **Bed rest** — continued activity beats it.
    - **A predetermined "series of three" ESIs** — repeat only on meaningful response.
    - **Particulate steroid for cervical transforaminal ESI** — rare but catastrophic neurovascular risk; use nonparticulate dexamethasone.
    - **Duloxetine for radiculopathy** — evidence is indeterminate.

!!! tip "TRICARE — radiculopathy prescribing"
    **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) and oral steroids are standard formulary with no PA** — the frictionless first step. **Gabapentin and pregabalin are formulary** but are not the move here on the evidence (see Avoid). **Epidural steroid injection** is a procedure, not a pharmacy item — it goes through the MTF/specialty referral route, not the formulary. *Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.* (TRICARE statuses were not re-verified against the live formulary in this run — see footer.)

### Conservative-treatment evidence at a glance

| Treatment | Typical adult regimen | Evidence (lumbar) | Evidence (cervical) | Practical role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NSAID** | Ibuprofen **400–600 mg PO q6–8h** or naproxen **500 mg PO BID**, ~1–2 weeks | Small short-term benefit for pain/disability; low-quality evidence of slight global improvement in sciatica; all NSAIDs perform about the same; systematic reviews show little/no clear improvement in pain or disability vs placebo for true radicular leg pain | Direct RCT evidence limited; commonly used for short-term nociceptive/inflammatory pain | Reasonable first-line analgesic if no GI/renal/CV contraindication — symptomatic, not disease-modifying |
| **Gabapentin / pregabalin** | If used for another compelling neuropathic indication: gabapentin often starts **300 mg daily → BID → TID**; pregabalin **75 mg BID** | PRECISE (NEJM 2017): no better than placebo for acute or chronic sciatica; more adverse effects | Controlled evidence lacking; reviews still mention them for persistent radicular pain | **Do not start routinely**, especially for lumbar sciatica |
| **Oral corticosteroid** | Prednisone **60 mg × 5 d → 40 mg × 5 d → 20 mg × 5 d** (Goldberg) | Pain ~0.56/10 better at <2 weeks; function +7.4/100; no change in surgery rates; VA/DoD and NICE recommend against | Prednisolone **50 mg/day × 5 d, taper over 5 d**: 75.8% vs 30% improved (single trial, n=59) | Selective only, for clear radicular findings; counsel benefit is small and short-term |
| **Epidural steroid injection** | Image-guided transforaminal or interlaminar, subacute phase | Probably reduces short-term (2–6 week) pain/disability; AAN 2025: probably effective short-term | Cervical: more caution; nonparticulate dexamethasone preferred | Bridge for severe disabling radicular pain — restores sleep, mobility, work, rehab participation; not definitive treatment of the compressive lesion |
| **PT / exercise** | Early referral; directional-preference exercise acutely; strengthening + neurodynamic mobilization subacutely | Early PT beat usual care for disability (Fritz trial, sciatica <90 days) | Stage-based expert consensus; technique superiority uncertain | **Centerpiece of conservative care**; universally recommended first-line nonpharmacologic care |

### Surgery — comparative outcomes

**Cervical: ACDF vs posterior cervical foraminotomy (PCF)** — for single-level cervical radiculopathy, PCF is **noninferior** to ACDF for arm-pain relief and success rate; the two differ mainly in complication profile and long-term revision risk.

| Outcome | ACDF | PCF (open or minimally invasive) |
|---|---|---|
| Success (Odom), FACET RCT | 76% at 1 year; 74% at 2 years | 88% at 1 year; 81% at 2 years (noninferior) |
| Arm pain / NDI | Comparable | Comparable |
| Neck pain | Somewhat worse; new cervicalgia 34% | Less neck pain (statistically better, small difference); 23% |
| Serious surgery-related adverse events, 2 years (FACET) | 9% (7 reoperations) | 8% (9 reoperations) |
| Reoperation at 2 years | ~6% | ~8% |
| Approach-specific complications | Dysphagia, dysphonia/RLN injury, esophageal/vascular injury, pseudarthrosis, implant failure, adjacent-segment disease | Early posterior neck pain, wound infection (2.2% vs 0.2%), dehiscence, pulmonary embolism, recurrent foraminal herniation |
| 5-year revision (national database, n≈32,000) | 14.8% | 19.0% |
| Length of stay / cost | Longer, higher | ~1 day shorter; ~$9,100 less |
| Motion preservation | No (fusion) | Yes |
| Best anatomy | Central/paracentral disc, significant anterior osteophyte/disc complex, disc-space collapse; useful when stabilization is desirable | Unilateral lateral/foraminal disc or osteophyte, preserved alignment and stability; best fit is unilateral foraminal soft disc or foraminal stenosis at 1–2 levels with lordosis preserved, in patients who want to keep motion |

**Lumbar: microdiscectomy vs laminectomy** — these treat **different problems** (disc herniation vs stenosis), so the procedure is chosen by pathology, not by head-to-head outcome. A small laminotomy/hemilaminectomy is often part of the exposure for a microdiscectomy, but a full decompressive laminectomy is generally unnecessary for an isolated posterolateral disc herniation.

| | Microdiscectomy (herniated disc with radiculopathy) | Laminectomy (central/lateral-recess stenosis, neurogenic claudication) |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect** | Leg-pain relief good to excellent in most; good/excellent result in **~70–90%** of appropriately selected patients. In chronic sciatica lasting 4–12 months, beat standardized nonoperative care at 1 year. SPORT (disc herniation, radiculopathy ≥6 weeks): faster and greater improvement in leg pain, function, and disability than continued nonoperative treatment in as-treated analyses; advantage persisted through 8 years (adjusted 8-year effects: SF-36 bodily pain +10.9, physical function +10.6, ODI −11.2). Even so, **30–70% report some ongoing pain** | Decompression improves pain over conservative care at **3–6 months**; benefit lasts but fades by **2–4 years**. Adding fusion gives no benefit over decompression alone for stenosis without instability |
| **Complications** | Overall ~10%: recurrent herniation ~7% (up to ~10% in other series; 4–6% in pooled series), reoperation ~4–8%, durotomy ~2–4%, deep infection <1%, wrong-level surgery 0.7%, nerve-root injury <1%. Open, micro, and endoscopic techniques give similar 2-year results; endoscopic shortens hospital stay but may carry more durotomy and reherniation | SPORT lumbar stenosis: dural tear ~9%, wound infection 2%, reoperation **13% at 4 years, 18% at 8 years** (recurrent stenosis or progressive spondylolisthesis ~half of reoperations). National registry: overall complication rate **16.8%** (durotomy, urinary retention, wound infection common); risk rises with age, higher ASA class, multilevel surgery, longer operative time. Wider exposure → more blood loss, durotomy (higher in revision surgery and elderly), iatrogenic instability or late spondylolisthesis if facets are taken |

**Choose by pathology, not by preference:**

| Pathology | Preferred operation |
|---|---|
| Unilateral cervical foraminal soft disc/osteophyte, preserved alignment/stability | PCF or ACDF — outcomes essentially equivalent |
| Cervical central/anterior compression, disc collapse, instability, kyphotic segment | Usually ACDF |
| Single-level lumbar posterolateral disc herniation causing sciatica | Microdiscectomy |
| Lumbar central/lateral-recess stenosis with claudication ± multiroot radiculopathy | Laminotomy/laminectomy decompression |
| Disc herniation + stenosis | Limited decompression plus discectomy, tailored to anatomy |
| Instability/spondylolisthesis requiring stabilization | Decompression ± fusion, depending on documented instability rather than radiculopathy alone |

### Plexopathy — management by cause

Treat the cause; supportive care is the mainstay. **Neuropathic pain drugs (gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine, TCAs)** — gabapentin often starts **300 mg daily → BID → TID**, pregabalin **75 mg BID** — plus **PT/OT, bracing**, and glycemic control in diabetic LRPN.

| Cause | Management |
|---|---|
| **Traumatic avulsion / rupture** | **Early surgical referral, ideally within ~3–6 months**, or when there is no clinical or EMG reinnervation. Options: nerve grafts, nerve transfers |
| **Tumor / neoplastic** (Pancoast lung, breast, lymphoma — usually painful, lower plexus) | Tumor-directed therapy (systemic therapy, radiotherapy and/or surgery) while treating neuropathic and cancer-related pain |
| **Radiation plexopathy** (usually painless, upper plexus, with myokymia) | **No established disease-reversing therapy.** Rehabilitation, orthoses/assistive devices, neuropathic-pain treatment; first exclude recurrent malignancy. Myokymic discharges are particularly suggestive of radiation injury and much more frequent in radiation-induced than neoplastic plexopathy, but neither their presence nor absence is absolutely diagnostic |
| **Neuralgic amyotrophy / Parsonage-Turner** (abrupt severe shoulder/upper-arm pain → patchy weakness days later; often surprisingly multifocal) | Pain control and targeted PT/OT are central. Early corticosteroids **may shorten the acute painful phase** in some patients, but high-quality randomized evidence is limited; chronic rehab emphasizes motor-control retraining and avoidance of excessive loading |
| **Diabetic / nondiabetic LRPN** (acute/subacute severe hip or thigh pain → asymmetric proximal weakness/atrophy → spread to other territories, sometimes bilateral, often with weight loss) | Optimize diabetes care, analgesia, gait/fall support, orthotics when needed, PT/OT. Microvasculitic inflammation is implicated; evidence for immunotherapy is limited. EDX is an axonal, multifocal radiculoplexus neuropathy — paraspinal denervation does **not** argue against the diagnosis. MRI is often used primarily to exclude malignancy, structural disease, infection, and other infiltrative causes |
| **Vasculitis / sarcoid / infection / abscess / hematoma** | Immunosuppression for vasculitis or sarcoid; abscess drainage; hematoma evacuation or stopping anticoagulation |
| **Compressive** (thoracic outlet, mass, retroperitoneal hematoma, psoas/iliacus lesion) | Relieve the compressive structure |

**Prognosis:** recovery is often incomplete — counsel realistically. In a 2026 tertiary series of 124 lumbosacral plexopathy patients, the commonest causes were **diabetic LRPN (n=43)**, perioperative injury (32), idiopathic/nondiabetic LRPN (18), and radiation (15), then autoimmune, neoplastic, traumatic, and infectious causes.

## Plexopathy vs radiculopathy vs mononeuropathy multiplex

| Feature | Radiculopathy | Plexopathy | Mononeuropathy multiplex |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clinical pattern** | Dermatomal pain/sensory loss, myotomal weakness, usually one or a few adjacent roots | Weakness and sensory loss across several nerves in one limb, proximal + distal, not fitting one root or one nerve | Discrete, stepwise deficits in individual named nerves, often in more than one limb, distal-predominant |
| **Neck/back pain** | Often prominent | Variable | — |
| **Lesion site vs DRG** | Preganglionic | Postganglionic | Postganglionic |
| **SNAPs** | Normal, even in a numb dermatome | Reduced/absent in the involved trunk/cord territory — which gives localizing value | Reduced in affected nerves only, often patchy across limbs |
| **Paraspinal EMG** | Denervation present | Normal (spared posterior primary rami) | Normal |
| **Limb EMG** | Muscles sharing a root across different nerves | Muscles sharing a plexus element (trunk/cord/division) across nerves and roots | Muscles of each affected nerve, with sparing of other nerves from the same root |
| **Plexus MRI** | Usually normal | May show nerve enlargement, T2 signal, enhancement, mass | — |
| **Spine MRI** | May show concordant foraminal/root disease | May be incidental | — |
| **Typical causes** | Disc, spondylosis, zoster, diabetic, carcinomatous | See causes table above | Vasculitis, diabetes, HNPP, leprosy, Lyme, HIV, sarcoid, lymphoma |

**Pitfalls:** radiculoplexus neuropathy (diabetic or nondiabetic LRPN) involves roots, plexus, and nerves together — paraspinal denervation does **not** exclude a plexus process. Bilateral or asymmetric multifocal disease means vasculitic mononeuritis multiplex, LRPN, and polyradiculopathy all need to be considered. The key conceptual distinction is **anatomic coherence**: plexopathy maps to a plexus; mononeuropathy multiplex maps to multiple separate nerves.

**A useful EDX definition:** abnormalities involving **at least two peripheral nerves derived from more than one root distribution**.

## Disposition

Uncomplicated radiculopathy is an **outpatient diagnosis** — treat, start PT, arrange follow-up, and give a written plan with the reassessment clock (2–6 weeks) and return precautions (new weakness, saddle numbness, bladder/bowel change, fever). Admit for red flags, myelopathy, cauda equina, or infection. Suspected plexopathy is outpatient unless the cause needs admission (tumor, abscess, hematoma) — but the workup (EMG/NCS + plexus MRI + etiologic labs) should not wait, and traumatic avulsion needs surgical referral within ~3–6 months.

!!! military "Military medicine — radiculopathy & plexopathy"
    *Framework — verify against current **AR 40-501**, **DoDI 6130.03**, **DoDI 1332.18**, service
    deployment/aeromedical policy (and any relevant JTS CPG) before counseling.*

    - **Deployability:** uncomplicated radiculopathy that is controlled on an NSAID and PT is usually deployable; progressive deficit, myelopathy, cauda equina, or a regimen requiring ESI, frequent imaging, or surgical follow-up is not. Plexopathy is not deployable while the cause is unestablished or while weakness is evolving. Gabapentinoids, sedating neuropathic agents, and opioids all matter downrange.
    - **Profile (DA Form 3349):** temporary profile during the acute phase, PT, or a steroid/ESI course; **permanent profile** for persistent weakness, sensory loss, or functional limits documented after treatment.
    - **EPTS / LOD:** document **onset and pre-service history**; disc disease and radiculopathy often predate service, and trauma in service (including training injuries and MVCs) affects line-of-duty determinations. Record the mechanism and the first presentation.
    - **Retention (AR 40-501 / DoDI 6130.03):** judged on persistent functional deficit and duty impact — radiculopathy with residual weakness or sensory loss that limits duty, and plexopathy with incomplete recovery, both engage the neurologic retention standards. **The specific paragraph was not pinned in this run — confirm the applicable AR 40-501 / DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 paragraph before counseling or documenting.**
    - **MEB / IDES:** refer when deficits persist after treatment and rehabilitation, or when the condition limits duty despite a profile; document the exam, imaging, EMG localization, and the functional impact objectively — that documentation carries a board.
    - **Aeromedical / special duty:** radiculopathy with persistent deficit, myelopathy, and plexopathy are disqualifying for flying duty pending evaluation; confirm current aeromedical policy and waiver requirements before counseling.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

*Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.*

- **The diagnosis is clinical; imaging is timed, not routine.** Without red flags, a focused exam plus a trial of conservative care is the right first move — routine early MRI does not improve outcomes and increases downstream procedures. MRI earns its place at ~4–6 weeks if symptoms persist and the result will change management, or immediately for cauda equina, myelopathy, infection, or progressive deficit.
- **The exam still carries numbers.** Spurling's is highly specific (0.84–1.00), a negative upper-limb neurodynamic cluster helps rule cervical radiculopathy out (sensitivity 0.97), and a crossed straight-leg raise is specific (0.90) — use them before ordering anything.
- **Conservative care is a time-limited trial, not a holding pattern.** Activity + NSAID + early PT is the centerpiece; the two drugs clinicians reach for most — gabapentinoids and oral steroids — have the weakest support for lumbar sciatica (PRECISE: pregabalin no better than placebo; VA/DoD and NICE both recommend against systemic steroids). ESI is a bridge for severe disabling pain, not a cure for the compressive lesion, and never a predetermined "series of three."
- **Surgery is a pathology choice.** PCF is noninferior to ACDF for single-level cervical radiculopathy (FACET: 2-year success 81% vs 74%), trading dysphagia and fusion for more wound infection and a higher 5-year revision rate (19.0% vs 14.8%). Lumbar microdiscectomy and laminectomy are not competitors — one treats herniation, the other stenosis — and SPORT still anchors the long-term picture (8-year ODI advantage −11.2 points for discectomy).
- **Plexopathy is a localization exercise, not a spine problem.** Multi-nerve, multi-root, plexus-coherent deficits plus the SNAP/paraspinal rule (reduced SNAPs, normal paraspinals) separate it from radiculopathy; dedicated plexus MRI/MR neurography is the imaging test (ACR), and brachial plexus MRI runs 81% sensitive / 91% specific. The commonest lumbosacral cause in a 2026 tertiary series was diabetic LRPN — a radiculoplexus neuropathy, so paraspinal denervation does not exclude a plexus process.

**Key sources:** ACR Appropriateness Criteria Cervical Pain or Cervical Radiculopathy: 2024 Update (Eldaya et al., JACR 2025); ACR Appropriateness Criteria Plexopathy: 2021 Update (Boulter et al., JACR 2021); VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Low Back Pain (2022); NICE Low Back Pain and Sciatica (NG59); AAN 2025 epidural steroid injection review; FACET trial (ACDF vs PCF); SPORT (lumbar disc herniation and lumbar stenosis); PRECISE trial (NEJM 2017); Goldberg et al. oral prednisone trial; Fritz et al. early PT trial; AANEM electrodiagnostic guidance.
