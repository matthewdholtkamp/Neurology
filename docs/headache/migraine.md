# Migraine

<span class="reviewed-stamp">Last reviewed: November 2026</span>

**One-liner:** recurrent, disabling headache — usually lateralized, throbbing, moderate-to-severe,
worse with activity, with photophobia/phonophobia, nausea, and sometimes aura. Diagnose the headache phenotype; a normal examination and absent red flags support a primary
headache diagnosis but do not establish migraine. Tension-type and other primary headaches still exist.

!!! danger "🚩 Before you call it migraine"
    Clear the [headache red flags](index.md) first. Thunderclap onset, fever/neck stiffness,
    focal deficit, papilledema, new headache after 50, new headache in pregnancy, or a
    **change in an established pattern** all mean *work it up as a secondary headache first.*

## Rapid approach

1. **Exclude secondary headache** (red-flag screen). Normal exam + no red flags → proceed.
2. **Diagnose by phenotype** (ICHD-3, below). Imaging is **not** required for typical migraine
   with a normal exam.
3. **Treat the attack** — stratified care: match drug strength to attack severity, treat early.
4. **Decide on prevention** — if attacks are frequent or disabling, or acute meds are overused.
5. **Screen for and unwind medication overuse.**
6. **Disposition:** outpatient when secondary causes have been addressed, symptoms are controlled,
   oral intake is adequate, and follow-up is safe. A migraine history does not override a new red flag.

## Diagnosis (ICHD-3, summarized)

**Migraine without aura:** ≥5 attacks lasting 4–72 h with **≥2 of** {unilateral, pulsating,
moderate/severe, aggravated by routine activity} **and ≥1 of** {nausea/vomiting, *or* photophobia
**and** phonophobia}, not better explained by another diagnosis.

**Migraine with aura:** ≥2 attacks with **≥1 fully reversible aura symptom** (visual, sensory,
speech/language, motor, brainstem, or retinal) **and ≥3 of these 6 characteristics:** (1) at least
one symptom spreads gradually over ≥5 min; (2) two or more symptoms occur in succession; (3) each
symptom lasts 5–60 min; (4) at least one symptom is **unilateral**; (5) at least one symptom is
**positive** (e.g., scintillations, pins-and-needles); (6) aura is accompanied by, or followed
within 60 min by, headache. **Motor aura = hemiplegic migraine; brainstem aura** — both are red
flags to distinguish from TIA/stroke on first presentation and both **contraindicate triptans/ergots**.

**Chronic migraine:** headache ≥15 days/month for >3 months, with migrainous features ≥8 days/month.
Screen for **medication overuse** in every chronic patient.

## Focused H&P + exam

- **Attack profile:** frequency (headache days/month), duration, disability (MIDAS/HIT-6 if outpatient),
  aura, triggers, and — critically — **how many days/month they take acute medication** (MOH screen).
- **Exam:** vitals with BP, **fundoscopy**, cranial nerves, visual fields, focal screen, gait. Normal
  between attacks. Any fixed deficit → re-open the secondary work-up.
- **Diary:** have the patient keep a headache diary; it drives both prevention decisions and
  (for service members) profile and disability documentation.

## Workup

Typical migraine with a normal exam needs **no imaging or labs**. Image (MRI preferred) only for
red flags, atypical/persistent aura, or a change in pattern. Do not order EEG for migraine.

## Acute (abortive) treatment — the ladder

**Four rules that matter more than drug choice:** (1) **treat early** — at the first sign of pain,
while it is still mild; (2) **match strength to severity** (stratified care — don't make a severe
attack fail acetaminophen first); (3) **give an adequate dose and route** — under-dosing and a
vomited pill are the two commonest "triptan failures"; (4) **cap acute days to prevent
medication-overuse headache (MOH)** — and note the *prevention cap* is stricter than the
*diagnostic threshold*:

- **Recommended cap (to prevent MOH):** **triptans, ergots, opioids, and combination analgesics
  ≤2 days/week (≈8–10 days/month)**; **plain NSAIDs/simple analgesics ≤3 days/week (≈10–15
  days/month)**.
- **ICHD-3 diagnostic threshold for MOH** (a higher bar — this is when you *label* it, see MOH
  section): **≥10 days/month** for triptans/ergots/opioids/combinations, **≥15 days/month** for
  simple NSAIDs/acetaminophen.
- **Gepants have not shown the established MOH signal of triptans/opioids, but this does not mean
  unlimited treatment.** Follow each label: safety beyond **8 treated attacks/30 days** is not
  established for ubrogepant or zavegepant, or beyond **18 rimegepant doses/30 days**. Frequent rescue
  use still prompts a prevention review; prescription quantity limits are a separate issue.

!!! orderset "Step 1 — mild-to-moderate attack (start here for most)"
    Any one of these, taken **early**, with an antiemetic if nauseated:

    - **Naproxen 500–550 mg**, **ibuprofen 400–600 mg**, or **aspirin 900–1000 mg** PO.
    - **APAP/aspirin/caffeine** (250/250/65 mg, 2 tabs) is another acute option **only when aspirin
      is appropriate**. It contains an NSAID; do not substitute it for an NSAID contraindication.
    - **Antiemetic adjunct:** **metoclopramide 10 mg** or **prochlorperazine 10 mg** — these
      treat the *headache*, not just the nausea, and speed oral-drug absorption.

    → No reliable relief across **2–3 attacks**, or attacks are moderate-to-severe from the
    start → **Step 2**.

!!! orderset "Step 2 — moderate-to-severe attack: a triptan (first-line migraine-specific drug)"
    **Default:** **sumatriptan 50–100 mg PO** at onset; **may repeat once after ≥2 h** (max 200
    mg/day). Even better for moderate/severe attacks: **sumatriptan 85 mg + naproxen 500 mg
    together** (co-administration aborts more attacks than either alone) — a reasonable go-to.

    Pick the **formulation** to fit the attack:

    - **Fast onset / vomiting / wakes with pain:** **sumatriptan 6 mg SC** (works in ~10 min —
      the most effective route) or **zolmitriptan 5 mg / sumatriptan 10–20 mg nasal**.
    - **Wants an oral that acts fast:** **rizatriptan 10 mg ODT** or **eletriptan 40 mg** (most
      potent oral triptan).
    - **Long attacks / high recurrence:** **naratriptan 2.5 mg** or **frovatriptan 2.5 mg**
      (long half-life, lower recurrence — also used for menstrual mini-prophylaxis).

    **"Triptan failure" means ≥2–3 different triptans tried adequately** — full dose, taken early,
    ideally including a non-oral route — not one under-dosed pill. Optimize before escalating.

    !!! danger "Do NOT give a triptan (or ergot) if:"
        Coronary, cerebrovascular, or peripheral vascular disease; uncontrolled hypertension;
        **hemiplegic or brainstem-aura migraine** under current product labeling. If a triptan is
        contraindicated, consider a non-vasoconstrictor after its own safety review. **Pregnancy needs
        a separate obstetric-informed plan; do not automatically substitute a gepant or lasmiditan.**

!!! orderset "Step 3 — triptan failed (≥2 tried) OR contraindicated: gepant or ditan"
    These agents do not cause triptan-like vasoconstriction, but **absence of that contraindication
    is not proof of safety in every vascular patient**. Check hypertension/Raynaud warnings, drug
    interactions, organ function, and pregnancy. Gepants have a lower observed MOH concern; label
    exposure limits still apply.

    - **Gepants (small-molecule CGRP-receptor antagonists — oral/nasal):**
        - **Ubrogepant (Ubrelvy) 50–100 mg** PO; may repeat once after ≥2 h (max 200 mg/day).
        - **Rimegepant (Nurtec ODT) 75 mg** — max 1/day; **uniquely doubles as a preventive**
          (every-other-day), so it's the pick when a patient needs both.
        - **Zavegepant (Zavzpret) 10 mg nasal** — fast, useful with vomiting.
    - **Ditan — lasmiditan (Reyvow) 50 / 100 / 200 mg** PO: effective with no vascular
      contraindication, **but sedating with a hard ≥8-hour driving/operating restriction** and CNS
      depression — poor fit for pilots, drivers, and duty. Don't stack with other serotonergic/CNS
      depressants.
    - **DHE is a separate vasoconstricting ergot option**, not a substitute when vascular disease
      contraindicates triptans. See the specialist status-migrainosus section and its exclusions.

    !!! tip "TRICARE — the acute gepant is Ubrelvy, and it needs a PA"
        On TRICARE, **ubrogepant (Ubrelvy)** is the **preferred** acute gepant, but it is **not
        PA-free**. The DoD prior-authorization criteria require: **age ≥18**, **prescribed by or in
        consultation with a neurologist**, and a **contraindication/intolerance/2-month failure of
        ≥2 triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, or eletriptan), and it **cannot be
        combined with another gepant/Nurtec**. Practically: **document two failed triptans and loop
        in neuro**, and the PA goes through. **Rimegepant (Nurtec) is non-preferred** (higher tier).

!!! warning "Do not use — for acute migraine"
    **Opioids and butalbital-containing combinations** (e.g., Fioricet). Poor efficacy, high
    dependence and MOH risk, and they blunt response to specific drugs. **Don't start them; taper
    patients already on them.** This holds in clinic, the ED, and on the ward.

## Preventive treatment — the ladder

!!! orderset "Who needs prevention (start it if ANY apply)"
    Per the **2026 AAN/AHS prevention guideline**: **tell every migraine patient that effective
    preventive treatments exist** *(Level B)*, and **offer prevention** *(Level B)* if ANY apply:

    - **≥4 migraine days/month**, **or**
    - **≥4 moderate-to-severe headache days/month**, **or**
    - **Substantial migraine-related disability — at any frequency**, **or**
    - Acute treatment **overused, failing, or contraindicated**, **or**
    - Patient preference, **or**
    - **Regardless of frequency:** hemiplegic or brainstem-aura migraine, prolonged/persistent
      aura, migrainous infarction, or status migrainosus.

    **Set expectations up front (2026 AAN/AHS, Recs 14–17):** every preventive gets an **adequate
    trial — at least 8–12 weeks at the recommended tolerated dose** before you call it a failure
    *(Level B)*; **onabotulinumtoxinA gets 24 weeks** (two injection cycles) *(Level B)*. If
    response is suboptimal at **8 weeks, push to the maximum tolerated dose** before switching
    *(Level B)*; still suboptimal at **12–24 weeks** → decide together whether to switch
    *(Level B)*. **Counsel on common and serious adverse effects before the first prescription**
    *(Level A)*. Track with a **diary** (or the same **HIT-6/MIDAS** at every visit) and judge
    response against the **patient's own goals** *(Level A)* — frequency, severity, associated
    symptoms, quality of life, and acute-med use, not headache days alone. After **6 months** of
    treatment, **discuss tapering** — and warn that stopping a preventive can **increase headache
    days and worsen quality of life** *(Level B)*. Start low, titrate slowly, and change **one**
    thing at a time.

### The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)

1. **Everyone:** lifestyle foundation (below) + a written acute plan + a headache diary.
2. **Pick the first preventive by the patient's priority** (2026 AAN/AHS Rec 3) — **efficacy** →
   a high/moderate-confidence agent (Tier 1 or 2); **tolerability** → atogepant or a CGRP
   antibody; **long-term safety** → propranolol/topiramate (episodic) or
   topiramate/onabotulinumtoxinA (chronic); **cost** → a generic oral. **No drug has to fail
   before another is tried** — the guideline found no agent clearly superior.
3. **Match the comorbidity** (Tier 1 table) — one drug treating two conditions beats two drugs.
4. **Inadequate after an adequate trial (12–24 weeks)?** Switch **class or mechanism** — oral ⇄
   CGRP, ligand ⇄ receptor antibody (see the box) — or move to an **oral gepant** preventive.
   (On TRICARE the frictionless CGRP entry point is **Emgality**, see below.)
5. **Chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month):** add **onabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT)**; it can be
   **combined with a CGRP** therapy in refractory disease (expert practice — the 2026 guideline
   makes no graded recommendation on combining preventives).
6. **Still refractory:** neurology referral, combine Botox + CGRP, consider **IV eptinezumab**.

=== "Tier 1 — oral first-line (pick by comorbidity)"
    Well-established, low-cost options. **Check the exact TRICARE product and dispensing rules**. Let the comorbidity choose
    the drug — that's how you get a two-for-one and the best adherence. (2026 AAN/AHS confidence:
    **propranolol, topiramate, and valproate are moderate-confidence** for episodic migraine;
    **amitriptyline and metoprolol are low-confidence**; **candesartan's evidence was judged
    insufficient** — see the evidence table below. All remain reasonable comorbidity-driven picks.)

    | Patient / comorbidity | Preferred agent | Target dose | Watch for |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | HTN, palpitations, anxiety, tremor | **Propranolol** (or metoprolol, timolol) | Propranolol LA **80–160 mg/day** (up to 240) | Fatigue, bradycardia, bronchospasm; **avoid in asthma**; caution in depression |
    | Overweight/obese, comorbid seizure | **Topiramate** | Titrate **25 mg/week → 50–100 mg/day** (divided) | Paresthesias, cognitive slowing, kidney stones, **teratogenic**, flat soda taste; **counsel: >200 mg/day can blunt hormonal contraception (Level A)**; the guideline's oral pick when BMI is increased *(Level B)* |
    | Insomnia, comorbid depression/tension-type, or fibromyalgia | **Amitriptyline / nortriptyline** | **10–50 mg qHS** | Anticholinergic effects, weight gain, morning grogginess (nortriptyline milder); **low-confidence episodic evidence in the 2026 review** — but the guideline's pick with comorbid **fibromyalgia** *(Level B)*; urinary-retention caution in older men |
    | Can't use a beta-blocker, wants once-daily | **Candesartan** | **8–16 mg/day** | Dizziness, hyperkalemia, renal function; **2026 review: insufficient evidence to rate** — widely used and reasonable, but not guideline-endorsed (its largest RCT post-dates the review's search) |
    | Untreated HTN — one drug for both (2026 Rec 9, *Level C*) | **Telmisartan, nifedipine, or enalapril** | Telmisartan **80 mg/day**; nifedipine ER **30–60 mg/day**; enalapril **10–20 mg/day** | Low-confidence migraine evidence — the point is dual benefit in untreated hypertension |
    | Frequent aura, **not** childbearing potential | **Valproate / divalproex** | **500–1000 mg/day** | **Never in pregnancy potential** (neural-tube defects); **counsel: increased PCOS risk (Level A)**; tremor, weight, LFTs |
    | Menstrual-pattern | **Frovatriptan / naratriptan** perimenstrual mini-prophylaxis | Frovatriptan 2.5 mg BID × 6 days around menses | Short-term, timed to cycle |

=== "Tier 2 — CGRP-targeted (first-line option — AHS 2024; 2026 AAN/AHS Rec 3)"
    Per the **2024 AHS position statement** and now the **2026 AAN/AHS guideline**, CGRP therapies
    are a **first-line option** — there is **no requirement to fail two older orals first**
    *clinically*. The 2026 guideline seats the **four antibodies and atogepant** in both its
    **efficacy-priority (Rec 3A)** and **tolerability-priority (Rec 3B)** lists for episodic and
    chronic migraine — without ranking them above older agents across the board (when long-term
    safety is the concern, it prefers propranolol/topiramate/onabotulinumtoxinA). (Insurers,
    including TRICARE, may still require step therapy for the *non-preferred* agents — see the
    TRICARE box.) Two formats:

    - **Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies** (injectable/IV): **erenumab 70–140 mg SC monthly**,
      **fremanezumab 225 mg SC monthly or 675 mg SC quarterly**, **galcanezumab 120 mg SC monthly**
      (240 mg loading dose), **eptinezumab 100–300 mg IV quarterly**. Best adherence, cleanest
      tolerability — **watch constipation and BP with erenumab**, injection-site reactions
      with all.
    - **Oral gepants for prevention:** **atogepant (Qulipta): episodic migraine 10, 30, or 60 mg daily; chronic migraine 60 mg daily**, or **rimegepant
      (Nurtec) 75 mg every other day** — rimegepant **uniquely doubles as acute + preventive**.
      Good for needle-averse patients; gepants **do not cause MOH**. Note the 2026 split:
      **atogepant** is in the guideline's efficacy-priority list; **rimegepant is not** (moderate
      confidence for headache-day reduction, **low** for the ≥50% responder outcome — see the
      evidence table below).

    See the **receptor-vs-ligand** box below to choose the antibody and to switch intelligently.

=== "Tier 3 — chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month)"
    - **OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT protocol):** **155 units across 31 fixed sites every 12 weeks**
      (up to **195 units / 39 sites** with the optional "follow-the-pain" additions) — **chronic
      migraine ONLY**, not episodic.
    - **Botox and CGRP therapies are both first-tier for chronic migraine and can be combined** in
      refractory cases.

=== "Foundation — non-drug (for every patient)"
    - Regular **sleep, meals, hydration, aerobic exercise**; trigger awareness; **limit caffeine and
      acute-med days**; treat comorbid **mood, sleep, and anxiety** disorders.
    - **Neuromodulation devices** (e-TNS/Cefaly, remote electrical neuromodulation/Nerivio, sTMS,
      non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation) — useful when drugs are contraindicated, unwanted, or in
      pregnancy. (Outside the 2026 guideline's scope — it graded drugs only.)

### The 2026 AAN/AHS evidence hierarchy — confidence by drug

The August 2026 guideline's companion systematic review (217 RCTs; evidence through June 2024)
grades **confidence in the evidence** drug by drug — the first time legacy orals, botulinum
toxin, and CGRP therapies sit on one scale. The ratings are **outcome-specific** (headache-day
reduction and ≥50% response can differ for the same drug) and are **not a superiority ranking**:
head-to-head evidence was mostly low or very low confidence, and the guideline is explicit that
**no single drug is clearly superior**.

| Confidence | Episodic migraine | Chronic migraine |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Erenumab, galcanezumab | Fremanezumab, galcanezumab, onabotulinumtoxinA |
| **Moderate** | Atogepant, eptinezumab, fremanezumab, propranolol, topiramate, valproate | Atogepant, eptinezumab, erenumab, topiramate, valproate |
| **Low** | Amitriptyline, bisoprolol, flunarizine, fluoxetine, levetiracetam, metoprolol, nifedipine, pizotifen, telmisartan | Not separately graded |
| **Insufficient / very low** | Candesartan — and, among others, atenolol, timolol, venlafaxine, gabapentin | — |

Three readings that change practice:

- **Amitriptyline is low-confidence** for episodic migraine — fifty years of habit outrun its
  trial base. It keeps defined roles (insomnia, tension-type overlap, comorbid fibromyalgia —
  see below), but it is no longer the default "strong" oral.
- **Rimegepant is the within-class exception:** moderate confidence for reducing headache
  days, **low** confidence for the ≥50% responder outcome — which is why the guideline leaves
  it **out of the efficacy-priority list** while atogepant is in it. "CGRP" is not itself an
  evidence tier.
- **Candesartan was judged insufficient-evidence — not ineffective.** The review's search
  closed in June 2024, before the largest candesartan RCT (Øie et al., *Lancet Neurology*
  2025). Keep using it where it fits (hypertension, once-daily, well tolerated); just don't
  cite the 2026 guideline as endorsing it.

### Special populations (2026 AAN/AHS)

- **Pregnancy and childbearing potential.** Counsel every patient of childbearing potential on
  fetal risk in an unplanned pregnancy, and **avoid divalproex/valproate and topiramate where
  possible** *(Level A)* — FDA labeling contraindicates valproate for migraine prevention in
  pregnancy outright. **Maximize nonpharmacologic prevention** (behavioral interventions,
  acupuncture, exercise, trigger management) in pregnancy and planning *(Level A)*; if a drug
  is unavoidable, decide jointly and use the least exposure that holds control *(Level A)*.
  When a preventive is needed in pregnancy: **nifedipine** may be offered *(Level C)*; if it
  fails or doesn't fit, **metoprolol or propranolol** *(Level C)*; for chronic migraine,
  **onabotulinumtoxinA** may be offered, with outcome data extremely limited *(Level C)*.
  **CGRP antibodies and gepants are not pregnancy options** in the guideline — insufficient
  data; stop long-half-life agents well before a planned pregnancy. **Lactation:** counsel
  that preventives enter breast milk in varying, often poorly characterized amounts, and check
  **LactMed** *(Level A)*.
- **Older adults.** Assess vascular disease, drug interactions, and renal/hepatic function
  *(Level B)*; discuss sedation and confusion risk (amitriptyline, valproate, topiramate,
  pizotifen) and consider lower starting doses *(Level B)*; discuss hypotension/orthostasis —
  avoid strong blood-pressure-lowering agents or start low *(Level A)*. In **older men**,
  weigh urinary-retention risk before anticholinergics such as amitriptyline *(Level B)*.
- **Comorbidity picks.** Untreated hypertension: **enalapril, nifedipine, or telmisartan** can
  treat both conditions *(Level C)*. **Fibromyalgia: offer amitriptyline** *(Level B)*.
  Increased BMI, when an oral preventive is being considered: **offer topiramate** *(Level B)*.
  Whether to treat a comorbidity with the same drug or separately is itself a shared decision
  *(Level B)*.
- **Two counseling mandates.** **Valproate → warn about increased polycystic ovary syndrome
  risk** *(Level A)*. **Topiramate → warn that doses above 200 mg/day can make hormonal
  contraception less effective** *(Level A)*.

### CGRP antibodies: receptor vs. ligand — which to start, how to switch

The user's key question, answered directly: **only one CGRP antibody blocks the receptor; the other
three bind the CGRP peptide (ligand) itself.** The oral **gepants** are all small-molecule
**receptor** antagonists.

| Agent (brand) | Target | Route / interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Erenumab (Aimovig)** | **CGRP *receptor*** (the only receptor mAb) | SC **monthly** (70 or 140 mg) | Watch **constipation** and **rising BP**; no loading dose |
| **Fremanezumab (Ajovy)** | CGRP *ligand* | SC **monthly or quarterly** | Only mAb with a **quarterly** option — good adherence |
| **Galcanezumab (Emgality)** | CGRP *ligand* | SC monthly (**240 mg load**, then 120 mg) | Also FDA-approved for **episodic cluster** |
| **Eptinezumab (Vyepti)** | CGRP *ligand* | **IV quarterly** | **Fastest onset**; guarantees adherence; good for chronic/refractory |
| Atogepant (Qulipta) · Rimegepant (Nurtec) | Receptor (small molecule) | Oral | Preventive gepants; Nurtec = acute + preventive |

!!! tip "How to choose and switch"
    - **Efficacy is comparable across the antibodies** — choose on **route/interval, comorbidity,
      and formulary** (on TRICARE that means **start Emgality**, see below).
    - **After an adequate trial, switching antibodies may help some patients.** A receptor-to-ligand
      switch or the reverse is reasonable, as is an alternative within the ligand group. Evidence is
      largely observational; it does **not** establish that crossing targets is consistently superior.
    - **Antibody class failing entirely?** Try an **oral gepant** (Qulipta/Nurtec) — different
      pharmacology, and a reasonable next mechanism before Botox/combination therapy.

!!! tip "TRICARE — verified rules and the current form"
    For migraine, **Emgality 120 mg** had no PA displayed in the live lookup; the **100 mg episodic-cluster formulation requires PA**. Ubrelvy/Qulipta have PA or claims-history rules; Nurtec is nonformulary with separate PA/MN requirements; Zavzpret is nonformulary with PA. See the **retrieved forms and exact failure/renewal criteria** below. A PTH/NDPH phenotype does not automatically establish covered Botox treatment.

    [Current PA/MN details, benefit routing and packet](../military/tricare.md) · [Live Formulary Search](https://www.express-scripts.com/frontend/open-enrollment/tricare/fst). Check the exact product, beneficiary and venue on the prescribing date.

## Order set — status migrainosus / ER

Graded to the **2025 American Headache Society guideline for parenteral migraine treatment in the
ED** (Robblee et al.). Two headline points: **prochlorperazine** and the **greater occipital nerve
block** are now **Level A "must offer,"** and **opioids are to be avoided** (IV hydromorphone is a
Level A *"must NOT offer"*). Nerve blocks are opioid-sparing, fast, and safe in pregnancy — reach
for them early, not just as a last resort.

**Choose an appropriate first-line option, then reassess pain, function, BP, sedation, and adverse
effects at about 30–60 minutes.** The guideline grades individual treatments; it does not require
every patient to receive an NSAID, dopamine antagonist, and block together. Avoid automatic early
redosing or stacking dopamine antagonists: review total dose, QT risk, hypotension, and akathisia
before any additional treatment. A worsening or atypical course reopens the diagnosis.

!!! orderset "Tier 1 — first-line (offer to everyone; exclude red flags first)"
    Offer a guideline-supported option matched to contraindications and patient preference.
    A second modality may be added after reassessment; combination treatment is individualized.

    - **Prochlorperazine 10 mg IV** *(Level A — first choice)*, or **metoclopramide 10–20 mg IV**
      *(Level B — if prochlorperazine unavailable)*. Give using the institution’s administration protocol; reassess before further dosing.
        - *Akathisia/dystonia:* many co-give **diphenhydramine 25 mg IV**; note the 2025 guideline
          rates diphenhydramine "may not offer" as a *migraine treatment* and evidence that it
          prevents akathisia is mixed — use it for the reaction, not for the headache.
    - **Ketorolac 15–30 mg IV** *(Level B)* — or **dexketoprofen 50 mg IV** or **diclofenac 75 mg
      IM** where available.
    - **Sumatriptan 6 mg SC** *(Level B)* — if not already used and no vascular contraindication.
    - **IV fluids — normal saline ~1 L** only if the patient is dehydrated (or before chlorpromazine);
      fluids alone are **not** a migraine treatment.

!!! orderset "Tier 1 — nerve blocks (opioid-sparing options)"
    - **Greater occipital nerve block (GONB)** *(Level A — must offer)*. **Bilateral**, ~**2–3 mL of
      bupivacaine 0.5%** (or 1–2% lidocaine) per side over the greater occipital nerve — landmark:
      ~1/3 of the way from the occipital protuberance toward the mastoid, medial to the occipital
      artery pulse. Onset ~10–15 min. Strong **adjunct** (a 2025 RCT: ~95% vs ~48% ≥50% relief at
      2 h as add-on); especially useful with **occipital tenderness** or when avoiding systemic drugs.
    - **Supraorbital / supratrochlear nerve blocks** *(Level B)* — for frontal pain; ~**1–2 mL of
      bupivacaine 0.5% or 1–2% lidocaine per site**.
    - **Sphenopalatine ganglion (SPG) block** *(emerging; heterogeneous evidence)* — transnasal
      **2–4% lidocaine** via a long cotton-tipped applicator or catheter device (e.g., ~1–2 mL per
      nostril); simple and low-risk, reasonable as an adjunct.

!!! orderset "Tier 2 — add if inadequate at ~60 min; reassess again at 30–60 min"
    - **Magnesium sulfate 1–2 g IV** over ~15–30 min — best evidence in **migraine with aura**.
    - **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV** *(Level C)* — modest acute benefit **and** reduces 24–72 h
      recurrence (NNT ~9); consider for recurrence prevention after weighing contraindications and recent steroid exposure.
    - **Valproate 500–1000 mg IV** *(Level C)* — avoid in pregnancy/childbearing potential.
    - **Chlorpromazine 12.5–25 mg IV** *(Level C)* — alternative neuroleptic. Use the monitored local protocol, assess volume status and BP, and account for preceding dopamine-antagonist doses. Avoid automatic repeat dosing.

!!! orderset "Tier 3 — refractory / status migrainosus (monitored; consider neurology)"
    - **Droperidol: the guideline’s Level C recommendation is for IM dosing, not IV** or **haloperidol 2.5–5 mg IV** *(Level C)* — alternative neuroleptics when preferred agents are unsuitable.
      Use a local protocol for dose/route and cumulative exposure. **Check ECG/QT and electrolytes**,
      consider prior dopamine-antagonist doses, and monitor hypotension, sedation, and extrapyramidal
      reactions. Do not automatically stack or redose these drugs.
    - **Dihydroergotamine (DHE) 0.5–1 mg IV**, preceded by an **antiemetic** (e.g., metoclopramide
      10 mg or ondansetron 4 mg IV); may repeat **0.5–1 mg q8h**. **Contraindicated** in coronary/
      peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled HTN, a triptan/ergot within 24 h, or pregnancy. For
      **admitted** status migrainosus, the inpatient **Raskin protocol** (IV DHE q8h over 2–3 days,
      with an antiemetic) can break the cycle.
    - **Specialist/monitored infusions** (procedural-sedation monitoring where relevant):
        - **IV lidocaine** — 1 mg/kg bolus, then ~2 mg/min infusion (do not exceed 4 mg/min; keep
          level < 5 µg/mL); status-migrainosus courses may run up to ~2 days.
        - **Propofol** — 0.5 mg/kg bolus, then 0.25 mg/kg every 10 min for up to 1 h.
        - **Low-dose ketamine** — subacute infusion ~0.1–0.4 mg/kg/h (note: a single **low-dose ED
          bolus was negative** in an RCT, so reserve ketamine for monitored refractory use).

!!! note "Eptinezumab (Vyepti) IV — not a routine ED drug"
    The 2025 AHS guideline evaluated **eptinezumab 100 mg IV** for acute treatment: it beat placebo
    for 2-h headache freedom (23.5% vs 12.0%), but the pivotal RCT was run **mostly outpatient**. So
    the guideline rates it **Level B ("should offer") *only* for patients matching that trial
    population**, and **Level U (no recommendation) for the general ED population**. Practically:
    **do not use it routinely for a typical ED visit** — infusion logistics, cost,
    and limited ED-specific evidence constrain its role. Its real role is **outpatient prevention** (see the preventive ladder).

!!! orderset "Before discharge & what to avoid"
    - **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV/IM once** if not already given — reduces early recurrence.
    - Start/adjust a **preventive**, give a **written acute-medication cap**, and arrange follow-up.
    - **Avoid opioids and butalbital** — Level A *must-not-offer* (hydromorphone); they worsen
      outcomes and seed medication-overuse headache.
    - Speak to the nurse directly to sequence meds; if admitted, add scheduled antiemetic and sleep
      hygiene.

## Medication overuse headache (MOH)

Suspect when headaches are frequent **and** acute meds are used often — **≥10 days/month**
for triptans/ergots/opioids/combination analgesics, or **≥15 days/month** for simple NSAIDs/
acetaminophen (ICHD-3 diagnostic thresholds). The **2026 AAN/AHS guideline** states the
**medication-overuse** cutoffs as acute migraine drugs on **>9 days/month** for prescription
pain-relieving medications, or **>14 days/month** for nonspecific pain medications — the same
boundaries as the ICHD-3 thresholds above, grouped into two buckets — and says to
**offer a preventive to every patient with medication overuse or MOH** *(Level B)*, **preferring
agents proven in that population: CGRP monoclonal antibodies, atogepant, onabotulinumtoxinA, and
topiramate** *(Level B)*. Management: **start the preventive** — in the evidence the guideline
reviewed, starting a preventive **alone** performed as well as preventive-plus-withdrawal, so lead
with prevention rather than detox alone — **cap or withdraw the overused agent**, bridge symptoms
(e.g., NSAID/antiemetic course ± short steroid taper), and counsel. **Taper butalbital and opioids
under supervision** (abrupt withdrawal risks seizures and withdrawal syndromes; the trial evidence
barely covers those two classes). **Gepants may be useful acute options when overuse is a concern.** Respect the product’s exposure limits and reassess the need for frequent rescue treatment.

## Disposition

Discharge when secondary causes have been addressed, symptoms and oral intake are manageable, and follow-up is reliable. Give an acute plan, return precautions, and a prevention review. Persistent inability to hydrate, an unresolved red flag, or a concerning change in pattern warrants observation, admission, or further evaluation.

!!! military "Military medicine — duty, retention and deployment"
    - **Profile / function:** Record incapacitating headache days, abortive success, preventive trials and drug effects. Grounding or hazardous-duty restrictions depend on attacks and treatment effects.
    - **Retention / MEB–MAR2:** Headache: DoDI V2 **5.26.g** and Army **3-31g**; trigeminal neuralgia and other diagnoses need their applicable standard, rather than automatically borrowing the migraine threshold. Use the [retention framework](../military/deployability.md); permanent P3/4 does not by itself select DES versus MAR2.
    - **Deployment / medications:** CENTCOM disruptive migraine may require waiver; injectable CGRP therapy/Botox is separately relevant. Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine are named medication exceptions even when used for pain/headache. See [the disease AND drug screen](../military/deployment-medications.md) for the current CENTCOM rules and waiver packet.
    - **Special duty:** flight, diving and other special assignments have separate standards and waiver authorities; a general duty profile does not clear them.
    - **LOD / EPTS:** document onset, duty status, exposures and aggravation objectively. The designated authority determines LOD/EPTS; neither in-service diagnosis nor a genetic finding settles it automatically.

## 📚 Discover — evidence brief

*Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.*

**What's changed and what matters now:**

- **Stratified beats step care.** Match acute-drug strength to attack severity from the start;
  a **triptan + NSAID** combination outperforms either agent alone for moderate/severe attacks.
- **Prevention is now governed by the August 2026 AAN/AHS guideline** (Potrebic et al.,
  *Neurology* 2026 — the first update since 2012), which replaces the 2012 drug-by-drug evidence
  levels with graded clinical recommendations. It **offers prevention at ≥4 migraine days/month,
  ≥4 moderate-to-severe headache days/month, or substantial disability at any frequency** (all
  *Level B*), chooses the drug by **patient priority — efficacy, tolerability, long-term safety,
  cost** — with **no step-therapy requirement**, and states plainly that **no drug is clearly
  superior**. It reaffirms the **2024 American Headache Society position statement** (Charles et
  al., *Headache*): anti-CGRP mAbs (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) and oral
  gepants (atogepant, rimegepant) are **a first-line option** with **no requirement to fail two
  8-week trials of older oral preventives first** — while adding the discipline the statement
  lacked: per-drug confidence ratings (next bullet).
- **The confidence table cuts across classes.** High confidence — episodic: **erenumab,
  galcanezumab**; chronic: **fremanezumab, galcanezumab, onabotulinumtoxinA**. **Amitriptyline is
  low-confidence**, **rimegepant is split** (moderate for headache-day reduction, low for ≥50%
  response — and excluded from the efficacy-priority list), and **candesartan was judged
  insufficient-evidence** because the review's June 2024 search predates its largest RCT. Legacy
  orals keep real roles: comorbidity two-for-ones, cost, pregnancy planning, and long track
  records — for long-term-safety concern the guideline actually prefers propranolol, topiramate,
  and onabotulinumtoxinA.
- **Special populations got graded answers for the first time:** avoid **valproate and
  topiramate** in childbearing potential *(A)*; in pregnancy maximize non-drug care *(A)*, then
  **nifedipine**, then **metoprolol/propranolol** *(C)*; counsel **PCOS risk with valproate** and
  **contraception failure with topiramate >200 mg/day** *(both A)*; in medication overuse, **lead
  with a preventive proven in overuse** *(B)*; **trial 8–12 weeks (24 for onabotulinumtoxinA)**
  before judging *(B)*; and **discuss tapering after 6 months** — stopping can increase headache
  days and worsen quality of life *(B)*.
- **Gepants and ditans expand acute options.** Gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant, zavegepant) and the
  ditan lasmiditan work **without vasoconstriction**, making them options in cardiovascular/
  cerebrovascular disease where triptans are contraindicated. **Gepants have a lower observed MOH concern; label exposure limits still apply.**
- **Receptor vs. ligand antibodies.** Only **erenumab (Aimovig)** blocks the CGRP *receptor*;
  **fremanezumab (Ajovy), galcanezumab (Emgality), eptinezumab (Vyepti)** bind the CGRP *ligand*.
  Efficacy is comparable, so choose on route/interval, comorbidity, and formulary — and when one
  fails, consider another antibody; observational evidence does **not establish that switching targets is superior** to switching within the ligand group.
- **TRICARE access affects the choice:** the live lookup showed no PA for **Emgality 120 mg for migraine**, while other products have PA/history rules or nonformulary requirements. This does not make it the best clinical choice for every patient or establish coverage for every phenotype. Use the [actual criteria and forms](../military/tricare.md#headache-current-forms-actually-retrieved).

- **OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT)** remains a first-tier preventive for **chronic** migraine only.
- **ED care leads with dopamine antagonists + nerve blocks, not opioids.** The **2025 AHS
  parenteral-ED guideline** (Robblee et al.) elevated **prochlorperazine** and the **greater
  occipital nerve block** to **Level A "must offer,"** kept metoclopramide/ketorolac/SC sumatriptan
  as "should offer," and made **IV hydromorphone a Level A "must-NOT-offer."** Nerve blocks can offer a rapid, opioid-sparing option. In pregnancy, review the procedure and local anesthetic with the treating obstetric team.
- **Avoid opioids and butalbital** — they worsen the disease and drive medication-overuse headache.
- **Prevention only works if trialed adequately** — 8–12 weeks at target dose before judging failure.
