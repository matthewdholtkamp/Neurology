    CIDP & IVIG Order Set¶

    Last reviewed: NOV 2026

    One-liner: the prescriptive approach to chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) under the 2021 EAN/PNS criteria — how to make the diagnosis and not miss its mimics, first-line IVIG vs steroids vs plasma exchange with exact doses, the practical IVIG order set (labs, rates, adverse-effect plan), subcutaneous immunoglobulin and efgartigimod for maintenance, and what to do when CIDP is refractory. For the neurologist, or the PCP/internist admitting a patient with progressive symmetric weakness and demyelinating nerve-conduction studies. Educational clinical decision-support, not medical advice — verify doses and eligibility against current labeling and guidelines before ordering.

    🚩 Red flags — reconsider CIDP, or act now

    Pattern Worry Do now
    Course <8 weeks to nadir, or rapidly progressive weakness Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (GBS/AIDP), not CIDP — CIDP requires ≥8 weeks Treat as GBS: admit, respiratory/bulbar monitoring, IVIG or PLEX urgently
    Respiratory or bulbar weakness Ventilatory failure — rare in CIDP, dangerous whenever present Admit, forced vital capacity / negative inspiratory force, ICU triage
    Marked asymmetry, severe pain, or stepwise deficits Vasculitic neuropathy or multifocal CIDP mimics (vasculitis, HNPP, multifocal motor neuropathy) Urgent vasculitis workup; do not default to "CIDP" on asymmetry alone
    IgM paraprotein, especially with distal sensory-predominant disease Anti-MAG neuropathy — no longer classed as CIDP, and it responds poorly to standard CIDP therapy Anti-MAG antibody; reframe the diagnosis before escalating immunotherapy
    Sensory neuronopathy pattern (sensory ataxia, pseudoathetosis, normal motor studies) Sensory neuronopathy or CISP, not sensory CIDP Dorsal-root-ganglion / paraneoplastic workup; sensory CIDP requires the pattern below
    Upper-motor-neuron signs, fasciculations with pure motor disease Motor neuron disease mimicking motor CIDP EMG + neurology review before committing to chronic immunotherapy
    Nodal–paranodal antibody clues (e.g. severe ataxia, tremor, poor IVIG response) Autoimmune nodopathy — a separate entity, not CIDP Nodal–paranodal antibody panel; nodopathies may need rituximab rather than IVIG

    Diagnosis / classification (2021 EAN/PNS)¶

    CIDP is a clinical phenotype + electrodiagnostic diagnosis. There are only two certainty levels — "CIDP" and "possible CIDP". The old "definite/probable/possible" scheme was dropped: without a gold standard, definite and probable performed no differently. All recommendations rest on low-certainty evidence, and real-world validation found more than a third of patients meeting electrodiagnostic criteria fit no defined phenotype — so phenotype first, numbers second.

    Typical CIDP — all of the following must be present¶

    • Symmetric weakness affecting proximal and distal muscles of the upper and lower limbs
    • Sensory involvement in ≥2 limbs
    • Progressive or relapsing course developing over ≥8 weeks
    • Reduced or absent tendon reflexes throughout the limbs

    For a variant, the distribution or modality differs as below; reflexes may remain normal in unaffected limbs.

    Variants¶

    Variant Defining clinical pattern Classic mimics to exclude
    Distal CIDP (formerly DADS) Distal sensory loss + gait instability; weakness if present is distal, worse in legs Anti-MAG / IgM paraprotein (~2/3 of the phenotype), diabetic, hereditary, amyloid
    Multifocal CIDP (Lewis–Sumner / MADSAM) Asymmetric sensory + motor involvement in ≥2 limbs, usually starting in the arms; cranial nerves more often involved Vasculitis, HNPP, multifocal motor neuropathy
    Focal CIDP Sensory + motor involvement confined to 1 limb Same as multifocal, localized
    Motor CIDP Motor symptoms/signs only, with normal sensory studies Multifocal motor neuropathy, motor neuron disease
    Motor-predominant CIDP Clinically motor presentation, with sensory abnormalities detected on nerve-conduction studies As for motor CIDP
    Sensory CIDP Sensory symptoms/signs only (motor conduction must be normal) Sensory neuronopathy, CISP, anti-MAG neuropathy
    Sensory-predominant CIDP Sensory presentation with minor motor involvement As for sensory CIDP

    No longer classed as CIDP: autoimmune nodopathies (nodal–paranodal antibodies), anti-MAG neuropathy, and chronic immune sensory polyradiculopathy (CISP). Calling them CIDP misdirects therapy.

    Electrodiagnostic criteria — motor conduction (strongly supportive of demyelination)¶

    Study >4 nerves, add proximal stimulation in the upper limbs, keep limbs warm (palm ≥33 °C, malleolus ≥30 °C), and repeat later if criteria are not met the first time. At least one of the following, with nerve counts by phenotype:

    • Distal motor latency ≥50% above the upper limit of normal (ULN) in 2 nerves (median nerve with carpal tunnel syndrome excluded)
    • Conduction velocity ≥30% below the lower limit of normal (LLN) in 2 nerves
    • F-wave latency ≥20% above ULN in 2 nerves (≥50% if distal CMAP <80% LLN)
    • Absent F-waves in 2 nerves (only if distal CMAP ≥20% LLN) plus ≥1 other demyelinating parameter in ≥1 other nerve
    • Motor conduction block — proximal/distal CMAP amplitude reduction ≥30% (distal CMAP ≥20% LLN) in 2 nerves, or 1 nerve + another demyelinating parameter in another nerve
    • Abnormal temporal dispersion in ≥2 nerves
    • Distal CMAP duration prolongation in ≥1 nerve + ≥1 other demyelinating parameter in another nerve

    Sensory conduction abnormalities are now required: typical CIDP needs them in ≥2 nerves.

    Certainty — how the pieces combine¶

    • Typical CIDP = "CIDP" if motor criteria are met in ≥2 nerves and sensory abnormalities in ≥2 nerves; "possible" if motor criteria in only 1 nerve.
    • If the clinical picture fits but minimal electrodiagnostic criteria are not met, possible typical CIDP can still be diagnosed with an objective response to IVIG, steroids, or plasma exchange.
    • Distal / multifocal / focal / motor variants follow the same structure: "CIDP" when ≥2 nerves meet criteria, "possible" when 1 does; the nerves must fit the phenotype pattern (distal criteria are especially strict — the main source of misdiagnosis).
    • Focal CIDP and sensory CIDP can only ever be "possible CIDP" — supportive criteria cannot upgrade them.

    Supportive criteria — upgrade "possible" → "CIDP" (two needed, or one + objective treatment response in some pathways)¶

    • Raised CSF protein with WBC <10/µL
    • Nerve ultrasound enlargement of the proximal median nerve, cervical roots, or brachial plexus
    • MRI gadolinium enhancement or hypertrophy of roots/plexus
    • Objective improvement after treatment on validated scales
    • Nerve biopsy (sural, occasionally superficial peroneal) showing demyelination/remyelination — a positive biopsy is not specific, and a negative biopsy does not exclude CIDP

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Exclude the dangerous thing first: course <8 weeks, respiratory/bulbar involvement, or severe pain/asymmetry → GBS or vasculitis pathway, not routine CIDP workup.
    2. Classify the phenotype from the table above — the phenotype decides which electrodiagnostic counts apply and which mimic to chase.
    3. Order the required workup (below) and the baseline IVIG labs in the same visit — paraprotein and antibody results change the diagnosis, and serologies must be drawn before any immunoglobulin infusion.
    4. Route to the tiered management below and fix an objective baseline (INCAT, grip strength, I-RODS) before the first dose — without it, you cannot judge response, remission, or failure.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Confirm the phenotype and the electrodiagnostic criteria; complete the required workup (paraprotein screen, anti-MAG if IgM, nodal–paranodal antibodies, CSF, ultrasound/MRI as indicated).
    2. Start Step 1 first-line therapy, matched to the patient (table below) — IVIG 2 g/kg induction for most, pulsed steroids when cost/access or lasting remission is the priority, PLEX for severe disease or first-line failure.
    3. Measure response objectively at the same scale points. IVIG non-response after one course is not failure — give a second course or 1 g/kg every 3 weeks × 2–5 doses before calling it.
    4. If Step 1 fails or is not tolerated, switch to another first-line option before adding anything else — and reconsider the diagnosis first, because misdiagnosis is common in "refractory" CIDP.
    5. Maintenance: lowest dose holding maximal improvement; try reducing every 6–12 months in the first 2–3 years, then every 1–2 years, to test for remission.
    6. Refractory disease (Step 3): PLEX induction, then rituximab / cyclophosphamide / cyclosporine with a 3–6 month stop rule; efgartigimod as a maintenance alternative for IgG-mediated disease.

    Workup¶

    Required CIDP workup — order at diagnosis

    • Monoclonal gammopathy screen in every patient (serum/urine immunofixation + free light chains)
    • Anti-MAG antibody if an IgM paraprotein is present
    • Nodal–paranodal antibody panel (neurofascin, contactin, Caspr1 and related targets per local panel)
    • CSF: protein + cell count (raised protein with WBC <10/µL is supportive)
    • Nerve ultrasound (proximal median nerve, cervical roots, brachial plexus) or MRI of roots/plexus with gadolinium, where available
    • Nerve biopsy only when the diagnosis remains uncertain after the above — sural, occasionally superficial peroneal
    • Baseline objective scales before treatment: INCAT disability score, grip dynamometry, I-RODS, MRC sum score, timed walking
    • Draw any needed serologies BEFORE the first immunoglobulin infusion — passively transferred antibodies cause false positives, including a positive Coombs test

    Management¶

    Step 1 — first-line (start here for most)

    • IVIG — 2 g/kg total, split over 2–5 days (usual daily cap ≤80 g/day). Fastest response, fewer long-term side effects than steroids; ~76% of treatment-naive patients respond. High-certainty evidence vs placebo. Then maintenance 1 g/kg every 3 weeks (given in 1 day, or 0.5 g/kg on 2 consecutive days), titrating over time to the lowest effective dose, typically 0.4–1 g/kg every 2–6 weeks.
    • OR corticosteroids (regimens appear equally effective — pick one): prednisone/prednisolone 60 mg/day orally, tapered slowly over 6–8 months; OR pulsed dexamethasone 40 mg/day × 4 days every 4 weeks × 6 months; OR pulsed IV methylprednisolone 500 mg/day × 4 days each month × 6 months (or 1,000 mg weekly or monthly). Pulsed regimens have fewer side effects and act faster than daily oral (low–moderate certainty). Add calcium + vitamin D, consider a bisphosphonate; watch gastric ulceration, diabetes, hypertension, cataracts, avascular necrosis.
    • OR plasma exchange — 5–10 exchanges on alternate days over 2–4 weeks for severe disease or when IVIG/steroids are unsuitable. Similarly effective for induction, but invasive, needs venous access and specialist facilities, and is rarely used for maintenance.
    • Non-response rule: after a first IVIG course, give either 1 g/kg every 3 weeks for 2–5 doses or a second 2 g/kg course a few weeks later before calling IVIG failure. In ProCID, 56% improved 3 weeks after the loading dose alone; maintenance response was 65% at 0.5 g/kg, 80% at 1.0 g/kg, and 92% at 2.0 g/kg every 3 weeks (only 0.5 vs 2.0 differed significantly).
    • → Inadequate, intolerable, or contraindicated → Step 2.

    Step 2 — switch first-line, then maintenance alternatives

    • Switch to another first-line option before adding anything else. After IVIG failure, 67% responded to PLEX and 59% to steroids; ~75% who then failed PLEX/steroids responded to the remaining option. Involve the patient in the choice.
    • Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIg) for maintenance — FDA-approved for adult CIDP maintenance since 2018: 0.2 or 0.4 g/kg/week (split 2–3 infusions/week for induction-style loading where used: 0.4 g/kg/week × 5 weeks, limited evidence). In PATH, both doses beat placebo (absolute relapse reduction 25% at 0.2 g/kg, 30% at 0.4 g/kg). Good candidates: poor venous access, headache/systemic IVIG side effects, long distance to an infusion centre, frequent travel. More injection-site reactions; fewer systemic effects; no venous access or premedication needed.
    • Efgartigimod alfa–hyaluronidase (Vyvgart Hytrulo) — subcutaneous once weekly as a maintenance alternative: 1,000 mg efgartigimod alfa + 10,000 units hyaluronidase in a prefilled syringe (patient/caregiver-administered), or 1,008 mg + 11,200 units from a vial. FDA-approved for adults with CIDP June 2024 (also EMA-approved) on the ADHERE trial — see Discover. The approval is not restricted to patients who have failed IVIG, and it applies to the subcutaneous combination product (IV Vyvgart remains labeled for generalized myasthenia gravis). No head-to-head vs IVIG and no established superiority to IVIG; treat as an alternative maintenance option, not a replacement for established first-line therapy. Reasonable when maintenance IVIG/SCIg is burdensome, poorly tolerated, or contraindicated, when IVIG supply/access is a problem, or when the patient prefers weekly home self-injection. Caution: response was lower in previously IVIG-treated patients, and FcRn blockade acts on IgG, so presumed IgM- or IgA-mediated disease may not respond. How best to transition from IgG therapy is not yet defined, and a switch from effective IVIG requires careful monitoring — clinician ChatGPT flagged an FDA 2026 safety update concerning severe CIDP worsening after initiation, which was not independently verified; check the current label before switching.
    • → Still refractory after all three first-line options → Step 3.

    Step 3 — refractory CIDP

    • Plasma exchange induction first: 5 exchanges over 10 days.
    • Second-line (EAN/PNS, case-series evidence only): rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or cyclosporine — stop if no objective benefit within 3–6 months.
    • Steroid-/IVIG-sparing agents: azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclosporine — take >6 months to work; evidence for azathioprine and methotrexate is not robust.
    • Investigational / last resort (flagged as such, not routine): riliprubart (C1s complement inhibitor), phase 2 — 88% responded after switching from standard therapy, 50% of refractory patients improved. Other FcRn inhibitors (nipocalimab, batoclimab) are in trials; small phase 2 rozanolixizumab was negative. Autologous haematopoietic stem-cell transplant has been reported to benefit selected patients. Not supported: alemtuzumab, natalizumab, fingolimod, methotrexate, and others.
    • Before escalating any "refractory" label: specialty referral and diagnostic reconsideration — many patients labeled IVIG-refractory improve when maintenance is increased (e.g. every-2-week dosing), and misdiagnosis is common.

    Choosing first-line therapy¶

    Patient / situation Preferred agent Why
    Pure motor CIDP IVIG Avoid steroids — they can worsen motor CIDP
    Severe or rapidly progressing disease IVIG (or PLEX) Fastest response
    Osteoporosis, diabetes, or other steroid contraindication IVIG / SCIg Avoids long-term steroid toxicity
    Need for fast response IVIG Weeks vs months for steroids
    Cost/access matters, no steroid risk factors, lasting remission is the goal Corticosteroids, pulsed preferred Cheap; pulsed regimens may give more and longer remissions at 6 months
    Severe disease, or IVIG/steroids failed or unsuitable Plasma exchange Similarly effective induction; invasive and facility-dependent
    Maintenance burden, poor venous access, IVIG intolerance, or home-therapy preference SCIg, or efgartigimod weekly SC See Step 2 doses

    Tapering — test for remission, don't just continue¶

    • IVIG: once stable, try reducing the dose (e.g. by 25% per infusion), lengthening the interval, or stopping — every 6–12 months for the first 2–3 years, then every 1–2 years. Fix end-of-dose wearing-off first by raising the dose or shortening the interval; do not mistake wearing-off for treatment failure.
    • Steroids: taper daily prednisolone slowly over 6–8 months, guided by response and side effects; pulsed regimens run 6 months, daily prednisolone up to 15 months.
    • All therapies: separate active disease from fixed axonal deficits, which will not improve with more immunotherapy. Treatment failure means failure of an adequate trial on objective scales — not persistent numbness, pain, or fatigue alone.

    Avoid

    • Steroids in pure motor CIDP — use IVIG first.
    • Labeling IVIG failure after a single course — a second course or 1 g/kg every 3 weeks × 2–5 doses is required first.
    • Escalating immunotherapy for fixed axonal deficits — more drug will not recover dead axons; reassess the diagnosis instead.
    • Routine premedication for every IVIG infusion — reserve it for high-risk patients or prior reactions (see order set).
    • Routine anti-IgA testing — low IgA alone is not a contraindication (see order set).

    IVIG order set — practical prescribing¶

    IVIG order set for CIDP (adult, first infusion)

    • Dose: 2 g/kg total induction, split over 2–5 days (usual cap ≤80 g/day); maintenance 1 g/kg every 3 weeks, titrating to the lowest effective dose (0.4–1 g/kg every 2–6 weeks).
    • Pre-infusion labs / screening: BUN + creatinine before the first infusion and periodically after; confirm the patient is not volume-depleted. CBC + ABO blood group (non-O groups carry higher hemolysis risk, highest in AB then A); baseline haemoglobin as the reference for post-infusion monitoring. Clot-risk review: age >65, prior VTE/arterial events, atherosclerosis/cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, immobility, dehydration, heart failure, kidney failure. Viscosity at baseline when hyperviscosity is possible (monoclonal gammopathy, cryoglobulins, markedly high triglycerides). Migraine history (raises aseptic-meningitis risk). IgA: low IgA alone is NOT a contraindication — IVIG is contraindicated only in IgA-deficient patients who have anti-IgA antibodies plus a prior hypersensitivity reaction; routine anti-IgA testing adds little.
    • Premedication: routine premedication NOT required. After a prior reaction: acetaminophen or ibuprofen + diphenhydramine or a non-sedating H1 blocker, with or without hydrocortisone, 1 hour before infusion. Hydration (oral or IV) before infusion helps prevent hypotension, renal injury, and aseptic meningitis — limit volume in heart failure/kidney failure. Keep epinephrine at the bedside for every infusion.
    • Infusion rates (10% product; Gamunex-C CIDP label): 2 mg/kg/min (0.02 mL/kg/min) for the first 30 minutes, then step up as tolerated to a maximum of 8 mg/kg/min (0.08 mL/kg/min). Use the minimum practical rate with kidney or clot risk. Other brands are product-specific (e.g. ASCENIV starts at 0.5 mg/kg/min); products are clinically equivalent for CIDP, and switching brands can relieve side effects. Reactions are most likely during the first infusion of a given product and less likely after 3 treatments with the same product.
    • Monitoring during infusion: vital signs; watch for anaphylaxis, fluid overload, and TRALI (respiratory distress, hypoxaemia, fever 1–6 hours after starting).
    • Monitoring after infusion: creatinine + urine output in at-risk patients; haemoglobin, reticulocytes, LDH, bilirubin, and direct antiglobulin test if haemolysis is suspected — haemolysis typically occurs 5–10 days after a high-dose course; consider routine checks in that window for non-O patients receiving 2 g/kg. Watch clot symptoms and headache/meningism for up to 48 hours. Sodium: distinguish true hyponatraemia from pseudohyponatraemia from high serum protein — restricting water in pseudohyponatraemia worsens viscosity/clot risk. Track efficacy objectively with INCAT, grip strength, and I-RODS.

    IVIG adverse effects — prevention and management¶

    Adverse effect When / who Prevention Management
    Infusion reactions (headache, flushing, chills, myalgia, BP changes) During infusion; 5–15% of infusions; headache was the most common adverse event in ProCID Slow titration, hydration, premedicate after a prior reaction Slow or stop 15–30 min, then restart at the last tolerated rate
    Anaphylaxis / IgA-related Seconds to hours; IgA deficiency with anti-IgA IgE Low-IgA product or SCIg after a significant reaction; epinephrine available Stop infusion; epinephrine, oxygen, antihistamines, IV steroids
    Acute kidney injury Pre-existing CKD, volume depletion, older age, diabetes, sucrose-stabilized products Hydrate, avoid sucrose products, minimum rate, check creatinine first Stop IVIG if kidney function worsens
    Thrombosis (MI, stroke, DVT/PE) Usually at the end of or after infusion; CV risk factors, age >65, hyperviscosity, immobility Hydrate, minimum rate, check viscosity if at risk, consider splitting doses over more days Standard clot management; reassess benefit vs risk
    Haemolysis 5–10 days after; blood group AB/A, dose ≥2 g/kg, first course Know the ABO group, products with reduced isoagglutinins, lower doses/splitting where possible Confirm with labs; supportive care; transfusion if needed
    Aseptic meningitis Hours to 2 days; dose ≥2 g/kg, rapid infusion, migraine history Acetaminophen, hydration, slow rate Lumbar puncture to exclude infection; analgesia; stop IVIG if possible; resolves within days with no lasting effects

    TRICARE — CIDP prescribing

    IVIG, SCIg, and efgartigimod are specialty, prior-authorization-gated therapies in most formularies, and steroids/PLEX carry their own facility and coverage rules — TRICARE tier and PA status for these agents was not verified for this page and is not stated here. Confirm the current preferred product, PA requirements (including step therapy and neurology-specialty requirements), and infusion-site rules before ordering, and verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Disposition¶

    • Admit for rapidly progressive weakness, respiratory/bulbar involvement, severe pain/asymmetry suggesting vasculitis, or a first PLEX course requiring central access and monitoring.
    • Outpatient for stable, confirmed CIDP on maintenance therapy, with neurology follow-up tied to infusion intervals and objective-scale reassessment at each taper decision.
    • Follow-up: reassess INCAT/grip/I-RODS at every maintenance interval change; formal taper attempts every 6–12 months in the first 2–3 years, then every 1–2 years.

    Military medicine — CIDP

    Framework — verify against current AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03, DoDI 1332.18, service deployment/aeromedical policy (and any relevant JTS CPG) before counseling. No paragraph-level retention or deployability threshold for CIDP was verified for this page — do not counsel from this box alone.

    • Deployability: chronic immunoglobulin therapy, infusion dependence, and progressive weakness generally limit deployability; maintenance infusions and cold-chain/logistics do not travel downrange.
    • Profile (DA Form 3349): temporary profile during induction and titration; permanent profile if weakness, sensory loss, or treatment burden imposes lasting functional limits.
    • EPTS / LOD: document onset relative to service entry and any pre-service neuropathy history; CIDP arising in service is typically service-connected for LOD purposes, subject to formal review.
    • Retention (AR 40-501 / DoDI 6130.03): retention review is triggered by persistent functional limitation or ongoing immunosuppressive/infusion therapy that interferes with duty — confirm the current standard before counseling.
    • MEB / IDES: refer when weakness or treatment burden produces lasting duty limitation despite optimal therapy; objective scales (INCAT, grip, I-RODS) and infusion records are the documentation that carries a board.
    • Aeromedical / special duty: flight status and special-duty eligibility require waiver review where weakness, sensory loss, or sedating premedication affects performance; do not assume retention of flight status on maintenance immunotherapy.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    • The 2021 EAN/PNS guideline rebuilt CIDP diagnosis around phenotype + electrodiagnostics and dropped "definite/probable" — a patient is "CIDP" or "possible CIDP," and focal/sensory CIDP can only ever be "possible." The practical consequence is diagnostic discipline: distal CIDP criteria are strict because that phenotype is where misdiagnosis concentrates, and anti-MAG disease, nodopathies, and CISP are now separate diagnoses, not CIDP subtypes.
    • First-line therapy is a three-way tie on efficacy, not a ladder: IVIG, steroids, and PLEX each help roughly 50–80%, and switching between them rescues most "failures" — which is why the guideline insists on an adequate trial of each (including a second IVIG course) before declaring refractory disease.
    • Maintenance is where the field moved: SCIg (PATH) made home maintenance routine, ProCID defined the dose–response curve for IVIG maintenance (0.5 → 1.0 → 2.0 g/kg every 3 weeks), and ADHERE added weekly subcutaneous efgartigimod as a maintenance alternative — with a 61% reduction in relapse risk vs placebo in the randomized-withdrawal phase, but no head-to-head against IVIG.
    • The order set is the safety story: most IVIG harm is preventable — hydration and slow titration for kidney injury and meningitis, ABO awareness for haemolysis at days 5–10, and clot-risk review before every high-dose course.

    Key sources: 2021 EAN/PNS CIDP guideline; ProCID IVIG dose-ranging trial; PATH SCIg trial; ADHERE efgartigimod trial; Gamunex-C and Privigen labeling; Ontario IVIG Infusion Guide.
