    Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)¶

    Last reviewed: August 2026

    One-liner: an antibody attack on presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels that throttles acetylcholine release — proximal weakness, autonomic dysfunction, and hyporeflexia. Half the time it is the first sign of a small-cell lung cancer.

    Who this is for: anyone working up proximal weakness that looks like myasthenia but isn't behaving — and the reason the page exists is that finding LEMS means starting a cancer hunt.

    🚩 LEMS is a paraneoplastic red flag until proven otherwise

    ~50–60% of LEMS is paraneoplastic, and the tumor is almost always small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). The neurologic syndrome frequently precedes the cancer diagnosis by months to years — so a new LEMS diagnosis is a mandate to search, and to keep searching if the first scan is clean.

    • Screen with thoracic CT and FDG-PET/CT — not PET only if CT is negative. CT sensitivity falls for central and mediastinal tumors, where PET was positive in 100% of cases against 50% for CT. (CT-first-then-PET is a reasonable resource-constrained fallback; know that you are making that trade.) Chest radiograph is not a screening test — 51% sensitivity vs 83% for CT at first screen.
    • Repeat imaging every 4–6 months for 2 years if the initial search is negative (2021 PNS-Care criteria for high-risk phenotypes) — the tumor may not yet be radiologically visible.
    • Two years is the right stopping point: 91–92% of SCLCs are found within 3 months of the LEMS diagnosis and 96% within 1 year. Detections beyond 2 years occurred almost entirely in patients who had been screened inadequately.
    • The strongest single risk factor is smoking. A smoker with LEMS has SCLC until proven otherwise.

    The triad that separates it from MG¶

    Feature LEMS Myasthenia gravis
    Weakness pattern Proximal, legs > arms; gait first Ocular/bulbar first; fatigable
    On brief exercise Transiently improves (post-exercise facilitation) Worsens
    Reflexes Reduced or absent — and may return after exercise Normal
    Autonomic features Prominent — dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, constipation, orthostasis Absent
    Ocular involvement Mild or late Common, early
    Antibody P/Q-type VGCC (~90%; near 100% in SCLC-associated disease) AChR / MuSK
    Cancer link SCLC in ~50–60% Thymoma in 10–15%

    The counterintuitive sign: strength and reflexes that improve after a few seconds of exercise (facilitation) point to LEMS, not MG. Dry mouth is the most consistent autonomic clue and is easy to ask about.

    Diagnosis¶

    • Serology — P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channel (VGCC) antibody, positive in ~90% (range 85–95%; approaching 100% in SCLC-associated disease, ~91% in non-cancer LEMS).
      • Do not order N-type VGCC antibody — it lacks diagnostic value in LEMS.
      • P/Q-type positivity does not distinguish paraneoplastic from non-paraneoplastic disease and has no role in cancer risk stratification. SOX1 antibody does — 95% specific but only 65% sensitive for SCLC-LEMS, so a positive result is informative and a negative one is not.
    • Electrodiagnostics are characteristic and confirmatory:
      • Low baseline CMAP amplitude.
      • Decrement on low-frequency (2–5 Hz, usually 3 Hz) RNS, ≥10% — like MG, present in 94–98% but not specific.
      • But marked facilitation — CMAP amplitude increase ≥60% after 10 seconds of maximal exercise or after 20 Hz stimulation. Use ≥60% as the operating threshold (97% sensitive, 99% specific); the older ≥100% cut-off is more specific but misses 4–16%. This post-exercise facilitation is the signature and distinguishes it from MG.
      • High-frequency (20–50 Hz) stimulation works but is very painful — avoid it when post-exercise testing is feasible.
      • A subtler discriminator: on low-frequency RNS the decrement nadir falls at the 7th–10th stimulus in LEMS vs the 4th–5th in MG (cut-off 6.5, AUC 0.90).

    • The DELTA-P score stratifies SCLC risk and guides how aggressively to hunt — 1 point each, maximum 6, assessed at onset or within 3 months:

      Component
      Age at onset ≥50 years 1
      Smoking at diagnosis 1
      Weight loss ≥5% 1
      Bulbar involvement 1
      Erectile dysfunction (males) 1
      Karnofsky performance status <70 1

      0–1 → 0–2.6% probability of SCLC; 4–6 → 83.9–100%. Prospective validation AUC 82.5% (the 94% figure comes from the derivation cohort and overstates real-world performance). The score shortens the surveillance interval — it never cancels the search. Every LEMS patient is screened regardless of score.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Recognize the triad — proximal weakness + autonomic features + hyporeflexia, with facilitation.
    2. Confirm — VGCC antibody + EDx showing post-exercise facilitation.
    3. Hunt for cancer — this is the priority. CT chest and PET/CT → serial imaging every 4–6 months for 2 years. Score DELTA-P to set the interval, not to decide whether to look.
    4. Treat symptoms — amifampridine, divided 3–5× daily, titrated toward 50–80 mg/day.
    5. Treat the cause — the tumor (paraneoplastic) or immunotherapy (autoimmune/non-tumor).
    6. Military disposition — and note that a paraneoplastic LEMS is a cancer diagnosis first.

    Treatment¶

    Step 1 — symptomatic: amifampridine

    Amifampridine (3,4-diaminopyridine; Firdapse) — the FDA-approved first-line symptomatic therapy (2018; label covers adults and children ≥6 years). A potassium-channel blocker that prolongs the presynaptic action potential, increasing calcium influx and ACh release.

    Adults / ≥45 kg Children <45 kg
    Start 15–30 mg/day 5–15 mg/day
    Titrate +5 mg/day every 3–4 days +2.5 mg/day every 3–4 days
    Max single dose 20 mg 10 mg
    Max daily 100 mg (US label) 50 mg
    • Divided dosing is mandatory, not stylistic — 3–5 times daily. The half-life is 1.8–2.5 hours (Tmax 20 min–1 h), so twice-daily dosing produces symptomatic troughs. Patients feel each dose within the hour.
    • The ceiling you should actually work to is 80 mg/day. The US label maximum is 100 mg/day, but 80 mg/day is both the EMA ceiling and the widely adopted expert cap on seizure-risk grounds — these are two different numbers, not a range. Real-world dosing sits well below either: mean 50.5 mg/day in the pivotal run-in and 66 mg/day long-term. Target 50–80 mg/day divided 3–4×.
    • Lower the starting dose in renal impairment (CLcr 15–90), any hepatic impairment, age ≥65, and — most importantly — NAT2 poor metabolizers. No dosing recommendation exists for ESRD or dialysis; it has not been studied.
    • NAT2 status matters more than renal function, and it is not a rare edge case. Poor acetylators have 3.5–4.5× the Cmax and 5.6–9× the AUC of rapid acetylators. Prevalence is 40–60% in White and African American populations, 10–30% in Japanese, Chinese and Korean populations. Start low.
    • Seizures occur in ~2% of patients without prior history and are dose-dependent; a prior seizure is an absolute contraindication, as is aminopyridine hypersensitivity. The reported seizures clustered at 100 mg/day, in a patient with brain metastases, and in one on high-dose theophylline — concomitant threshold-lowering drugs matter more than the absolute dose. A dedicated QTc study found no clinically relevant prolongation, so routine ECG monitoring is not required.
    • Expect paresthesia — it is the commonest adverse effect at 62%. Perioral and digital, starting within minutes of a ≥10 mg dose and lasting 5–10 minutes. Warn the patient in advance; most come to treat it as a marker that the dose has landed rather than a reason to stop. Also >10%: URI 33%; abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhea, headache, elevated LFTs and back pain ~14% each.
    • In children, watch weight — clinically significant weight loss occurred at doses ≥60 mg/day.

    Pyridostigmine — not a substitute, but worth a trial as an add-on

    The page used to say it "adds little," which is stronger than the evidence supports. The only randomized trial was intravenous, single-dose, and enrolled 9 patients (pyridostigmine alone was no better than placebo, and adding it to IV 3,4-DAP conferred no extra benefit). Chronic oral co-administration has never been randomized, and the observational signal runs the other way: 68% of a long-term cohort used it and 67% reported subjective benefit, and 13 of 25 patients in an open-label extension improved further when it was added.

    Reasonable position: amifampridine first and always; a trial of oral pyridostigmine is justified in incomplete responders, where the risk is low and the potential benefit real.

    Step 2 — treat the underlying driver

    Paraneoplastic LEMS: treat the SCLC. Cancer treatment improves the neurologic syndrome — 7 of 11 patients surviving >2 months after tumor therapy improved substantially, one to complete remission at 7 years, though in 3 the improvement was transient. A pretreatment CMAP amplitude >3.0 mV predicts a better neurologic outcome. Expect 6–12 months, not weeks.

    Non-tumor (autoimmune) LEMS: immunotherapy. Be honest with yourself about the evidence — no randomized trial exists for any oral immunosuppressant, or for plasma exchange, in LEMS.

    • Prednisone + azathioprine — first-line immunosuppression, open-label evidence only. Needed in 70% of non-tumor LEMS vs 44% of SCLC-LEMS. The regimen behind the best-reported results is prednisolone 60–100 mg on alternate days plus azathioprine 2.5 mg/kg/day (near-complete remission in 3 of 6 non-cancer patients within 6–12 months). Check TPMT and NUDT15 before azathioprine, and monitor CBC — see the MG page for the full monitoring schedule and the allopurinol interaction. Mycophenolate mofetil 1,000 mg PO BID is a reasonable substitute if azathioprine is not tolerated, but has no LEMS-specific data at all — and carries a boxed warning for embryofetal toxicity.
    • IVIG 2 g/kg over 2 days — the one crossover RCT (n=9) showed significant limb, bulbar and respiratory gains with a fall in VGCC titre; peak at 2–4 weeks, declining by 8. An acute or bridging therapy, not maintenance.
    • Plasma exchange — open-label only; 8 of 9 responded, peak at 10–15 days. For acute severe or rapidly progressive weakness.
    • Rituximab — fourth-line, for refractory disease, on case-level evidence only. No LEMS-specific protocol exists; the borrowed MG regimens are 375 mg/m² IV weekly × 4 or 1 g IV × 2 doses two weeks apart. Off-label. Screen HBsAg and anti-HBc before the first dose — boxed warning.
    • Give it time. Treatment effect on functional scales is generally reached at 6–12 months; escalating before then is usually premature. Treatment is typically indefinite, but sustained remission does occur — 5 of 63 patients stopped everything at a median of 4 years and stayed in remission for a median of 12 — so a cautious withdrawal trial is reasonable in durable remission.

    Do not extrapolate the myasthenia biologics to LEMS

    None of the agents that reorganized MG care has been trialed in LEMS. For complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan) this is a mechanistic mismatch, not merely a gap in the evidence — LEMS neuromuscular junctions do not show complement deposits, LEMS IgG works just as well in C5-deficient mice, and complement-incapable F(ab')₂ fragments transfer the phenotype. The mechanism is cross-linking and depletion of calcium channels, not membrane attack complex. Complement inhibition should not be expected to work here.

    FcRn inhibitors have a better theoretical rationale, since LEMS is IgG-mediated — but remain untested in LEMS. Anti-CD19 CAR T has two case reports in MG/LEMS overlap: striking (wheelchair to full mobility in 2 months) and investigational, n=2.

    Two exposures that catch LEMS patients out

    • Anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade. LEMS patients are markedly sensitive to neuromuscular blocking drugs. In a series of 37 patients across 60 operations, 4 (11%) had respiratory complications — and 3 of those were undiagnosed or untreated at the time. The risk concentrates in the patient nobody knew had LEMS. Tell anesthesia before any procedure, especially before thoracic surgery in a paraneoplastic case.
    • Immune checkpoint inhibitors can worsen pre-existing LEMS and have been reported to trigger de novo LEMS in neurologically asymptomatic SCLC patients. The causal evidence is graded low and rests largely on case reports — treat it as grounds for vigilance, not an established causal link. It matters increasingly, because checkpoint inhibitors are standard in exactly this population.

    Same NMJ-drug cautions as MG

    LEMS shares MG's sensitivity to neuromuscular-blocking drugs — aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, IV magnesium, and neuromuscular blockers can all worsen it. If a paraneoplastic patient is heading for thoracic surgery or chemotherapy, flag the NMJ sensitivity to the anesthesia and oncology teams. See the MG precipitant list.

    Military medicine — LEMS

    Framework — verify against current AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 and Vol 2, DoDI 1332.18, and service aeromedical policy. See Profiles, MEB/MAR2 & deployability.

    LEMS is named in no military medical standard. Every citation below is a reasoned choice among analogies — cite them that way in a memo, and say so.

    • Retention (non-paraneoplastic) — the Army hook is 3-31k, not a bare "3-31." AR 40-501 para 3-31k, "Other neurologic conditions," is the only subparagraph that reaches an unnamed condition: "…there remain residual symptoms and impairments such as … weakness, paralysis, or atrophy of important muscle groups … as to meet the definition of a disqualifying medical condition … as in paragraph 3-1." Proximal girdle weakness is that, on the face of the text.
      • Do not cite 3-31b (all primary muscle disorders — LEMS is a presynaptic junctionopathy, not a myopathy) or 3-31c (myasthenia gravis; its extraocular carve-out does not transfer).
      • Because 3-31k closes on para 3-1, name the limbs actually met: 3-1b (medication requiring frequent physician monitoring), 3-1d (dependence on medication, frequent special treatments, or frequent clinical monitoring — amifampridine dependence, immunotherapy, 2-year serial cancer surveillance), and 3-1a (duty interference substantiated by the commander).
    • DoD floor — lead with 5.26.e, and do not use 5.26.i. DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.26.e, "Neuromuscular disorders and muscular dystrophy including, but not limited to…" — the enumerated examples are dystrophies, but the lead-in is expressly non-exhaustive and LEMS is by definition a neuromuscular disorder.
      • 5.26.i ("peripheral neuropathy or paralytic disorders resulting in permanent functional impairment") works against you. It is the only 5.26 subparagraph carrying its own permanence qualifier on top of the 5.26.a gate, so citing it raises the bar and invites the reply that a Soldier responding to amifampridine has no permanent impairment. Use it only as a secondary citation where permanent impairment is documented — and never describe LEMS as a peripheral neuropathy.
      • 5.26.n — "Recurrent, neurogenic, or unexplained syncope or near syncope that interferes with duty" — is a genuinely named standard the dysautonomia engages directly, without analogy. Cite it when the autonomic burden is documented.
    • LEMS is not an "upon diagnosis" condition — 5.26.a gates it. Only 5.26.d (dementia) and 5.26.k (epilepsy) carry "Paragraph 5.26.a. does not apply." Document both prongs: the condition persists despite appropriate treatment (name drug, dose, duration — amifampridine titrated to maximum tolerated, ± immunotherapy) and it impairs function so as to preclude satisfactory performance of duty. A well-controlled Soldier has not met the neurologic standard on the neuro hook alone; the case is then carried by 3-1b/3-1d, by 5.26.n if there is near-syncope, or by the malignancy standard.
    • Paraneoplastic LEMS — cite the malignancy standard by paragraph, and lead with it. AR 40-501 para 3-34a: "Malignant neoplasms that are unresponsive to therapy. Or when therapy is such as to require prolonged, intensive medical supervision, or when the residuals of treatment themselves meet the definition… as in paragraph 3-1." SCLC therapy meets the second limb on its face — you do not wait for the cancer to fail treatment, which is the point most memos miss. 3-34c covers an inadequate post-treatment observation period. DoD floor: DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.29 (its own persist-despite-treatment gate in the lead-in; 5.29.a surveillance, 5.29.b residuals).
      • Cite both standards in the same memo. The malignancy paragraphs carry the case; the neurologic paragraphs document the residual deficit that persists after the tumour is treated — which in LEMS is common, and is exactly the "residuals" language in 3-34a and 5.29.b.
    • EPTS / LOD — onset-dating is the contested question here. Symptoms commonly predate diagnosis by months to years (proximal leg weakness written off as deconditioning, dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, constipation). Date the first attributable symptom, not the serology. Say what you reviewed — pre-accession records, prior PHAs, ACFT trend, sick-call notes for "leg weakness." AR 40-501 3-2, Note: accession standards govern the first 6 months of AD (separation via EPSBD); after 6 months, chapter 3 governs. Accession counterparts: DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 6.26.q (history of chronic nervous system disorders — non-exhaustive) and 6.26.n (history of paralysis, weakness, lack of coordination), plus 6.29.b (history of malignancy) in a paraneoplastic case. Check 3-3c before referring: a condition waived at accession, essentially unchanged and not meeting para 3-1, is not a DES referral. If diagnosed in service, state LOD affirmatively — a missing LOD is not excludable time.
    • Profile (DA Form 3349). LEMS profiles on the P factor; add L/U only where documented girdle weakness limits extremity function (E is rarely engaged — ocular involvement is mild or late).
      • Temporary through diagnostic work-up, amifampridine titration, immunotherapy induction, and — if paraneoplastic — staging and oncologic treatment. ≤90 days per instrument, and link every extension so the days-on-profile count is real.
      • Convert to permanent at clinical MRDP or the 12-month administrative MRDP, whichever comes first. Generalized disease at MRDP → permanent P-3, which is the DES referral (AR 40-501 3-4).
      • Section 4 limits, written functionally: no sustained load-carriage or road march; no repeated stair or ladder climbing; no sustained overhead work; no duty requiring rapid unaided rise from prone; no prolonged standing in formation (orthostasis); no unrestricted work in high heat; no duty requiring reliable independent egress under load.
    • MEB / MAR2 — run the fork, do not default to "refer." Refer to the DES when MRDP is reached and the Soldier fails retention standards under 3-31k → 3-1 (or 3-34 if paraneoplastic). If the Soldier meets retention standards on well-controlled disease but cannot perform their PMOS, that is a MAR2 — an administrative review, not a disability process — and sending it to DES gets the packet bounced. Do not refer before MRDP. The permanent 3/4 profile in e-Profile is the referral; the approval authority's second signature starts the clock.
      • Document: first attributable symptom date, VGCC serostatus, EDx (baseline CMAP, decrement, post-exercise facilitation), amifampridine dose and response, immunotherapy by drug/dose/duration, autonomic burden, objective strength and timed measures, the cancer-surveillance schedule and findings, and commander-corroborated duty impact.
      • Whether the Soldier is found fit is the PEB's decision, not yours — write the standard and the evidence, not the outcome. Put the oncologist's staging and prognosis in the packet as evidence, not in the profile as a prediction.
    • Deployability — what actually fails forward:
      • NMBA hypersensitivity is the deployment-defining hazard. A Soldier needing emergency anaesthesia forward — trauma laparotomy, appendicectomy, airway — risks prolonged post-operative ventilation at a Role 2 with no ICU and no spare ventilator. Carry it on the medical alert documentation, not just the neurology note.
      • Amifampridine has no theatre supply line. Orphan drug, specialty-pharmacy distribution, not on any theatre formulary, not locally resupplyable, not substitutable — and dosed in multiple divided doses across the day, so a delayed shipment converts to weakness within days.
      • It lowers the seizure threshold, which interacts badly with heat, sleep deprivation, exertional hyponatraemia, and other threshold-lowering agents in the deployed formulary.
      • Autonomic failure is operational, not merely clinical — orthostasis in formation, under load, and in armour; impaired sweating in a heat-injury environment.
      • On prednisone plus a steroid-sparing agent, live vaccines required for theatre entry are contraindicated — a theatre-entry blocker independent of disease control.
      • Rescue therapy is unavailable downrange — IVIG needs continuous 2–8 °C cold chain; PLEX does not exist below Role 3.
      • Paraneoplastic cases are non-deployable on the cancer alone — active SCLC, treatment cycles, and 4–6 monthly imaging for 2 years are, in the words of 3-34a, "prolonged, intensive medical supervision."
      • Field/formulary drugs that worsen NMJ transmission — aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, IV magnesium — constrain deployed medical support.
    • Aeromedical: disqualifying — by import, and categorically if paraneoplastic. AR 40-501 Chapter 4 never names LEMS. Para 4-27 (Neurological) makes the flying-duty standard "the causes in the accession standards plus the following" → DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 6.26.q and 6.26.n, neither with a carve-out. In a paraneoplastic case the answer is categorical and independent of the neurology: AR 40-501 para 4-30b, "History of any malignant tumor" — a history of SCLC ends flying-duty qualification on its own terms, whether or not the LEMS improves. Waivers run through USAAMA and the applicable Aeromedical Policy Letters (4-1c); amifampridine is not an approved aviation medication and a seizure-threshold-lowering agent will not be treated as one. Non-Army Services follow their own policy — confirm before counselling.

    TRICARE — LEMS prescribing

    Amifampridine (Firdapse) is an orphan specialty drug — assume it is prior-authorisation-gated. Expect the PA to require a documented LEMS diagnosis (VGCC seropositivity and/or the characteristic electrodiagnostic study), a neurologist prescriber, and dispensing through the specialty / home-delivery channel rather than a retail counter. Start the authorisation the day you make the diagnosis — this is not a drug an MTF pharmacy keeps on the shelf.

    Know which benefit you are billing — LEMS treatment spans both sides. Oral amifampridine runs through the pharmacy benefit (Express Scripts; an approval applies at military pharmacies, network pharmacies, and home delivery). IVIG and plasma exchange run through the medical benefit, at an infusion or apheresis site, under a different authorisation path. That split decides who authorises, who pays, and where the patient is treated — and it is the commonest reason a LEMS treatment plan stalls.

    Verify current tier and criteria at the TRICARE Formulary Search before you counsel a patient — DoD P&T revises formulary status and PA criteria without notice. Firdapse's actual TRICARE tier and PA criteria text could not be confirmed from public sources for this page; treat the expectations above as expectations, not as TRICARE rules.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Presynaptic, not postsynaptic — and that is the whole story. In LEMS, antibodies against P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels on the motor nerve terminal reduce the calcium influx that triggers acetylcholine vesicle release. Fewer quanta are released per impulse, so the first contraction is weak — but a burst of activity transiently loads the terminal with calcium, briefly increasing release and producing the facilitation that defines the syndrome on exam and on EMG. The same channels sit on autonomic terminals, which is why dry mouth and orthostasis travel with the weakness. It is the mirror image of myasthenia gravis, where the defect is postsynaptic receptor loss and repeated activity only worsens transmission.

    The tumor connection is mechanistic. Small-cell lung cancer expresses functional voltage-gated calcium channels, and the anti-tumor immune response cross-reacts with the identical channels at the neuromuscular junction. LEMS is, in a real sense, the immune system finding the cancer before the radiologist does — which is exactly why the neurologic diagnosis obligates the search.

    And that immune response appears to help. SCLC with LEMS carries substantially better survival than SCLC alone — median 17 vs 7 months in the Dutch registry and 18 vs 9.5 in a prospective UK cohort, with 5-year survival 21% vs 4.4%. The advantage persists in extensive disease and survives adjustment for stage, age, sex, performance status and sodium, so it is not simply lead-time bias from earlier detection. Tellingly, SCLC patients with low-titre VGCC antibodies but no clinical LEMS get no survival benefit — it appears to be the functionally pathogenic antibody response that matters. NCCN states plainly that the presence of LEMS does not imply metastatic or incurable disease. Counsel the patient accordingly; a paraneoplastic diagnosis here is not the worst version of an SCLC diagnosis.

    But do not use the LEMS as a tumor monitor. This is the counterintuitive correction to a tempting assumption. Of 9 patients followed through tumor recurrence, only 2 had simultaneous worsening of their LEMS, and in both the link was questionable. The syndrome frequently does not flare when the cancer comes back. Oncologic surveillance runs on its own schedule; neurologic stability is not reassurance.

    Key evidence:

    • Firdapse (amifampridine) FDA approval, 2018, expanded to children ≥6 years — the only amifampridine product now marketed in the US.
    • P/Q-type VGCC antibody — the confirmatory serology (~90%); SOX1 for cancer risk.
    • DELTA-P score — stratifies SCLC risk at diagnosis (prospective AUC 82.5%).
    • 2021 PNS-Care criteria — 4–6 monthly surveillance for 2 years in high-risk phenotypes.
    • Wirtz 2009 — the only randomized pyridostigmine trial (IV, n=9), and the reason the oral-add-on question is still open.
    • Amifampridine failed in myasthenia gravis (2026) — a negative randomized crossover trial of amifampridine added to pyridostigmine in AChR-positive MG. The presynaptic drug does not transfer to the postsynaptic disease, which is a neat confirmation of the mechanism.

Cancer surveillance updated to every 4–6 months for 2 years per the 2021 PNS-Care criteria (the former 3–6 months derived from the 2011 Titulaer recommendation), and screening changed to CT and FDG-PET/CT rather than PET only if CT is negative. The CMAP-facilitation threshold is resolved to ≥60% (97% sensitive, 99% specific). The pyridostigmine position was softened because the only randomized trial was intravenous, single-dose, n=9 while observational cohorts report ~67% subjective benefit from oral add-on. Two findings that reverse common assumptions are now stated explicitly: LEMS does not reliably flare when the tumour recurs, so it cannot serve as a recurrence sentinel; and SCLC with LEMS carries materially better survival than SCLC alone.**
