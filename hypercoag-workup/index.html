    Hypercoagulable workup¶

    Last reviewed: November 2026

    One-liner: For the on-call neuro, ED, or clinic clinician seeing a young adult with unprovoked VTE, arterial thrombosis, or unusual-site thrombosis — which thrombophilia tests to order, which to skip (MTHFR is out), when to send them, and when the result actually changes management.

    🚩 Red flags — don't let the workup delay the treatment

    Pattern Worry Do now
    Arterial thrombosis (stroke, MI, limb ischemia) in a patient <50 y with no atherosclerotic explanation Antiphospholipid syndrome Anticoagulate; send the full APS panel (LA + aCL + anti-β2GPI) — do not order the inherited panel for an arterial event
    Cerebral venous thrombosis or splanchnic-vein thrombosis APS, JAK2-positive MPN, PNH Venous imaging now (MRV/CTV for CVT; contrast CT for splanchnic); anticoagulate — see Cerebral venous thrombosis
    Recurrent VTE, or VTE + thrombocytopenia / livedo racemosa / valve disease APS (often triple-positive) Anticoagulate; send the APS panel; do not start a DOAC until APS is excluded — triple-positive APS needs warfarin
    Thrombosis + cytopenias, hemolysis, or unexplained erythrocytosis/thrombocytosis PNH, JAK2-positive myeloproliferative neoplasm CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes; JAK2 V617F; PNH flow cytometry — hematology
    Any acute thrombosis The workup itself Anticoagulate first. Do not delay treatment to draw the panel — DNA and antibody tests can be sent anytime; functional tests (protein C/S, antithrombin, lupus anticoagulant) are unreliable acutely and on anticoagulation, so they wait

    Diagnosis / classification¶

    "Thrombophilia" here means two buckets — inherited and acquired. The inherited defects are factor V Leiden (FVL), prothrombin G20210A, and antithrombin, protein C, and protein S deficiency. The acquired one that matters is antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Inherited thrombophilias are principally venous — their association with arterial stroke, MI, and peripheral arterial thrombosis is weak and inconsistent, so do not order them for arterial events.

    The valid panel — order these, and only these¶

    Test What it is Notes
    Antithrombin activity Physiologic anticoagulant deficiency Functional — send later, off anticoagulation (see timing)
    Protein C activity Physiologic anticoagulant deficiency Functional — send later; warfarin falsely lowers it
    Protein S (free antigen preferred) Physiologic anticoagulant deficiency Functional — send later; warfarin falsely lowers it; pregnancy lowers activity
    Factor V Leiden (PCR) DNA test Valid anytime, on or off anticoagulation
    Prothrombin G20210A (PCR) DNA test Valid anytime, on or off anticoagulation
    Lupus anticoagulant (LA) APS — functional/clot-based Most anticoagulant-sensitive test in the panel; send off anticoagulation if possible
    Anticardiolipin IgG/IgM (aCL) APS — ELISA Generally valid anytime
    Anti-β2-glycoprotein-I IgG/IgM (anti-β2GPI) APS — ELISA Generally valid anytime

    Antiphospholipid syndrome — 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria¶

    These are research classification criteria, not diagnostic criteria — a patient can have APS clinically without meeting the score. Validation: specificity 99% vs 86% for the 2006 revised Sapporo criteria; sensitivity 84% vs 99%.

    Entry criterion: at least 1 documented APS-associated clinical criterion and at least 1 positive aPL test (LA, or moderate/high aCL, or moderate/high anti-β2GPI, IgG or IgM), with the positive laboratory test occurring within 3 years of the clinical criterion.

    Classification requires ≥3 clinical points AND ≥3 laboratory points — you cannot compensate for an inadequate clinical score with extra laboratory points, or vice versa. Within each domain, only the highest-weighted item counts. Do not score a manifestation if an equally or more likely alternative explanation exists.

    Clinical domains:

    Domain Criterion Points
    1. Macrovascular VTE VTE with a high-risk VTE profile (e.g., major provoking factor) 1
    VTE without a high-risk VTE profile 3
    2. Macrovascular arterial thrombosis Arterial thrombosis with a high-risk cardiovascular profile 2
    Arterial thrombosis without a high-risk cardiovascular profile 4
    3. Microvascular Suspected: livedo racemosa, livedoid vasculopathy, aPL nephropathy, or pulmonary hemorrhage (exam/imaging) 2
    Established: same findings confirmed by biopsy or definitive testing, plus myocardial disease or adrenal hemorrhage 5
    4. Obstetric ≥3 consecutive pre-fetal losses <10 wk and/or early fetal deaths 10 wk–15 wk 6 d 1
    Fetal death 16 wk–33 wk 6 d without severe preeclampsia or placental insufficiency 1
    Severe preeclampsia or severe placental insufficiency <34 wk, ± fetal death 3
    Severe preeclampsia and severe placental insufficiency <34 wk, ± fetal death 4
    5. Cardiac valve Otherwise unexplained valve thickening 2
    Otherwise unexplained valve vegetation 4
    6. Hematologic Otherwise unexplained thrombocytopenia, lowest-ever platelet count 20–130 ×10⁹/L 2

    Laboratory domains:

    Laboratory finding Points
    Lupus anticoagulant positive once 1
    Persistent lupus anticoagulant 5
    Persistent moderate/high IgM aCL and/or anti-β2GPI 1
    Persistent moderate IgG aCL and/or anti-β2GPI 4
    Persistent high IgG aCL or anti-β2GPI 5
    Persistent high IgG aCL and high IgG anti-β2GPI 7

    Titer thresholds (standardized ELISA): moderate is 40–79 units; high is ≥80 units. The old ">99th percentile" cutoff is gone for classification. Low titers, IgA, and non-criteria antibodies (anti-phosphatidylserine/prothrombin, anti-domain-I β2GPI) do not score. IgG and IgM are not added together — a patient whose only positive test is IgM aCL/anti-β2GPI can reach at most 1 laboratory point and cannot be classified. Chemiluminescence and other automated assays have no validated moderate/high equivalents yet — do not transfer the ELISA cutoffs to them (2024/2025 ISTH laboratory guidance uses the assay-specific local cutoff, usually >99th percentile).

    Persistence: two consecutive positive tests ≥12 weeks apart. If one serial result is moderate and the other high, score it moderate unless two consecutive high results are available. A single LA result can earn 1 point when repeat testing is not available.

    Triple positivity = persistent positivity for LA + aCL + anti-β2GPI (studies applying the 2023 thresholds generally require IgG/IgM ≥40 units for both solid-phase tests). It is the strongest serologic predictor of thrombotic and obstetric events. Isotype matters: IgG-driven triple positivity carries much more risk than IgM-only — in one cohort, about half of IgM-only triple-positive patients had no clinical involvement. There is no separate "triple-positive = X points" row: persistent LA scores 5 points and high IgG on both solid-phase antibodies scores 7, in their respective domains.

    What no longer scores: early pregnancy losses on their own, VTE in a patient with a high-risk VTE profile on its own, and isolated IgM. A "B-level" VTE combined with an obstetric item also does not reach the clinical threshold.

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Anticoagulate now. Never delay treatment to draw the panel.
    2. Ask the one question that decides whether to test at all: "Will a positive or negative result change something I am actually going to do?" If the answer is no, don't test.
    3. Send the anytime tests now (DNA + antibody immunoassays). Defer the functional tests (protein C, protein S, antithrombin, lupus anticoagulant) until the acute phase has passed and anticoagulation is off or bridged.
    4. Act on the result that matters most: APS. A positive inherited test after a first unprovoked VTE usually changes nothing; APS changes the drug.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Anticoagulate for the event itself — primary treatment is generally 3–6 months; the duration decision comes after. Do not wait for any test result.
    2. Decide test vs no-test by scenario (table below). Default: do not test after a first unprovoked VTE or a surgery-provoked VTE. Test when the default is to stop anticoagulation and a positive result would extend it.
    3. Send Step 1 tests now (DNA + aCL/anti-β2GPI — valid anytime, even anticoagulated).
    4. Send Step 2 tests later (protein C, protein S, antithrombin, lupus anticoagulant) — ≥3 months into treatment, off interfering anticoagulants, with the washouts in the table below. Repeat any low result off confounders before labeling a hereditary deficiency.
    5. Interpret and act: APS (especially triple-positive) → warfarin, INR 2–3; avoid DOACs. Positive inherited thrombophilia after a first unprovoked VTE → usually no change (indefinite anticoagulation was already favored). Provoked VTE (nonsurgical major transient / pregnancy / postpartum / COC) with a positive test → continue indefinitely. Negative panel → do not use it as reassurance to stop after an otherwise unprovoked VTE.
    6. Family planning and relatives: test for a known familial high-risk defect (antithrombin, protein C, or protein S deficiency) when it would change contraception, HRT, or pregnancy prophylaxis. Do not screen the general population before COCs.
    7. Disposition and follow-up: driven by the thrombotic event, not the panel. Hematology referral for APS, triple positivity, unusual-site thrombosis, or a confirmed deficiency. Confirm APS persistence at ≥12 weeks.
    Scenario Test to guide duration? ASH 2023 approach
    First unprovoked DVT/PE No Do not test to determine duration (Rec 1 — conditional, very-low-certainty)
    Surgery-provoked VTE No Do not test to determine duration
    VTE with a nonsurgical major transient risk factor (e.g., ≥3 days bed rest with acute illness) Yes Test; if thrombophilia found, continue indefinitely, else stop (Recs 3–5)
    Pregnancy- or postpartum-associated VTE Yes Test; same approach as above
    Combined-oral-contraceptive-associated VTE Yes Test; same approach as above
    CVT or splanchnic thrombosis, and you plan to stop anticoagulation Yes Test; if thrombophilia found, continue indefinitely (Recs 7, 9)
    CVT or splanchnic thrombosis, already planned for indefinite anticoagulation No Do not test solely for duration (Recs 8, 10)
    Known familial AT/PC/PS deficiency (patient or relative) Yes, selective Test for the known defect — can change prophylaxis / contraception / HRT decisions
    General population before starting COCs No Do not screen

    Workup¶

    Step 1 — send now (valid anytime, even anticoagulated)

    • Factor V Leiden PCR — DNA test; unaffected by acute thrombosis, heparin, warfarin, or DOACs.
    • Prothrombin G20210A PCR — DNA test; same.
    • Anticardiolipin IgG and IgM — ELISA; generally valid anytime (transient positivity possible with acute illness — confirm persistence at ≥12 weeks).
    • Anti-β2-glycoprotein-I IgG and IgM — ELISA; same.
    • Lupus anticoagulant — defer if possible. LA is the most anticoagulant-sensitive test in the panel: UFH/LMWH, warfarin, and DOACs all distort dRVVT/aPTT assays (false positives and negatives). If it must be sent on anticoagulation, the result needs review/validation by an expert laboratory.
    • → If Step 1 is normal and the plan is to stop anticoagulation, stop the drug, wait out the washout, then send Step 2. Two-stage approach: DNA + aCL/anti-β2GPI on anticoagulation, then LA + protein C/S + antithrombin off it.

    Step 2 — send later (functional tests, off interfering anticoagulants)

    • Timing: protein C, protein S, and antithrombin no sooner than ≥3 months into anticoagulation for the acute thrombosis (BSH Grade 2B). An alternative is to test 2–4 weeks off anticoagulation, which fits the usual timing of a D-dimer for stop/continue decisions.
    • Protein C activity — chromogenic activity assays are less DOAC-susceptible than clot-based assays; clot-based PC can be falsely increased by DOACs (masking deficiency). Warfarin falsely lowers PC.
    • Protein S, free antigen preferred — C4b-binding protein rises acutely, lowering free protein S; warfarin falsely lowers PS; activity assays are DOAC-affected (anti-Xa drugs gave falsely high PS in one study).
    • Antithrombin activity — heparin lowers AT activity/antigen; acute thrombosis consumes AT; anti-Xa drugs gave falsely low AT in one study (assay-dependent), while factor Xa inhibitors can falsely increase AT in Xa-based assays and dabigatran can falsely increase AT in thrombin/IIa-based assays.
    • Lupus anticoagulant — repeat to confirm persistence (≥12 weeks apart). For LMWH, draw ≥12 h after the last dose, as close as possible to the next dose. Do not test on therapeutic UFH unless the lab has validated heparin-neutralizing methodology.
    • Washouts before testing (Anticoagulation Forum): warfarin ≥2 weeks (if PC/PS is needed while anticoagulation must continue, switch to heparin/LMWH for ≥2 weeks first); DOACs ≥48 hours, longer with renal impairment (Connors: ≥5 half-lives, usually 2–3 days or more); heparin/LMWH ≥24 hours; fondaparinux ≥48 hours, longer with renal impairment.
    • Confirm before labeling: repeat any low protein C, protein S, or antithrombin on a separate sample off confounders. Rule out acquired causes first — liver disease, DIC, sepsis, vitamin K deficiency. Do not interrupt clinically necessary anticoagulation solely to obtain a panel.

    Step 3 — unusual-site thrombosis extras (when CVT or splanchnic thrombosis, or cytopenias/hemolysis)

    • JAK2 V617F — with unexplained erythrocytosis, thrombocytosis, or leukocytosis, or splanchnic thrombosis. The ASH thrombophilia panel does not cover JAK2-positive MPN — order it separately.
    • PNH flow cytometry — with cytopenias, hemolysis, or unusual-site thrombosis.
    • CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes — screen for the cytopenia/hemolysis pattern that points at PNH or an MPN.
    • → Hematology referral. BSH does not recommend heritable testing for unusual site alone — it does recommend aPL testing, JAK2 or an MPN panel, and PNH testing when cytopenias or hemolysis are present.

    aCL, anti-β2GPI (ELISA) Not affected Not affected Not affected Not affected

    Protein S (free antigen preferred) Can be falsely low Usually not affected Falsely low Activity assays affected; anti-Xa drugs gave falsely high PS in one study

    Memory aid: DNA tests anytime; aCL/β2GPI usually anytime; protein C/S/antithrombin later and off interfering anticoagulants; lupus anticoagulant preferably later and off essentially all anticoagulants. If recurrence risk is too high to stop anticoagulation at all, the decision to continue has already been made, and testing rarely changes management.

    Do NOT order¶

    Test / panel Why not
    MTHFR C677T / A1298C Do not order. Not associated with VTE (ASH 2023); ACMG 2025 technical standard — routine MTHFR testing not recommended for thrombophilia, recurrent pregnancy loss, or reproductive carrier screening; ISTH-affiliated 2022 consensus — not risk factors for arterial or venous thrombosis, should be removed from panels; ACOG and BSH agree. Variants are present in up to 45% of people by ethnicity and predict neither first VTE nor recurrence. Heterozygosity, homozygosity, or compound heterozygosity should not be labeled a thrombophilia and should not alter anticoagulation. (Distinct from rare severe autosomal-recessive MTHFR deficiency — homocystinuria phenotype.) Routine fasting homocysteine is also insufficient.
    Factor VIII, IX, XI levels Low-value extras — not part of the recommended panel
    PAI-1 level; SERPINE1 / PAI-1 4G/5G polymorphism Low-value extras — omit
    Broad thrombophilia genetic / polygenic-risk panels Omit outside a defined indication
    Anti-phosphatidylserine/prothrombin (aPS/PT) or anti-domain-I β2GPI as routine first-line ISTH recommends against adding them as routine first-line APS assays; they do not score in the 2023 criteria
    Inherited panel (FVL, prothrombin, PC/PS/AT) for an arterial event Inherited thrombophilias are principally venous — order the complete APS panel instead. ACMG: routine FVL/prothrombin testing not generally recommended for personal/family history of arterial thrombotic disorders (CAD, ischemic stroke)
    Population-wide thrombophilia screening before COCs ASH strongly recommends against it

    Management¶

    Step 1 — thrombotic APS, especially triple-positive: warfarin, not a DOAC

    • Warfarin (VKA) — target INR 2–3 for a first unprovoked venous thrombotic APS event. This is the conventional standard and the only drug target verified in this run's research.
    • Avoid DOACs in triple-positive APS. BSH recommends against starting DOACs in triple-positive APS (1B); patients already on a DOAC should generally be switched to a VKA after discussion of risks and benefits (continuing the DOAC beats no anticoagulation if the patient declines). EULAR specifically recommends against rivaroxaban in triple-positive APS (excess arterial events in the high-risk TRAPS population). The 2026 ACC statement likewise reserves VKA for high-risk APS: triple positivity, arterial or small-vessel thrombosis, organ involvement, or valve disease. Across RCTs, DOACs carried about 5-fold higher odds of arterial events, mainly stroke, than warfarin — and the meta-analysis found no clear interaction by triple-positive status, which suggests VKA is the better choice for most thrombotic APS.
    • Arterial APS is more individualized: VKA remains preferred, INR 2–3 versus 3–4, or in selected circumstances VKA plus low-dose aspirin, per recurrent-thrombosis and bleeding risk. Triple positivity alone is not an indication to automatically target INR 3–4.
    • Duration: long-term anticoagulation is suggested even after VTE with minor provoking factors in triple-positive patients (BSH 2C).
    • Asymptomatic triple-positive carriers (no APS by criteria): thromboprophylaxis in high-risk situations such as surgery or immobility; hydroxychloroquine considered case by case; aggressive control of cardiovascular risk factors and counseling against estrogen; risk assessment for antenatal and/or postpartum prophylaxis in pregnancy. BSH does not routinely recommend low-dose aspirin for primary prevention (2B); EULAR 2019, by contrast, recommends low-dose aspirin for high-risk-profile carriers — the two disagree, so document the reasoning.
    • → For non-APS VTE, the DOAC is the usual agent (apixaban is the most commonly used in practice — see Cerebral venous thrombosis for the house DOAC discussion). DOAC doses are not restated here — see the VTE/CVT treatment pages.

    Step 2 — inherited thrombophilia positive after a first unprovoked VTE: usually no change

    • Heterozygous FVL or prothrombin G20210A: usually does not change duration or drug choice. It may matter for counseling, pregnancy/hormonal exposures, and sometimes relatives — but the VTE itself, not the mutation, is usually driving the decision for extended therapy.
    • Protein C, protein S, or antithrombin deficiency: potentially more consequential for family counseling/testing, pregnancy, prophylaxis during transient high-risk situations, and assessment of unusual or recurrent thrombosis — but after a first conventional unprovoked proximal DVT/PE, the indication for extended anticoagulation usually already exists independent of the result.
    • Confirm the deficiency first: a reported deficiency must be biologically credible and appropriately confirmed (repeat off confounders), because acute thrombosis and anticoagulant therapy confound these assays.
    • The exception within the exception: if the patient has unprovoked VTE and high bleeding risk and is leaning toward stopping, a positive result — especially AT/PC/PS deficiency, which carries higher recurrence risk — may tip the decision toward extended therapy.

    Step 3 — provoked VTE with a positive test, or a known familial defect: extend or prevent

    • Provoked VTE (nonsurgical major transient risk factor, pregnancy/postpartum, COC-associated) with thrombophilia found: continue anticoagulation indefinitely rather than stopping after primary treatment. Conditional recommendation, very-low-certainty evidence — largely modeling, not direct RCTs — and the most debated part of the 2023 guideline.
    • Family planning — hormonal contraception/HRT: for a woman with a family history of VTE and a known familial antithrombin, protein C, or protein S deficiency, test for the known familial defect; if positive, avoid combined oral contraceptives and use the result when considering HRT. Do not test solely for known heterozygous FVL or prothrombin mutation in the family to decide COCs/HRT.
    • Family planning — pregnancy: testing can change antepartum/postpartum prophylaxis when there is a known high-risk familial thrombophilia — ASH specifically supports selective testing in families with antithrombin deficiency, homozygous FVL, or combined FVL + prothrombin mutation (a positive result can lead to antepartum prophylaxis); postpartum testing/prophylaxis applies to a somewhat broader set including protein C/S deficiencies. For a woman who has already had VTE, her own history already calls for avoiding estrogen and giving antepartum/postpartum prophylaxis whatever the test shows — the main value of testing is for asymptomatic female relatives considering estrogen or pregnancy.
    • Unaffected relatives: testing is most useful when the family has a specific, known, high-risk defect. If a first-degree relative has confirmed antithrombin, protein C, or protein S deficiency, selective testing can influence thromboprophylaxis during otherwise minor transient risks (immobilization, illness). A family history of heterozygous FVL or prothrombin G20210A generally does not justify testing to decide prophylaxis around minor risk factors.

    Avoid

    • Ordering MTHFR and acting on it — a positive MTHFR variant is not a thrombophilia and must not extend anticoagulation, trigger family screening, or change contraception decisions.
    • Using a negative panel as reassurance to stop anticoagulation after an otherwise unprovoked VTE — ASH explicitly modeled that strategy and rejected it.
    • Using a positive inherited test to extend anticoagulation after a first unprovoked VTE — the unprovoked event itself already usually justifies considering indefinite therapy.
    • Starting or continuing a DOAC in triple-positive APS — especially rivaroxaban, especially with arterial disease.
    • Testing functional assays during the acute event or on interfering anticoagulants and calling the result a hereditary deficiency — repeat off confounders first.
    • Ordering the inherited panel for arterial thrombosis — send the APS panel instead.

    TRICARE — anticoagulant prescribing

    Warfarin and enoxaparin (LMWH) are generic/formulary. DOACs are covered — a preferred DOAC exists on the Uniform Formulary. Verify the current preferred agent and any PA at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers. Warfarin needs INR monitoring (a deployment and access consideration — see the military box); a DOAC avoids monitoring but is the wrong drug in triple-positive APS regardless of formulary status.

    Disposition¶

    • Admit vs discharge is driven by the thrombotic event, not the panel — PE with hemodynamic compromise, extensive DVT, CVT with hemorrhage or depressed consciousness, and splanchnic thrombosis with bowel ischemia admit; a stable first DVT/PE with reliable follow-up can go home on anticoagulation.
    • Follow-up: repeat/confirmatory testing at ≥12 weeks for APS persistence (LA, aCL, anti-β2GPI) and at ≥3 months for protein C/S/antithrombin. Hematology referral for APS, triple positivity, unusual-site thrombosis, or a confirmed deficiency. Document the provoked/unprovoked classification — it, not the panel, drives the duration decision.
    • Counseling points: a positive inherited test after unprovoked VTE does not make the patient "more clot-prone" in a way that changes their own treatment — its value is mostly for family planning and relatives. A negative test does not make them low-risk.

    Military medicine — hypercoagulable workup

    Framework — verify against current AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03, DoDI 1332.18, service deployment/aeromedical policy (and any relevant JTS CPG) before counseling.

    • Deployability: acute VTE and the anticoagulation that follows are deployment-limiting. Indefinite anticoagulation is a deployment problem in itself — warfarin needs INR monitoring that does not travel well downrange; a DOAC avoids monitoring but the patient on indefinite therapy is still non-deployable in most settings. Estrogen contraception should be changed after VTE.
    • Profile (DA Form 3349): temporary profile during the acute event and anticoagulation titration; permanent profile if indefinite anticoagulation or persistent functional limits are documented.
    • EPTS / LOD: hereditary thrombophilia usually predates service — document onset and pre-service history; it affects existed-prior-to-service and line-of-duty determinations. A VTE during service is a line-of-duty event unless a pre-existing defect is the sole cause.
    • Retention (AR 40-501 / DoDI 6130.03): recurrent VTE, and a thrombophilia requiring indefinite anticoagulation, generally triggers referral to the DES (MEB/PEB). Document etiology, imaging, the provoked/unprovoked classification, anticoagulation plan, and duration — that packet is what the board decides on.
    • MEB / IDES: refer when indefinite anticoagulation is the plan, when there is residual deficit from the event (infarct, hemorrhage, post-thrombotic syndrome), or when seizures or other sequelae follow a cerebral event.
    • Aeromedical / special duty: a history of VTE, cerebral venous thrombosis, seizures, and chronic anticoagulation are disqualifying / waiver-dependent for flying duty. Confirm current aeromedical policy before prescribing — many anticoagulants are independently disqualifying.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    • ASH 2023 is the anchor, and its logic is counterintuitive on purpose. The guideline recommends against testing after unprovoked VTE — not because the tests are useless, but because indefinite anticoagulation is already the plan, so the result cannot change it. Testing is suggested exactly where the default is to stop: nonsurgical major transient risk factors, pregnancy/postpartum, COC-associated VTE, and CVT/splanchnic thrombosis where stopping is planned. Test only when the result has a plausible management consequence.
    • MTHFR is out. ASH, ACMG (2025 technical standard), ISTH-affiliated consensus, ACOG, and BSH all agree: MTHFR C677T/A1298C is not associated with VTE, is present in up to 45% of people, and should be removed from panels. A positive result must not be labeled a thrombophilia or used to extend anticoagulation.
    • APS is the exception that changes the drug, not just the duration. The 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria are weighted (≥3 clinical + ≥3 laboratory points, entry criterion, 3-year window, persistence ≥12 weeks) and deliberately trade sensitivity (84%) for specificity (99%). Triple positivity — persistent LA + aCL + anti-β2GPI — is the highest-risk serologic profile, and it means warfarin (INR 2–3), not a DOAC: DOACs carried ~5-fold higher odds of arterial events, mainly stroke, than warfarin across RCTs. BSH (1B) and EULAR (against rivaroxaban) and the 2026 ACC statement all point the same way.
    • Timing is half the test. DNA tests and aCL/anti-β2GPI ELISAs can be sent anytime; protein C, protein S, antithrombin, and lupus anticoagulant cannot be trusted acutely or on anticoagulation. Warfarin falsely lowers protein C/S; heparin lowers antithrombin; DOACs cause false-positive lupus anticoagulants and can falsely raise clot-based protein C/S. The two-stage approach (DNA + antibodies now, functional tests later) is the practical fix.
    • The family-planning use is the clearest one. Testing a known familial high-risk defect (antithrombin, protein C, or protein S deficiency) changes contraception, HRT, and pregnancy prophylaxis — for the patient and for asymptomatic relatives. Heterozygous FVL/prothrombin alone does not justify testing for those decisions, and population screening before COCs is strongly discouraged.

Blood Advances. 2023;7(22):7101-7138. — The anchor: Recommendation 1 (do not test to guide duration after unprovoked VTE), Recs 3–5 (test for nonsurgical major transient / pregnancy / postpartum / COC-associated VTE), Recs 7–10 (CVT/splanchnic — test only if stopping is planned).
    2. Barbhaiya M, Zuily S, Naden R, et al. The 2023 ACR/EULAR Antiphospholipid Syndrome Classification Criteria. Arthritis & Rheumatology / Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases. 2023. — Weighted criteria: entry criterion, ≥3 clinical + ≥3 laboratory points, titer thresholds 40–79 / ≥80 units, persistence ≥12 weeks, 3-year window.
    3. Knight JS, Erkan D. Rethinking antiphospholipid syndrome to guide future management and research. Nature Reviews Rheumatology. 2024. — Triple positivity as the highest-risk serologic phenotype; IgG vs IgM isotype risk.
    4. American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. MTHFR technical standard. 2025. — Routine MTHFR testing not recommended for thrombophilia, recurrent pregnancy loss, or reproductive carrier screening.
    5. British Society for Haematology. Guideline on antiphospholipid syndrome / thrombophilia testing. — Against starting DOACs in triple-positive APS (1B); aPL screening after unprovoked VTE (Grade 1B); long-term anticoagulation even after VTE with minor provoking factors in triple-positive patients (2C); no routine low-dose aspirin for primary prevention (2B).
    6. ISTH Scientific and Standardization Committee. Laboratory detection and interpretation of antiphospholipid antibodies (2024/2025 update). — Assay-specific local cutoffs (>99th percentile); LA per ISTH methodology; aPS/PT and anti-domain-I β2GPI not routine first-line assays.
    7. Connors JM. Thrombophilia testing and venous thrombosis. New England Journal of Medicine. 2017. — NEJM testing algorithm (Figure 1, September 21, 2017): who to test after a first VTE; washout intervals (warfarin ≥2 weeks; DOACs ≥5 half-lives).
    8. TRICARE / DoD Uniform Formulary: warfarin and enoxaparin generic/formulary; a preferred DOAC exists on the Uniform Formulary. Verify at the TRICARE Formulary Search.
