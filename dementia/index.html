    Approach to cognitive impairment¶

    Last reviewed: August 2026

    One-liner: cognitive complaints are common and most of them are not neurodegenerative disease — so the job is to separate the treatable from the degenerative, name the syndrome before you name the disease, and only then decide whether a biomarker changes anything.

    Who this is for: the provider handed "memory problems" in clinic, and the one who has to decide whether this is depression, drugs, sleep apnoea, a rapidly progressive dementia that needs admitting tonight, or Alzheimer's disease. In a military setting the same complaint usually arrives as post-concussive cognitive symptoms in a young service member — that is a different problem and lives on the TBI cognitive page.

    🚩 Red flags — do not work this up as slow neurodegeneration

    Pattern Worry Do now
    Progression over weeks to months, not years Rapidly progressive dementia — CJD, autoimmune encephalitis, malignancy, infection Admit or expedite. Go to rapidly progressive dementia
    Age <65, especially <60 Young-onset — FTD, genetic, autoimmune, metabolic, HIV, neurosyphilis Broader work-up; refer to behavioural neurology
    Early falls, parkinsonism, or visual hallucinations Dementia with Lewy bodies Do not give a typical antipsychotic — see the sensitivity warning
    Personality or conduct change dominating memory Frontotemporal dementia Collateral history is the test; imaging supports
    Gait disturbance early, urinary incontinence, "magnetic" gait NPH, vascular, B12 deficiency, cord pathology Imaging + B12/copper; consider gait-responsive causes
    Fluctuating consciousness, inattention, acute onset Delirium — but ask what is causing it Find the cause; do not diagnose dementia in a delirious patient. Autoimmune encephalitis, CNS infection and non-convulsive status all present exactly this way — "it's delirium" is a description, not a diagnosis. If there is no obvious precipitant, or it does not clear, go to rapidly progressive dementia
    Headache, seizure, focal deficit, papilloedema Mass, subdural, vasculitis Image before anything else

    Two rules that prevent most diagnostic errors: never diagnose dementia during a delirium, and never diagnose Alzheimer's disease in a patient you have not screened for depression, drugs, sleep apnoea and thyroid or B12 deficiency.

    Delirium, MCI, dementia — get the level right first¶

    Onset Attention Course Consciousness
    Delirium Hours–days Impaired — the defining feature Fluctuating Altered
    Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) Months–years Usually intact Slowly progressive Normal
    Dementia (major neurocognitive disorder) Years Intact until late Progressive Normal
    • MCI = objective cognitive decline with preserved independence in instrumental activities. Dementia = cognitive decline that interferes with independence. The line is functional, not a test score, and the collateral historian draws it, not the patient.
    • MCI is not a disease — it is a stage with many causes, and the numbers are worth carrying: over a mean 5 years, roughly 41.5% convert to dementia in clinic populations (27% in population-based samples), about half stay stable, and 8.7% revert to normal in clinics — 28.2% in population samples. Annual conversion is 3–10% in the community, 10–15% in specialty clinics.
      • Reversion is not the all-clear. In one cohort 65% of reverters ultimately progressed. Tell patients they are at elevated risk, screen for treatable causes, and reassess at 6–12 months.
    • Subjective cognitive decline (complaint without objective deficit) carries a modestly increased risk but is not a diagnosis. Follow it; do not work it up as disease.

    Name the syndrome before you name the disease¶

    The single most useful diagnostic move is to ask which cognitive domain went first, and take that answer from someone who lives with the patient.

    Leading domain Think first Page
    Episodic memory (repeats questions, loses the thread of recent events) Alzheimer's disease AD
    Visuospatial, attention that fluctuates, visual hallucinations Dementia with Lewy bodies DLB/PDD
    Behaviour, personality, disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy Behavioural-variant FTD FTD
    Language — effortful speech, or loss of word meaning Primary progressive aphasia FTD
    Processing speed and executive function, stepwise or with vascular risk Vascular cognitive impairment Vascular
    Any of the above, but over weeks to months Rapidly progressive dementia RPD

    Mixed pathology is the norm, not the exception, particularly over 80 — vascular disease and Alzheimer's pathology coexist far more often than either occurs alone. Say "predominantly X" rather than pretending to a single diagnosis you cannot support.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Rule out delirium — then ask what caused it. Acute, fluctuating, inattentive → find the cause. This is where the pathway forks, not where it stops: if there is no obvious precipitant, if it does not clear with treatment of one, or if there are seizures, movement disorder, autonomic instability or fever, that is rapidly progressive dementia until proven otherwise — and the window is measured in weeks.
    2. Take a collateral history. Non-negotiable. What went first, when, and what can they no longer do? A patient's own account of their memory is the least reliable data in the encounter.
    3. Screen objectively — MoCA or MMSE, plus a functional scale. Record the raw score and the education adjustment.
    4. Screen the treatable contributors before anything expensive: depression, medications, sleep apnoea, alcohol, thyroid, B12. Most "reversible dementia" is one of these.
    5. Lab panel + structural imaging (MRI preferred) — see Workup.
    6. Decide the syndrome from the leading domain, and route to the disease page.
    7. Only then ask whether a biomarker changes management. It usually does not, unless anti-amyloid therapy is genuinely on the table.
    8. Address safety and capacity from the first visit — driving, firearms, finances, medications, and the caregiver.
    9. Military disposition — and note that dementia is disqualifying upon diagnosis, which is unlike almost everything else in this reference.

    Workup¶

    Every patient — the baseline set

    • Labs: CBC, CMP, TSH, vitamin B12 (add methylmalonic acid if borderline), HbA1c, lipids. Add: HIV and RPR/treponemal testing where risk or young onset.
    • Structural imaging — MRI brain without contrast is preferred (CT only if MRI is contraindicated or unavailable). You are looking for hippocampal and lobar atrophy patterns, vascular burden, subdural collections, hydrocephalus, and mass lesions.
    • Medication review — every visit. See the Avoid box.
    • Depression screen (PHQ-9 or GDS) and a sleep apnoea screen (STOP-BANG).
    • Objective cognitive testing — MoCA is more sensitive than MMSE for MCI and for executive and visuospatial deficits; MMSE is more familiar and better validated longitudinally. Use one consistently and record the version.

    Add when the picture demands it

    • Young onset (<65), atypical, or rapid → expand: autoimmune and paraneoplastic panels, heavy metals, copper/ceruloplasmin, ammonia, EEG, CSF. See RPD.
    • Neuropsychological testing — when the syndrome is unclear, when the deficit is mild and the stakes are high (capacity, occupation, a service member's fitness), or to establish a baseline before treatment.
    • EEG — suspected seizures, fluctuation, or RPD.
    • DaT-SPECT — supports DLB when parkinsonism is equivocal.
    • Polysomnography — REM sleep behaviour disorder, or suspected OSA.

    Reversible and contributing causes — screen before you diagnose

    Be honest about what "reversible" means. Potentially reversible causes account for 9–23% of dementia depending on setting and definition — but only about 11% actually resolve, and only 3% fully. The commonest genuinely reversible contributors are medications (28.2%), depression (26.2%) and metabolic disorders (15.5%).

    So the screen is not mandatory because reversal is likely. It is mandatory because contributors are common, and every one you fix makes the patient better than they would otherwise have been. B12 deficiency and hypothyroidism usually contribute rather than reverse once neurodegeneration is established; NPH, subdural haematoma and neurosyphilis genuinely can reverse if caught early.

    • Depression — the classic mimic. Prominent complaint with relatively preserved objective performance, poor effort, "I don't know" answers. Treat and reassess.
    • Medications — anticholinergics (oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics), benzodiazepines, opioids, sedating antihistamines, high anticholinergic burden overall.
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea and chronic sleep deprivation.
    • Alcohol and other substances.
    • B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism.
    • Normal pressure hydrocephalus — gait first, then urinary, then cognition. Gait must lead.
    • Subdural haematoma — especially in an anticoagulated or fall-prone patient.
    • Neurosyphilis, HIV, and in the right setting CNS Whipple, Lyme.
    • Hearing and vision loss — under-treated, and they masquerade as cognitive decline.

    Biomarkers — what they answer, and when to ask¶

    The question a biomarker answers is narrow: "is Alzheimer's pathology present?" It does not tell you the syndrome, it does not exclude co-pathology, and in most patients it does not change what you do. Order it when the answer would change management.

    Test What it shows Practical note
    Plasma p-tau217 Alzheimer's pathology, with the best performance of the blood markers Pooled sensitivity 88.1%, specificity 88.7%, AUROC 91.1%. Accuracy 89–91% in secondary care but 85% in primary care — and lower again over age 80 (83%). Unaffected by CKD, diabetes, sex or ApoE genotype
    CSF Aβ42/40 ratio + p-tau181 or p-tau217 Amyloid and tau pathology Long-established; requires LP
    Amyloid PET Fibrillar amyloid burden Confirms pathology; required or expected before anti-amyloid therapy
    Tau PET Tau distribution and stage Correlates with symptoms better than amyloid does
    FDG-PET Regional hypometabolism pattern Helps separate AD from FTD when the clinical picture is ambiguous
    DaT-SPECT Presynaptic dopaminergic deficit Supports DLB; does not diagnose AD

    Use a two-cutoff rule, and know what is actually FDA-cleared

    A single cutoff throws away accuracy. Using two — one to rule in, one to rule out, with the 12–17% who land in between going on to CSF or PET — raises accuracy to 92–94% in both primary and secondary care. Report results that way rather than as a binary.

    The first and so far only FDA-cleared blood-based IVD is the p-tau217/Aβ42 ratio on the Lumipulse platform (Fujirebio), for detecting AD pathology in symptomatic patients. The other assays in circulation — PrecivityAD2, ALZpath, Lilly — are laboratory-developed tests without IVD clearance. That distinction matters for what you can defend ordering.

    Testing asymptomatic people is not recommended outside research.

    A positive amyloid biomarker is not a diagnosis of dementia

    Amyloid pathology is common in cognitively normal older adults, and the prevalence rises steeply with age: ~12% at 50, 18% at 60, 25% at 70, 33% at 80, 43% at 90. ApoE ε4 homozygotes reach 15% by roughly age 40.

    A positive test in a patient without objective impairment tells you about risk, not disease — and it cannot be un-told. Do not order amyloid biomarkers in a patient you would not treat, and counsel before you test, not after.

    This is also why the 2024 biological definition of Alzheimer's disease is contested. Under the Alzheimer's Association Revised Criteria (2024) — which replace the 2018 NIA-AA research framework — an abnormal Core 1 biomarker is sufficient for diagnosis without symptoms. Applied literally to the prevalence figures above, that makes a third of cognitively normal 80-year-olds "cases." Several expert groups argue it should stay in the research domain. This site's position: characterise the symptomatic, do not diagnose the asymptomatic.

    Management — the frame¶

    Disease-specific treatment lives on the disease pages: Alzheimer's (including anti-amyloid therapy), DLB/PDD, FTD, vascular. What follows applies to everyone.

    Step 1 — the things that work regardless of diagnosis

    • Stop or reduce the cognition-toxic drugs. Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines, opioids where possible. This is the highest-yield prescribing act on the page.
    • Treat the contributors — depression, sleep apnoea, pain, hearing and vision loss. Hearing loss is the single largest modifiable dementia risk factor in the Lancet Commission's accounting (9% population-attributable fraction) — but be precise about what the evidence supports. ACHIEVE, the first RCT, was negative overall and positive only in a prespecified higher-risk subgroup; observational ASPREE data found 33% lower 7-year dementia risk with hearing aids. Correcting hearing is risk-modifying, not a treatment for established dementia — offer it on that basis.
    • Vascular risk factor control — BP, lipids, diabetes, smoking. Relevant in every dementia, not just vascular, because mixed pathology is the norm.
    • Exercise, social engagement, and structured routine.
    • Caregiver support and education from the first visit — caregiver burden predicts institutionalization more reliably than the patient's test score.

    Avoid — and this list is as important as anything you prescribe

    • Benzodiazepines — worsen cognition, increase falls, and are hard to stop.
    • Anticholinergics — oxybutynin, diphenhydramine, tricyclics, older antipsychotics. Cumulative anticholinergic burden is a modifiable risk.
    • Typical antipsychotics in suspected DLB — severe sensitivity reactions.
    • Antipsychotics generally for behavioural symptoms — all carry a boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis. Non-pharmacological management first, every time.
    • Do not treat an amyloid biomarker. Treat a patient with a syndrome.

    Safety, capacity and the conversation¶

    • Driving — ask at every visit, and ask the collateral historian separately. A dementia diagnosis alone does not mandate cessation, and the AAN parameter gives usable thresholds:
      • CDR 0.5 (questionable/very mild) — may still drive, assess individually.
      • CDR 1 (mild dementia) — substantially higher crash risk; on-road test pass rates are only 41–76%. Strongly counsel toward stopping.
      • CDR ≥2 (moderate) — incompatible with safe driving, across every guideline.
      • Reassess every 6 months in anyone still driving. Raise concern faster for caregiver worry, any crash or ticket, self-restricted driving patterns, or aggressive driving.
      • DLB and FTD impair driving fitness more severely at milder stages — visuospatial, motor and behavioural deficits do not track CDR. Do not apply the AD thresholds to them.
      • Know your state reporting requirements.
    • Firearms — in a military and veteran population this must be asked directly and early, framed as storage planning while the patient can still participate in the decision.
    • Finances — vulnerability to fraud and financial mismanagement often precedes the diagnosis.
    • Advance planning and capacity — the window for the patient to express their own wishes closes. Raise durable power of attorney and advance directives at the first visit, not the fifth.
    • Say the diagnosis. Patients and families overwhelmingly want to be told. Vague euphemism transfers the burden of uncertainty onto the family without sparing anyone.

    TRICARE — cognitive impairment work-up

    • The symptomatic drugs are generic and formulary — donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, memantine. Start there; nothing on this page requires a prior authorization.
    • The expensive decisions are diagnostic, not therapeutic. Amyloid PET, tau PET and plasma biomarker assays are where coverage questions arise, and coverage has moved quickly — check current policy before you order, not after.
    • Anti-amyloid monoclonals are a different order of magnitude — high-cost specialty infusions with their own coverage, site-of-care and MRI-monitoring requirements. Those are covered on the Alzheimer's page.
    • Neuropsychological testing frequently needs authorization; the referral question and the clinical justification matter more than the CPT code.
    • Verify current coverage at the TRICARE Formulary Search and with the regional contractor before counselling — DoD P&T revises criteria without notice.

    Military medicine — cognitive impairment and dementia

    Framework — verify against current AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 and Vol 2, DoDI 1332.18 and service aeromedical policy. See Profiles, MEB/MAR2 & deployability. This box carries the section-wide framing; the disease pages carry only what differs.**

    • Get the population right first. True neurodegenerative dementia is rare on active duty. In an MTF the overwhelming majority of dementia care is for retirees and dependents, where the questions are diagnostic, caregiver and coverage questions rather than fitness ones. In a young service member, cognitive complaints are far more likely to be post-concussive, sleep, mood, substance or medication-related — start at TBI — cognitive, not here. The two pages where this changes are FTD and rapidly progressive dementia.
    • Retention — this is the inverse of every other neurology page on this site. DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 para 5.26.d covers "permanent or progressive cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer's disease or other dementias" and states it "is not compatible with retention, and the Service should initiate appropriate medical and personnel actions upon diagnosis. Paragraph 5.26.a. does not apply." That sentence appears exactly twice in all of para 5.26 — here and at epilepsy, 5.26.k. There is no persists-despite-treatment prong to argue and no MRDP to wait out. Do not import the reasoning from MG, LEMS or botulism, where the 5.26.a gate is the whole argument.
    • What the standard turns on is "permanent or progressive," not the word "dementia." The gate has moved from treatment response to diagnostic certainty — and that is the only defence the Soldier has left. A referral built on a screening score and a worried spouse will not survive review, and the cost of being wrong is a career. Before the word enters the record, the note must carry: collateral history from someone who sees the Soldier daily; formal neuropsychological testing, not a MoCA; structural imaging; documented exclusion of depression, medication effect (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids, antiseizure drugs), sleep apnoea and sleep debt, substance use, thyroid and B12; and evidence of decline over time or irreversibility — because that is the element 5.26.d actually names.
    • Army hook — cite the DoD paragraph as the operative one. The AR counterpart is the catch-all AR 40-501 3-31k, which expressly reaches "alteration of consciousness, speech, personality, or cognitive and mental function" rising to the para 3-1 definition. But note the mismatch and handle it explicitly: 3-31k carries its own "after adequate treatment… residual symptoms" gate that 5.26.d waives. Cite 5.26.d as the standard that acts on diagnosis and 3-31k + 3-1 as the Army definition of the disqualifying condition — not the reverse. Where a named subparagraph fits the underlying disease better, use it alongside: 3-31d (basal ganglia/cerebellar degeneration, Parkinson's named) for DLB/PDD; 3-31f (stroke with residuals) for vascular; 3-31j (TBI) where head injury is the substrate.
    • Profile (DA Form 3349) — the primary factor is P, not S. DA Pam 40-502 defines P — physical capacity or stamina as including "conditions of the… nervous system", and confines S to "personality, emotional stability, and psychiatric diseases." A neurodegenerative dementia is a nervous-system disease: profile it P, adding S only where personality change, disinhibition or emotional instability is a separately limiting feature (bvFTD, DLB neuropsychiatric symptoms). AR 40-501 itself writes neurologic profiles as P-3. Temporary while the work-up and treatable-cause trial run; permanent once the diagnosis is established — and a permanent 3 or 4 is itself the DES referral (AR 40-501 3-4). Section 4 limits must speak in functional terms to weapons access, driving and vehicle operation, independent duty, supervisory and financial responsibility, and safety-sensitive tasks.
    • EPTS / LOD. Insidious onset makes dating genuinely hard, and the earliest evidence is usually not in the medical record — review OERs/NCOERs, ACFT trend, counselling statements (DA Form 4856) and prior PHAs, and date the first attributable functional change. Remember the 6-month rule: for conditions predating enlistment, accession standards govern the first 6 months of active service (EPSBD); after that, retention standards apply. In young-onset cases ask specifically about family history — several dementias are autosomal dominant — and genetic testing in a service member follows counselling and a considered decision, not curiosity.
    • MEB / DES — the referral sequence, in order. (1) Work the differential and treat the reversible contributors on a temporary profile, linking every extension. (2) When the diagnosis is established to the standard above, that is the trigger — no 12-month administrative MRDP to run out, no clinical-stability argument to make. (3) Write the permanent P-3 in e-Profile with functional Section 4 limits; that profile is the referral (AR 40-501 3-4). (4) An approval authority second-signs, and that signature starts the DES clock. MAR2 is not the pathway here — MAR2 exists for Soldiers who meet retention standards but cannot perform their PMOS, and a Soldier meeting 5.26.d does not meet them. Sending this case to MAR2 delays a board the Soldier is entitled to. Whether the Soldier is found fit is the PEB's decision — write the standard and the evidence, never the outcome.
    • Deployability: non-deployable — and not primarily because of the memory. Impaired judgment under stress, weapons access, and no capacity for independent or unsupervised duty, against an operational environment whose specific stressors — sleep deprivation, heat, dehydration, altered routine, unfamiliar surroundings — reliably worsen cognition and precipitate delirium. The 72-hour deploy-on-receipt-of-mission expectation (AR 40-501 3-1) cannot be met. Practically: forward locations cannot deliver neuropsychological reassessment, MRI-based monitoring or caregiver structure, and the agents used for neuropsychiatric symptoms bring their own controlled-substance and monitoring problems.
    • Aeromedical: disqualifying, and AR 40-501 names it. AR 40-501 4-27i disqualifies "history of degenerative or demyelinating process. Such as multiple sclerosis, dementia, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, or basal ganglia disease" for flying duty Classes 1, 2, 2F, 2P, 3 and 4. 4-27h adds "history of organic mental syndromes… until reviewed by the USAAMA" — which is where the USAAMA routing comes from. On the accession side the imported paragraph is DoDI 6130.03 Vol 1 para 6.26.d, "history of neurodegenerative disorders" — not 6.26.q, whose exemplars are non-degenerative chronic disorders. Ground the aircrew member and route to USAAMA; the same logic applies to ATC and UAS duties.
    • The TBI overlap needs saying plainly. Service members with repetitive head-impact exposure ask whether they are developing CTE. Traumatic encephalopathy syndrome is a research construct, CTE is a post-mortem neuropathological diagnosis, and neither can be diagnosed in a living patient. Counsel honestly, treat the treatable contributors, and see TBI — work-up and return to duty.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    The field changed at the point of diagnosis before it changed at the point of treatment. For thirty years Alzheimer's disease was a clinical diagnosis confirmed at autopsy. Now amyloid and tau pathology can be detected in life — first with PET, then CSF, and now with blood-based markers led by plasma p-tau217, which performs closest to the CSF standard. That has two consequences the clinic feels immediately: the diagnosis can be biologically confirmed rather than inferred, and it can be confirmed in people whose symptoms do not warrant treatment. A test that is easy to order is easy to over-order, and the pretest probability in front of you determines whether the result means anything.

    Anti-amyloid therapy is real, modest, and demanding. Lecanemab and donanemab genuinely remove amyloid and genuinely slow decline — by margins that are statistically robust and clinically modest — at the cost of ARIA, infusion or injection burden, ApoE ε4 genotyping, and serial MRI. Whether that trade is worth it is a conversation, not a protocol, and it belongs to the patient. The details, including the ARIA monitoring schedule and the interaction with cerebral amyloid angiopathy, are on the Alzheimer's page and the CAA page.

    Meanwhile the highest-yield interventions remain unglamorous. Deprescribing anticholinergics and benzodiazepines, treating sleep apnoea, correcting hearing loss, managing vascular risk, and supporting the caregiver do more for more patients than anything in the biomarker column — and they work regardless of which protein is misfolding.
