    Vascular cognitive impairment¶

    Last reviewed: August 2026

    Part of Approach to cognitive impairment.

    One-liner: cognitive impairment caused by cerebrovascular disease — the only common dementia with a genuinely modifiable cause, which is why it is worth identifying even when it coexists with Alzheimer's pathology, as it usually does.

    Who this is for: the provider looking at a scan full of white matter change wondering whether it explains the patient, and the one deciding what to actually do about it. The answer is mostly on the stroke prevention page — this page is about recognising the syndrome and knowing which lever to pull.

    🚩 What changes the plan

    Pattern Worry Do
    Cognitive decline within 3–6 months of a stroke Post-stroke cognitive impairment Formal cognitive assessment — it is routinely missed in stroke follow-up
    Lobar microbleeds and superficial siderosis Cerebral amyloid angiopathy — not just "small vessel disease" Changes anticoagulation decisions and anti-amyloid eligibility. Apply Boston v2.0
    Young patient, migraine with aura, family history, confluent anterior temporal white matter change CADASIL and other monogenic small vessel disease Genetic referral; do not write it off as hypertensive change
    Gait disturbance leading, urinary symptoms, "magnetic" gait NPH vs vascular vs both These overlap and coexist; do not assume
    Rapid stepwise decline with headache or systemic features Vasculitis, endocarditis, hypercoagulable state Go to rapidly progressive dementia

    Diagnosis¶

    The requirement is a plausible causal link, not merely coexistence. White matter hyperintensities are near-universal in older adults; finding them does not make the dementia vascular.

    What supports a vascular contribution:

    • A temporal relationship — decline beginning within months of a clinical stroke, or a stepwise rather than smoothly progressive course.
    • A matching cognitive profile — processing speed and executive dysfunction lead, with memory relatively better preserved and, critically, helped by cueing (a retrieval rather than encoding failure — the opposite of Alzheimer's).
    • Imaging burden proportionate to the deficit — strategic infarcts (thalamus, angular gyrus, caudate, genu of internal capsule), multiple lacunes, or confluent white matter hyperintensity, not a few scattered dots.
    • Corroborating findings — gait disturbance, early urinary urgency, pseudobulbar affect, focal signs.

    Subtypes worth distinguishing because they behave differently: post-stroke cognitive impairment; subcortical ischaemic small vessel disease, the commonest and most insidious; multi-infarct; strategic single infarct; hypoperfusion; and haemorrhagic, which brings CAA into the frame.

    Mixed is the default, not the exception

    Over 80, coexisting Alzheimer's and vascular pathology is the commonest finding at autopsy, and the two are more than additive — vascular burden lowers the threshold at which Alzheimer's pathology produces symptoms.

    Practical consequence: a patient can have both, and treating the vascular half is worthwhile even when the amnestic syndrome points to AD. "Mixed dementia" is an honest diagnosis, not a hedge — and it justifies both a cholinesterase inhibitor and aggressive vascular risk reduction.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Confirm the syndrome is executive- and speed-led, and that memory improves with cueing.
    2. Image, and read the scan properly — burden, location, strategic infarcts, and the microbleed pattern. Lobar microbleeds mean CAA, which changes decisions.
    3. Work the stroke aetiology if there has been a clinical event — this is a stroke work-up, not a dementia work-up.
    4. Attack the modifiable risk factors hard. This is the therapeutic core of the page.
    5. Screen for and treat depression and apathy — both are more common here than in AD and both are treatable.
    6. Consider a cholinesterase inhibitor where mixed pathology is likely.
    7. Rehabilitate — gait, falls, and cognitive rehabilitation where available.
    8. Military disposition — dementia is disqualifying upon diagnosis; the vascular risk factors have their own retention implications.

    Management¶

    Step 1 — vascular risk factor control (this is the treatment)

    Full regimens are on the stroke prevention page; this is the cognitive-specific framing.

    • Blood pressure control is the single best-supported intervention for preventing and slowing vascular cognitive impairment. Target per current stroke-prevention guidance. Avoid over-treatment causing orthostatic hypotension in a falls-prone older patient — check standing pressures.
    • Lipids, diabetes, smoking cessation, atrial fibrillation detection and anticoagulation — as for secondary stroke prevention.
    • Antithrombotics per the stroke indication, not for the dementia. There is no indication for antiplatelet therapy to treat cognitive impairment itself, and in a patient with lobar microbleeds or suspected CAA the calculus shifts toward harm.
    • Physical activity — supported for both vascular and cognitive endpoints.

    Step 2 — symptomatic and supportive

    • Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine are not FDA-labelled for vascular dementia. Their use here is off-label, and the effect where present is small. The defensible case is probable mixed pathology, where you are treating the Alzheimer's component — say that explicitly rather than treating "vascular dementia" with an AD drug.
    • Treat depression and apathy — both common post-stroke and both under-treated. An SSRI is the usual choice.
    • Gait, falls and continence — physiotherapy, falls assessment, home safety.
    • Pseudobulbar affect — recognise it and explain it to the family; it is distressing and frequently mistaken for depression.

    Avoid

    • Treating white matter hyperintensities as a diagnosis. They are a finding; the patient is the diagnosis.
    • Antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy prescribed for cognition — no indication, and real harm where CAA is present.
    • Aggressive BP lowering without checking standing pressures — orthostatic hypotension causes falls and may worsen cerebral hypoperfusion.
    • Anticholinergics and benzodiazepines — as everywhere in this section.
    • Assuming "vascular" excludes Alzheimer's. It usually does not.

    TRICARE — vascular cognitive impairment

    • Everything with evidence behind it is generic and formulary — antihypertensives, statins, metformin, antiplatelets, and the SSRIs. This is the cheapest dementia to treat well, because the treatment is cardiovascular risk management.
    • Anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation follow the stroke pages; DOAC tier and PA status change, so verify.
    • Cholinesterase inhibitors used off-label for presumed mixed pathology are generic and unlikely to be challenged, but document the mixed-pathology reasoning rather than coding an AD indication you have not established.
    • Cardiac rehabilitation, physiotherapy and falls programmes are usually the higher-value authorization requests here.
    • Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search. Not independently verified for this page.

    Military medicine — vascular cognitive impairment

    Section-wide framing on the hub. Only the deltas are here.

    • This is the one page in the section where 5.26.d is often NOT the operative standard. Below the dementia threshold this is an ordinary post-stroke disposition running MRDP → the MAR2-versus-DES fork, gated normally by 5.26.a. Only when permanent or progressive cognitive impairment is established does 5.26.d take over and the gate disappear.
    • Named Army hook: AR 40-501 3-31f — "stroke… when residuals affect performance" — cited ahead of 3-31k, with DoDI 6130.03 Vol 2 5.26.b as the DoD floor.
    • The risk factors carry their own standards. The stroke, the hypertension and the atrial fibrillation each have retention implications independent of the cognition — cite them separately; see stroke.
    • The realistic active-duty case is post-stroke cognitive impairment in a 40s–50s service member, not late-life small vessel disease. Formal cognitive assessment is routinely omitted from stroke follow-up — and in a service member that omission decides whether a real deficit is documented before a return-to-duty decision. Test it deliberately.
    • This is the only page in the section where the military box can legitimately be about prevention. Blood pressure, lipids, tobacco and weight management in mid-career service members are cognitive interventions as well as cardiovascular ones.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    The only dementia you can meaningfully prevent. Alzheimer's disease has a modifiable component; vascular cognitive impairment is mostly modifiable component. Blood pressure control has the strongest supporting evidence, and the interventions are the same ones already being pushed for cardiovascular reasons — which makes this the rare neurology page whose main therapeutic message is "do the cardiology properly."

    The diagnostic trap is the scan. White matter hyperintensities are so prevalent in older adults that finding them explains nothing on its own, and "small vessel disease" has become a radiological shrug that closes down thinking. The discipline is to ask whether the burden and location are proportionate to the deficit and its profile — and to notice when the microbleed pattern is lobar, because that is cerebral amyloid angiopathy, which changes anticoagulation decisions and anti-amyloid eligibility rather than just adding a descriptor.

    And the honest position on most older patients is "mixed." Coexisting Alzheimer's and vascular pathology is the commonest autopsy finding above 80, and the two interact — vascular burden lowers the threshold at which amyloid and tau produce symptoms. That is not a diagnostic failure to apologise for; it is a reason to treat both halves, and it is why the vascular risk factors deserve attention in every dementia clinic, not just this page.

    Key sources: vascular cognitive impairment consensus criteria; the stroke-prevention evidence base as carried on the stroke prevention page; Boston v2.0 criteria for CAA.
