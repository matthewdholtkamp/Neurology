    Frontotemporal dementia¶

    Last reviewed: August 2026

    Part of Approach to cognitive impairment.

    One-liner: a group of young-onset degenerative syndromes that begin with behaviour, personality or language rather than memory — frequently misdiagnosed as psychiatric illness for years, frequently inherited, and the dementia most likely to present in someone still working.

    Who this is for: the provider seeing a 45–65-year-old whose personality has changed, whose job performance has collapsed, or whose speech is failing — and who has already been through psychiatry. In a military population this is the one degenerative dementia that can genuinely present on active duty.

    🚩 The pattern that should stop you calling it psychiatric

    Finding Why it matters
    Personality change in a 50-year-old with no psychiatric history New-onset primary psychiatric illness in mid-life is uncommon. Late-onset "depression" that is really apathy, or "mania" that is really disinhibition, is bvFTD until proven otherwise
    Loss of empathy, socially inappropriate behaviour, loss of insight Not a mood disorder. The family, not the patient, brings the complaint
    New compulsive or ritualised behaviour; dietary change, especially carbohydrate craving Highly characteristic and rarely volunteered — ask
    Progressive language failure with preserved memory Primary progressive aphasia
    Any of the above + fasciculations, weakness, dysphagia FTD–ALS overlap. Look for it deliberately; it changes prognosis and counselling entirely
    A parent or sibling with young-onset dementia, ALS, or "early Alzheimer's" Up to a third of FTD is autosomal dominant — take a three-generation family history

    The commonest error is years of psychiatric treatment before anyone images the brain. In a mid-life patient with new behavioural change and declining function, imaging is not over-investigation.

    Diagnosis¶

    Behavioural-variant FTD (bvFTD) — the commonest form. Diagnosis rests on early and progressive change in behaviour or personality, with features drawn from: disinhibition, apathy or inertia, loss of sympathy or empathy, perseverative, stereotyped or compulsive behaviour, hyperorality and dietary change, and a dysexecutive neuropsychological profile with relative sparing of memory and visuospatial function. Supportive imaging shows frontal and/or anterior temporal atrophy or hypometabolism.

    Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) — language is the earliest and most prominent deficit:

    Variant Core deficit Typical pathology
    Nonfluent/agrammatic Effortful, halting speech; agrammatism; apraxia of speech Usually FTD (tau)
    Semantic Loss of word meaning; impaired naming and single-word comprehension; surface dyslexia Usually FTD (TDP-43)
    Logopenic Word-finding pauses, impaired sentence repetition Usually Alzheimer's disease — see AD

    The logopenic caveat matters: a PPA syndrome does not mean FTD pathology. If the variant is logopenic, the underlying disease is most often AD, and that changes both prognosis and whether amyloid biomarkers are worth pursuing.

    FTD–ALS overlap: motor neuron disease develops in a meaningful minority of FTD patients, and cognitive/behavioural change occurs in a substantial proportion of ALS patients. Examine for fasciculations, wasting, weakness, brisk reflexes and bulbar signs at every visit — the finding transforms the conversation about prognosis and about advance planning.

    Genetics — ask, then decide whether to test

    A family history of dementia is reported in up to 40% of FTD, with clear autosomal dominant inheritance in roughly 10–30%. Three genes account for the large majority — about 60% of familial cases: C9orf72 (also the commonest genetic cause of ALS, and the classic FTD–ALS link), MAPT (tau) and GRN (progranulin).

    • Take a three-generation family history, and ask specifically about ALS and about psychiatric illness — affected relatives are often mislabelled.
    • Genetic testing is a counselling event, not a lab test. It has implications for adult children, for insurance, and — in a service member — potentially for career. Refer to genetic counselling before testing, not after the result.

    What supports the diagnosis: MRI showing frontal and/or anterior temporal atrophy, often asymmetric; FDG-PET showing frontotemporal hypometabolism, which is particularly useful when MRI is equivocal and when the question is FTD versus AD; and neuropsychological testing showing a dysexecutive or language-led profile with relatively preserved episodic memory. Amyloid biomarkers are useful mainly to argue against AD in an ambiguous case.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Take the collateral history. In bvFTD the patient's account is not merely unreliable — the loss of insight is a diagnostic feature.
    2. Image early. MRI brain; add FDG-PET if the clinical picture is ambiguous or the MRI is unremarkable.
    3. Examine for motor neuron signs — and repeat it at follow-up.
    4. Neuropsychological testing to establish the profile and a baseline.
    5. Take a three-generation family history; refer to genetic counselling if the pattern suggests inheritance.
    6. Stop the drugs that will not help and may harm — cholinesterase inhibitors, antipsychotics where avoidable.
    7. Treat the behaviour that most endangers the patient or the family, non-pharmacologically first, then an SSRI.
    8. Address safety early and bluntly — driving, firearms, finances, and the patient's capacity to make major decisions, which is often already impaired at diagnosis.
    9. Support the caregiver. Caregiver burden in bvFTD exceeds that in AD at equivalent stages, because behaviour is harder to live with than forgetfulness.
    10. Military disposition — and this is the dementia most likely to arise on active duty.

    Management¶

    There is no disease-modifying treatment for FTD. Everything below is symptomatic, and the most valuable interventions are non-pharmacological.

    Step 1 — non-pharmacological, and deprescribing

    • Identify triggers and restructure the environment. Behaviour in bvFTD is often stimulus-driven and routine-dependent; changing the environment works better than changing the neurochemistry.
    • Caregiver education is the intervention. Families need to hear that the behaviour is the disease, not a choice and not a personality flaw — that reframing changes outcomes.
    • Stop cholinesterase inhibitors if someone has started one. They are not indicated in FTD, do not help, and may worsen behavioural symptoms — a common inherited error from an assumed Alzheimer's diagnosis.
    • Memantine is not indicated in FTD and has not shown benefit.
    • Review every sedating and anticholinergic drug.

    Step 2 — if medication is needed for behaviour

    • SSRIs are the usual first choice for disinhibition, compulsive and repetitive behaviours, overeating and irritability. Sertraline is the pragmatic default; citalopram carries dose-dependent QT prolongation. All are off-label for FTD.
    • Trazodone is used for agitation and sleep, also off-label. It carries the antidepressant class boxed warning for suicidal thoughts and behaviours in young adults — which matters more here than in late-life dementia, because FTD patients are frequently under 65 and sometimes under 55.
    • Antipsychotics: avoid where possible. The boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis applies, and patients with FTD may be unusually sensitive to extrapyramidal effects. If genuinely required, use the lowest dose of an atypical for the shortest time, with a documented review date.
    • Do not treat apathy with a stimulant reflexively — the evidence is thin and the cardiovascular and behavioural risks are real.

    Avoid

    • Cholinesterase inhibitors — no benefit, possible worsening.
    • Memantine — no demonstrated benefit in FTD.
    • Antipsychotics as a default for behaviour — boxed warning, extrapyramidal sensitivity.
    • Benzodiazepines — disinhibition can worsen.
    • Assuming a PPA syndrome is FTD — the logopenic variant is usually Alzheimer's disease.
    • Genetic testing without counselling, particularly in a service member.

    TRICARE — FTD prescribing

    • There is no disease-modifying therapy to authorize, and the symptomatic agents — SSRIs, trazodone — are generic and formulary. The prescribing side of this page is inexpensive.
    • The cost and coverage questions here are diagnostic and supportive, not pharmaceutical: FDG-PET, neuropsychological testing, genetic testing and counselling, speech and language therapy for PPA, and — the largest by far — long-term care and caregiver support for a patient who may be decades from retirement age.
    • Young-onset disease creates benefit problems that older-onset disease does not — dependent children, a working spouse, and an active-duty or recently separated status that determines which system the family lands in. Involve case management early; this is the single most useful thing an MTF can do for these families.
    • Verify current coverage at the TRICARE Formulary Search and with the regional contractor. Not independently verified for this page.

    Military medicine — frontotemporal dementia

    Section-wide framing on the hub. Only the deltas are here — and this is the page where they matter most.

    • This is the one degenerative dementia that genuinely presents on active duty. Peak onset spans the late 40s to early 60s — squarely within a senior NCO or field-grade career.
    • The realistic presentation is a disciplinary or performance problem, not a medical one. Uncharacteristic misconduct, financial irresponsibility, inappropriate remarks, apathy read as laziness, or a collapse in evaluation reports. A previously exemplary senior Soldier whose conduct changes in mid-life deserves a neurological thought before an administrative one. By the time the diagnosis is made, the record may already contain adverse actions the disease caused.
    • The documentation problem is the screening instrument. In bvFTD, judgment and impulse control are impaired before cognition tests abnormal — a MoCA of 27 does not exclude it, and normal screening does not rebut the diagnosis. What carries a board is a formal executive and behavioural neuropsychological battery plus collateral and command corroboration, which is also what satisfies the AR 40-501 3-1a limb (duty interference "as substantiated by the Soldier's commander or supervisor") while a screening score is normal.
    • Profile: P primary, with S carried explicitly — disinhibition and personality change are exactly what DA Pam 40-502 puts under S. Section 4 must reach weapons access, financial responsibility and supervisory authority.
    • EPTS/LOD is most contested here. Symptoms precede diagnosis by years and the evidence sits in evaluation reports and financial or disciplinary records, not in medicine.
    • Where misconduct has already occurred, the interaction between the medical diagnosis and any administrative or UCMJ action is a legal and command question, not a medical one — document the neurological findings and their timeline factually and promptly, and let the appropriate authorities weigh them. Do not editorialise about culpability in the medical record.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    FTD is the dementia that arrives disguised as a character change. Because the earliest damage is to the frontal and anterior temporal regions that govern behaviour, empathy and language rather than memory, the first people to notice are a spouse, a supervisor or a bank — not a doctor, and not the patient, whose loss of insight is itself part of the syndrome. The result is a diagnostic delay measured in years, frequently spent in psychiatric care, and often accompanied by damage to relationships, finances and careers that a timely diagnosis would have limited.

    It is also the dementia with the most genetics. A large minority is autosomal dominant, and C9orf72 links it to ALS — which is why the motor examination belongs in every FTD visit and why the family history is a clinical instrument rather than a formality. That inheritance turns the diagnosis into a family event, which is exactly why testing belongs downstream of counselling.

    And it is the dementia where treatment has least to offer and management has most. There is no disease-modifying therapy, cholinesterase inhibitors do not help and may harm, and antipsychotics carry real risk. What does change outcomes is unglamorous: naming the disease early, restructuring the environment, coaching the family to stop attributing the behaviour to choice, protecting the patient's finances and safety while there is still something to protect, and supporting a caregiver whose burden is measurably heavier than in Alzheimer's disease.

    Key sources: international consensus criteria for behavioural-variant FTD and for the primary progressive aphasias; FTD genetics literature for C9orf72, MAPT and GRN.
