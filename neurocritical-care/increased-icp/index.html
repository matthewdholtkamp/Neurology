    Increased intracranial pressure (ICP)¶

    Last reviewed: November 2026

    One-liner: raised ICP and — more dangerous — brain herniation, in the acutely brain-injured adult (TBI, ICH, SAH, large stroke, mass lesion, hydrocephalus). For the on-call provider: treat the exam, not just the number — a patient can herniate with a "normal" ICP reading, and a complete Cushing triad is a late finding you must never wait for. Management is tiered: general measures for everyone, osmotherapy early (hypertonic saline first for most), and escalation only while ICP stays above threshold.

    🚩 Red flags — impending or established herniation (treat now, image as you go)

    Pattern Worry Do now
    New unilateral dilated, sluggish, or nonreactive pupil + declining consciousness Uncal herniation (CN III compression) Hyperosmolar rescue now (below), HOB 30–45°, brief hyperventilation as a bridge, emergent neurosurgery + CT when transport is safe
    GCS drop ≥2 points, new/worsening posturing, progressive bilateral pupil changes or loss of brainstem responses Central/downward herniation Same rescue pathway; do not wait for the ICP number to "confirm" it
    Cushing response — hypertension with wide pulse pressure + bradycardia + irregular breathing Late brainstem compression (seen in only ~⅓ of tonsillar herniations) Treat as herniation until proven otherwise
    Pinpoint pupils + sudden cardiorespiratory instability Upward/tonsillar herniation (posterior fossa) Urgent neurosurgery; if an EVD is in, drain slowly (overdrainage worsens upward herniation)
    Coma with hydrocephalus on CT (ICH/IVH, SAH) Obstructive hydrocephalus driving the pressure EVD — this is a therapeutic indication, not just monitoring

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Recognize herniation clinically and treat it immediately — deterioration in consciousness, a new pupil abnormality, posturing, or evolving brainstem dysfunction. Do not wait for a complete Cushing triad or invasive confirmation.
    2. Stabilize and position: ABCs, prevent hypotension and hypoxia, head of bed 30–45°, neck midline, loosen collar/ETT ties.
    3. Urgent noncontrast head CT — in active herniation, stabilization and rescue treatment precede transport. MRI later; it must not delay emergency management.
    4. Start Tier 0 measures on everyone and give osmotherapy early (Step 1) — hypertonic saline is the default agent for most patients (see the comparison table).
    5. Call neurosurgery early — referral triggers are listed below; the benefit of surgery is timing-dependent, so refer before you are sure it is needed.
    6. Escalate by tier only while ICP stays above ~22 mmHg or herniation signs persist (Steps 2–3).

    Recognition — signs, herniation syndromes, imaging¶

    Intracranial hypertension (a pressure) is not the same as herniation (tissue displacement). They often coexist, but local pressure gradients can displace tissue dangerously without a markedly elevated reading from a distant monitor.

    Clinical signs of raised ICP — none is sensitive, and several are unavailable in the intubated, aphasic, or obtunded patient:

    Finding Interpretation
    Headache (worse in the morning, lying flat, or with Valsalva), nausea, repeated vomiting Compatible but nonspecific
    Declining arousal — increasing somnolence, confusion, loss of command-following, progression toward coma The most concerning bedside sign when new or progressive
    Papilledema Supports intracranial hypertension when present — but may be delayed or absent in acute intracranial hypertension, even when pressure is severely elevated
    Diplopia from a sixth-nerve palsy False-localizing; does not identify the lesion side
    Pupil dilation, sluggish reactivity, or asymmetry >1 mm Automated pupillometry is more objective than the bedside exam; pupillary dilation alone has only ~28% sensitivity for elevated ICP
    GCS <9 and motor GCS <4 Severity markers that predict intracranial hypertension

    The absence of headache or papilledema is not reassuring in an acutely deteriorating patient. Document the exam trajectory and its confounders: sedative/paralytic exposure, seizures, hypoxemia, hypercapnia, metabolic abnormalities, and pre-existing ocular abnormalities. An unexplained change prompts evaluation — not automatic attribution to sedation.

    Herniation syndromes:

    Syndrome Signature findings
    Uncal (lateral transtentorial) Fixed, dilated pupil ipsilateral to the lesion (CN III compression) with contralateral weakness. Compression of the opposite peduncle against the tentorium causes ipsilateral weakness — the Kernohan false-localizing sign, so weakness alone does not lateralize the lesion
    Central (downward transtentorial) Agitation → obtundation; small or mid-position fixed pupils bilaterally; decorticate then decerebrate posturing; Cushing triad
    Subfalcine Abulia; ACA compression → leg weakness, urinary retention
    Upward (cerebellar) / tonsillar Pinpoint pupils, sudden cardiorespiratory instability, Cheyne–Stokes breathing, sudden death

    Imaging signs (CT):

    Finding What it means
    Basal cisterns absent or compressed The CT sign most strongly linked to measured raised ICP (diagnostic OR ~9.6); crowding around the brainstem, concerning for transtentorial displacement
    Midline shift >5 mm significant, >15 mm poor prognosis (measure at the septum pellucidum, level of the foramen of Monro) Asymmetric mass effect — magnitude does not translate directly into a particular ICP
    Sulcal effacement or obliteration Loss of normal CSF spaces from swelling or mass effect
    Compression of the lateral or third ventricles Swelling/mass effect — but small ventricles are not required for raised ICP
    Mass lesion >25 cm³, hydrocephalus, loss of grey–white differentiation Mechanism and urgency drivers
    Posterior fossa crowding / fourth-ventricle compression Brainstem compression and obstructive hydrocephalus — may occur without substantial supratentorial shift
    Visible tissue displacement (subfalcine, uncal, downward, tonsillar, upward) Direct evidence of dangerous compartmental displacement

    CT cannot supply an exact pressure, and no single examination or CT finding reliably excludes intracranial hypertension — absence of midline shift does not exclude diffuse intracranial hypertension. Optic nerve sheath ultrasound, transcranial Doppler, and pupillometry are adjuncts, not substitutes for indicated invasive measurement (ONSD cutoffs vary; TCD pulsatility index alone performs poorly).

    Do not tap for an opening pressure

    No lumbar puncture to obtain an opening pressure when there is a mass lesion with mass effect, a posterior fossa mass, obstructed CSF pathways, or suspected herniation — lowering spinal CSF pressure can worsen a dangerous pressure gradient.

    ICP monitoring — who, with what, and to what targets¶

    The practical question is: will continuous ICP and CPP information change treatment in a patient at meaningful risk of intracranial hypertension? Monitoring is most useful when the examination is unreliable or insensitive — coma, sedation, or neuromuscular blockade. Device selection and indications follow the underlying disease, not coma alone.

    Severe TBI — the familiar Brain Trauma Foundation selection criteria (third-edition criteria restated in the fourth edition; they do not meet the fourth edition's evidentiary standards for formal selection recommendations — BTF nonetheless recommends ICP-informed management of severe TBI, Level IIB):

    • Post-resuscitation GCS 3–8 plus an abnormal CT (hematoma, contusion, swelling, herniation, or compressed basal cisterns), or
    • Severe TBI with a normal CT plus at least two admission features: age >40 years, unilateral/bilateral motor posturing, or SBP <90 mmHg.

    Contemporary ACS guidance (2024) supports monitoring with GCS ≤8 and structural CT injury, with consideration at higher GCS when lesions may progress, the neurological status deteriorates, or the examination will be limited — and favors serial examination/imaging when CT shows neither structural injury nor pressure signs. Use the post-resuscitation examination: a pharmacologically suppressed GCS is not equivalent to an unconfounded severe-TBI examination.

    Beyond TBI (AHA/ASA classes):

    Clinical setting Indication and limitations
    Spontaneous ICH/IVH with hydrocephalus contributing to depressed consciousness Ventricular drainage should be performed to reduce mortality (Class 1, B-NR) — a therapeutic EVD indication, not merely pressure observation
    Moderate/severe ICH or IVH with reduced consciousness ICP monitoring and treatment may be considered (Class 2b, B-NR); routine monitoring of every ICH patient is not established
    Aneurysmal SAH with acute symptomatic hydrocephalus Urgent CSF diversion is recommended (Class 1, B-NR); EVD is commonly appropriate, lumbar drainage only in selected CSF-flow patterns
    aSAH without hydrocephalus Monitoring may be considered when intracranial hypertension is suspected, particularly when the examination is limited
    Cerebellar infarction with obstructive hydrocephalus AHA/ASA recommends ventriculostomy; concomitant/subsequent decompression depends on neurological condition, brainstem compression, infarct size, and response — deterioration from brainstem compression needs urgent surgical assessment, and a supratentorial pressure reading must not delay it

    Two exceptions to a broad "coma means monitor" approach:

    • Large hemispheric infarction: routine ICP monitoring has not been shown to improve outcomes and is discouraged by ESO consensus. Clinical herniation can precede a frontal ICP rise because of compartmental gradients — decompression decisions follow clinical and imaging evolution, without waiting for a pressure threshold.
    • Acute liver failure: SCCM (2023) suggests against invasive ICP monitoring even with advanced encephalopathy (conditional recommendation, very-low-certainty evidence).

    Devices:

    Device Main advantage Main limitation
    External ventricular drain (EVD) Gold standard — measures ventricular pressure and drains CSF therapeutically; preferred when hydrocephalus is present Hemorrhage, infection, obstruction, and overdrainage risks; accurate measurement requires leveling/care (below)
    Intraparenchymal monitor Continuous local pressure without needing ventricular access Cannot drain CSF; most devices cannot be re-zeroed after placement; a regional reading may not represent another compartment

    Targets (BTF fourth edition, severe TBI):

    Parameter Recommendation Level
    ICP Treat >22 mmHg IIB
    CPP Target 60–70 mmHg (CPP = MAP − ICP) IIB
    CPP augmentation Avoid aggressively forcing CPP >70 mmHg with fluids/pressors (respiratory failure risk) III
    Treatment decisions Integrate ICP, examination, and CT III
    SBP ≥100 mmHg at ages 50–69; ≥110 mmHg at ages 15–49 or >70 III
    PbtO₂ (if monitored) Keep ≥15 mmHg — (ACS)

    Working notes on the numbers:

    • Normal resting adult ICP is ~7–15 mmHg — do not confuse the upper end of the normal reference range with a treatment threshold.
    • BTF sets no mandatory delay at 22 mmHg. ACS operational guidance uses 20–25 mmHg sustained for >5 minutes as the treatment trigger, and that range is reasonable when weighing higher-risk escalation such as decompressive craniectomy. Interpret magnitude, duration, recurrence, and CPP together — a transient cough-related spike and a sustained elevation are different problems. Clinical herniation requires immediate action at any number.
    • CPP is a gradient estimate, not a measurement of blood flow or oxygen delivery. MAP 85 with ICP 20 gives CPP 65 (in target); MAP 70 with ICP 20 gives CPP 50 — low despite an ICP below the treatment threshold; MAP 95 with ICP 30 also gives CPP 65 — in range, but the intracranial hypertension remains a separate problem. Neither "ICP acceptable" nor "CPP acceptable" is sufficient alone.
    • Where in 60–70? It depends partly on autoregulation. With preserved autoregulation, raising MAP may cause arteriolar constriction, reduce cerebral blood volume, and sometimes lower ICP; with impaired autoregulation it can do the opposite. A supervised MAP challenge or multimodal monitoring can individualize the target (SIBICC treats CPP manipulation as a monitored physiological intervention). If CPP is low, identify the dominant mechanism — low MAP, high ICP, or both — and treat that.
    • Outside TBI, ENLS uses CPP 60–70 in its general emergency algorithm, making it a reasonable initial working range in many monitored acute brain injuries — but the evidence for a universal ICP/CPP pair is substantially weaker outside TBI. Adapt to the disease, examination, imaging, hydrocephalus, and autoregulatory response.

    Measurement details that change management: level the EVD transducer at the external auditory meatus/tragus (≈ foramen of Monro) and relevel after position changes; an ordinary fluid-coupled EVD does not give an accurate static ICP while simultaneously draining (briefly close per protocol, or use a separate sensor); an EVD drainage height ordered in cm H₂O is not the same number as an ICP displayed in mmHg; document whether MAP is referenced at the heart or the head — with head elevation, heart-level MAP overestimates cerebral-level perfusion pressure. These errors bite during transport, repositioning, and head-elevation changes. A functioning monitor adds information; it never replaces serial neurological assessment.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Recognize and stabilize: herniation signs → rescue treatment now; ABCs, HOB 30–45°, prevent hypotension/hypoxia; urgent CT in parallel, neurosurgery called early.
    2. Tier 0 on everyone (Step 1, first box): positioning, analgesia/sedation, normocapnia, CPP support, normothermia, seizure treatment.
    3. Osmotherapy early (Step 1, second box): hypertonic saline first for most patients; mannitol when saline is unsuitable. Drain CSF if an EVD is in place; deepen sedation.
    4. Still >22 mmHg (or herniating)? → Step 2: mild hyperventilation as a bridge only, MAP challenge, neuromuscular blockade trial, continuous 3% saline.
    5. Refractory? → Step 3: barbiturate coma, decompressive craniectomy, ± mild hypothermia — with the family conversation about the mortality/disability trade-off.
    6. Disposition: neurocritical care unit; treat the cause (evacuate the lesion, drain the hydrocephalus, secure the aneurysm) — the tiers buy time, they are not the treatment.

    Workup¶

    • Urgent noncontrast head CT (hemorrhage, swelling, hydrocephalus, surgical mass lesion); repeat with any unexplained change. MRI after the immediate crisis is controlled.
    • Labs: BMP with sodium and chloride, measured serum osmolality + osmolar gap (osmotherapy monitoring — see the table), CBC, coagulation studies, glucose; ABG for PaCO₂ confirmation (EtCO₂ underestimates PaCO₂).
    • Continuous EEG when seizures are suspected, with neuromuscular blockade (paralysis hides seizures), or in barbiturate coma (burst-suppression endpoint).
    • No LP for opening pressure in this population (see the warning above).

    Management¶

    Step 1 — Tier 0: general measures (every patient, immediately)

    • Head of bed 30–45°, neck midline, loose cervical collar and ETT ties — protect venous outflow.
    • Analgesia + sedation: fentanyl infusion with propofol or midazolam — lowers ICP, cuts metabolic demand, prevents ventilator dyssynchrony. (Propofol dosing and cautions: Step 2 box.)
    • Normocapnia: PaCO₂ 35–40 mmHg. Keep SpO₂ >94%; avoid hypoxia.
    • CPP 60–70 mmHg, MAP >70, euvolemia, avoid hypotonic fluids.
    • Normothermia (<38 °C), glucose control, treat seizures; consider EEG — subclinical seizures raise ICP.

    Step 1 — Tier 1: osmotherapy + CSF drainage + deeper sedation

    Give osmotherapy for ICP >22 mmHg sustained, or immediately for herniation signs. Default agent: hypertonic saline (choice rationale and full comparison in the table below).

    • Hypertonic saline 3% — 250 mL IV over 15–30 min (≈2–5 mL/kg), peripheral access acceptable; or 23.4% — 30 mL over 10–20 min, central line only. Titrate doses to ICP.
    • Mannitol 20% — 0.5–1 g/kg IV over 15–30 min for a crisis (in-line filter); maintenance 0.25–0.5 g/kg every 4–6 h.
    • CSF drainage if an EVD is in place (EVD details: Step 2 box). If the monitor is intraparenchymal only, consider placing a ventriculostomy.
    • Deepen sedation and analgesia.

    → ICP controlled → hold the tier, monitor, wean deliberately. Still >22 mmHg → Step 2.

    Step 2 — Tier 2: bridge and augment

    • Mild hyperventilation to PaCO₂ 32–35 mmHg — bridge only (see the warning box: rescue rules, 24-hour TBI caution, slow wean).
    • MAP challenge to test autoregulation and individualize the CPP target within 60–70.
    • Neuromuscular blockade — trial dose first: cisatracurium 0.1–0.2 mg/kg IV bolus; continue as an infusion (1–3 mcg/kg/min) only if the trial lowers ICP, and stop as soon as feasible. (Rocuronium ~0.6–1 mg/kg is the alternative bolus.) Mandatory deep sedation + analgesia, ideally processed-EEG verified; train-of-four target 1–2 twitches; cEEG (paralysis hides seizures); pupils become the motor exam. Its most consistent benefit is blunting ICP surges from coughing, suctioning, dyssynchrony, or posturing.
    • Continuous 3% saline infusion — ~0.5–1 mL/kg/h, titrated to a goal sodium (commonly 145–155) — the CREVICE approach. Note: the NCS endorses bolus therapy and accepts goal-sodium infusion, but does not endorse a standard adult infusion regimen; the rate above is published adult practice, not a guideline dose. Continuous hyperosmolar therapy also has no proven outcome benefit — the COBI trial of continuous 20% saline in moderate-to-severe TBI found no improvement in 6-month neurologic outcome (adjusted common OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.71–1.47) — so an infusion is a bridge for ICP control, not a therapy in itself. Wean gradually (below).
    • EVD drainage strategy: a closed system with intermittent drainage (drain only when ICP exceeds threshold — typically 22 mmHg; SIBICC uses 18–22) preserves continuous ICP measurement; continuous drainage controls ICP more effectively, but then measure ICP intermittently by clamping or with a separate parenchymal monitor. Level the transducer at the tragus; drain height usually 10–20 cm H₂O above it. Removing even small CSF volumes lowers ICP sharply (steep pressure–volume curve). Complications: tract hemorrhage in ~15%, malposition, ventriculitis — intermittent drainage and early weaning mean fewer complications.

    → Still refractory → Step 3. Any herniation sign at any point → rescue (osmotherapy ± brief PaCO₂ <30) while definitive treatment is arranged.

    Step 3 — Tier 3: refractory ICP (salvage tier — time-limited, goals-of-care aware)

    • Barbiturate coma (pentobarbital) — only for ICP refractory despite maximal medical and surgical treatment, and only in a hemodynamically stable patient. Never prophylactically to induce burst suppression; SIBICC starts based on response to a test dose. Load 10 mg/kg IV over 30 min, then 5 mg/kg/h for 3 h, then 1–4 mg/kg/h. Endpoint is ICP control, not a serum level or the EEG — run continuous EEG and stop escalating once burst suppression appears (ICP will not fall further; toxicity will). Arterial line + cardiac output monitoring; strictly avoid hypotension.
    • Decompressive craniectomy — see the evidence section below; if done for TBI, a large frontotemporoparietal flap (≥12 × 15 cm).
    • Mild hypothermia 35–36 °C — an option in the refractory tier at some centers; not a substitute for the two measures above.
    • Propofol (from Step 1 sedation, deepened): target RASS −3 to −5. Start 5–20 mcg/kg/min, titrate to 50 mcg/kg/min, up to 75–80 mcg/kg/min (≈4.5–5 mg/kg/h); small boluses (0.5 mg/kg) can blunt an acute spike — only with hypotension anticipated and covered by a vasopressor. Propofol lowers ICP but has not been shown to improve mortality or 6-month outcome, and high doses cause significant harm.

    Hypertonic saline vs mannitol — the choice (nudge: saline first)¶

    The Neurocritical Care Society 2020 cerebral edema guideline prefers hypertonic saline over mannitol for raised ICP in TBI and ICH (weak/conditional recommendation, low- to very-low-quality evidence — in SAH it specifically favors symptom-triggered HTS boluses over sodium-target regimens); in ischemic stroke either agent is acceptable. SIBICC, by contrast, endorses neither over the other and applies the same sodium/osmolality limits to both. Two honest caveats: no trial has shown a mortality or functional-outcome difference between the agents, and laboratory safety limits are not treatment goals — the ICP and examination response guides therapy, and routinely driving sodium to a preset high value has not been shown to improve neurologic outcomes.

    Parameter Hypertonic saline Mannitol (20%)
    Bolus 23.4%: 30 mL over 10–20 min, central line only. 3%: 250 mL, or ~2–5 mL/kg over 15–30 min, peripheral acceptable. (7.5%/5% also used.) Doses titrated to ICP 0.5–1 g/kg IV over 15–30 min for a crisis; maintenance 0.25–0.5 g/kg q4–6h (FDA label: 0.25 g/kg over ≥30 min q6–8h). In-line filter
    Continuous infusion 3% at ~0.5–1 mL/kg/h, titrated to goal sodium (commonly 145–155); NCS accepts goal-sodium infusion or symptom-based boluses, but endorses no standard adult infusion regimen Not recommended — accumulates in injured brain, may cause rebound edema
    Mechanism Raises osmolality directly and expands intravascular volume Osmotic diuresis + rheologic effect; onset 10–15 min, peak 20–60 min, lasts 1–5 h
    Sodium target Na 145–155 mEq/L; little added benefit above 160 Not a direct target (diuresis can raise sodium)
    Usual limits SIBICC (both agents): Na <155, osmolality <320. Higher Na tolerated in crises; keep sustained Na <160 (DVT risk) and <170 (thrombocytopenia, anemia) Osmolar gap ≥20 is the usual hold point (accumulation, renal risk) — the gap is the NCS-preferred marker, but the NCS validates no specific cutoff; osmolality 320 is a traditional ceiling
    Monitoring Na every 4–6 h, osmolality, chloride (hyperchloremic acidosis), volume status, cardiopulmonary status (pulmonary edema), creatinine Osmolality + osmolar gap (draw ≥20 min after a dose, or the gap is falsely high), BMP, creatinine, urine output, volume status; replace losses to prevent hypovolemia
    Main risks Volume overload, CHF/pulmonary edema, hyperchloremia, osmotic demyelination if baseline Na is low, rebound edema if sodium is dropped quickly Hypovolemia and hypotension (lowers CPP), AKI, electrolyte loss, rebound ICP

    Why saline wins for most patients: (1) larger and faster ICP reduction — meta-analyses show HTS lowers ICP more than equiosmolar mannitol, and it can work after mannitol has failed; (2) it keeps volume and CPP up instead of diuresing — decisive in TBI with hypotension and in SAH. Mannitol remains reasonable when saline is unsuitable (e.g., volume overload, hypernatremia already at limit) — and a hypotensive patient is precisely the one mannitol can hurt.

    Osmotherapy cautions that apply to both: the Na/osmolality limits are not absolute — extra doses can still lower ICP, so weigh herniation risk against toxicity rather than withholding rescue at a number. Wean HTS gradually — let sodium fall no faster than ~8–10 mEq/L per 24 h to avoid rebound edema.

    Avoid

    • Prolonged or prophylactic hyperventilation — vasoconstriction reduces CBF and risks ischemia (worst where autoregulation is impaired); avoid hyperventilation in the first 24 h after TBI, when CBF is lowest. Mild hypocapnia (32–35) is a Tier-2 bridge (one review suggests a maximum of ~2 h; others advise reversing promptly once ICP stabilizes); PaCO₂ <30 is rescue-only — refractory ICP or impending herniation (e.g., a dilated unreactive pupil), as a bridge to osmotherapy or surgery, for as short a time as possible. Confirm with an ABG; ideally guide with PbtO₂ or SjvO₂. Wean over hours — abrupt normalization causes rebound ICP (CSF bicarbonate adaptation).
    • Prophylactic mannitol in ICH — not shown to help; may promote hematoma expansion.
    • Corticosteroids for TBI, ICH, or stroke edema — no role. (Steroids may be used for tumor vasogenic edema; in brain abscess, reserve them for severe edema/impending herniation under specialist guidance, rather than routine use.)
    • Hypotonic fluids and any avoidable hypotension or hypoxia — each is a secondary-insult multiplier.
    • Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS): risk rises at >4 mg/kg/h for >48 h, with catecholamine or corticosteroid co-therapy, young age, and low carbohydrate intake. Features: unexplained metabolic (lactic) acidosis, rhabdomyolysis (rising CK), hyperkalemia, hypertriglyceridemia, AKI, Brugada-like ECG changes or arrhythmias, refractory cardiac failure. Check lactate, CK, triglycerides, K⁺, creatinine at baseline and every 24–48 h (more often at high dose); watch for pancreatitis; count the lipid calories. If PRIS is suspected: stop propofol immediately, supportive care, consider ECMO or RRT.
    • Barbiturate coma pitfalls: hypotension and myocardial depression (expect pressors/ inotropes), immunosuppression (pneumonia, sepsis), ileus, hypokalemia during infusion with rebound hyperkalemia on withdrawal, hepatic dysfunction, hypothermia — and the loss of the neurological exam for days (very long context-sensitive half-life; pupils may become small or fixed; brain death cannot be assessed until levels clear). Taper; do not stop abruptly.
    • Continuous paralysis beyond need: longer ICU stays and more pneumonia, with no neurologic benefit; plus ICU-acquired weakness (worse with steroids), VTE, pressure injury, and corneal injury.

    TRICARE — verified rules and the current form

    Emergency stabilization and inpatient treatment must proceed through the clinical/facility pathway. Outpatient drugs, prolonged home infusions and follow-up services may need separate benefit authorization. The official list flags Xifaxan 550 mg for PA; exact indication and quantity rules matter. Do not extrapolate this to every rifaximin strength or to all ICU drugs.

    Decompressive craniectomy — the evidence, honestly stated¶

    Decompression reliably lowers ICP and saves lives across conditions; whether it improves function depends on the disease, the timing, and the patient's age. Every trial shows the same trade: death is traded for survival with dependency — so the patient's pre-injury function and likely values drive consent.

    DECRA (2011) — early RESCUEicp (2016) — late, last-tier
    Population Diffuse TBI, no mass lesion, within 72 h TBI aged 10–65, within 10 days; mostly diffuse injury
    ICP trigger >20 mmHg for >15 min within 1 h despite Tier-1 therapy (early, modest elevation) >25 mmHg for 1–12 h despite 2 tiers of therapy (late, refractory)
    Operation Bifrontal Bifrontal (most) or unilateral
    Mortality No difference 6 mo: 26.9% vs 48.9%; 24 mo: 33.5% vs 54.0% (DC vs medical)
    Function Worse GOS-E at 6 mo; still favored medical care at 12 mo, but no longer significant More vegetative state and severe disability. Favorable outcome (upper severe disability or better): 42.8% vs 34.6% at 6 mo (NS); 45.4% vs 32.4% at 12 mo (P=.01)
    ICP/ICU Lower ICP, shorter ICU stay Same

    The RESCUEicp trade-off, per 100 patients treated surgically: 21 more alive at 24 months — of these, 4 vegetative, 9 with severe disability, 8 with moderate disability. Good recovery was the same in both arms (~11%). Many survivors kept improving between 6 and 24 months (30% vs 14% improved by at least one GOS-E grade) — judge the outcome late, not at discharge. Note that "favorable outcome" in RESCUEicp included upper severe disability (independent at home for part of the day).

    Guideline position (BTF 2020 severe-TBI update): secondary DC is recommended for late refractory ICP elevation (the RESCUEicp scenario) and advised against for early refractory elevation (the DECRA scenario) when the goal is improved outcome. These recommendations do not apply to primary DC (flap left off while evacuating a mass lesion); for acute subdural hematoma, RESCUE-ASDH found no functional difference between primary DC and craniotomy, with different complication profiles.

    Malignant MCA infarction: in the pooled DECIMAL, DESTINY, and HAMLET trials, early hemicraniectomy reduced death (OR 0.19) and death or mRS >4 at 12 months (OR 0.26); a Cochrane analysis of 9 RCTs found death or severe disability with an OR of 0.22. Timing is the lever: surgery ideally within 48 h of onset and before herniation gives the largest benefit. Age splits the outcome: benefit for both survival and function is established under 60; over 60 (DESTINY II), mortality still falls, but a larger share of survivors are severely disabled (mRS 4–5).

    Intracerebral hemorrhage: AHA/ASA (2022) — decompressive hemicraniectomy, with or without clot evacuation, may be considered to reduce mortality in supratentorial ICH when the patient is comatose, has a large hematoma with midline shift, or has ICP refractory to medical therapy; functional benefit is uncertain. The SWITCH trial (DC without evacuation for deep ICH 30–100 mL, ages 18–75, within 72 h) stopped early; its primary outcome trended toward benefit but was not significant (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.59–1.01); the remaining evidence is small RCTs and retrospective series. Cerebellar ICH is different: urgent suboccipital evacuation if the patient is deteriorating, the brainstem is compressed, or there is hydrocephalus — an EVD alone is not adequate.

    Other space-occupying lesions (standard practice): traumatic EDH/SDH — evacuate if the clot is large or there is significant shift, the GCS is falling, or a pupil becomes abnormal (common thresholds: EDH >30 mL, SDH >10 mm thick). Space-occupying cerebellar infarction — suboccipital decompression is well established. Tumors, abscesses, and hydrocephalus — treat the cause (resection, drainage, EVD or shunt); steroids for tumor vasogenic edema.

    When to refer to neurosurgery — early, before you are sure¶

    Benefit in stroke and mass lesions depends on timing, so refer immediately or within the first hours for:

    • Any TBI with GCS ≤8, an operable mass lesion, or CT showing midline shift or effaced cisterns — for ICP-monitoring or evacuation consideration.
    • Clinical herniation: new fixed or dilated pupil, GCS drop ≥2 points, Cushing response.
    • ICP sustained above 20–22 mmHg despite Tier-1 therapy — so Tier-2 options and RESCUEicp-type decompression are considered before the patient becomes futile.
    • Large MCA infarct at diagnosis (>50% of the MCA territory on CT, or DWI volume >~145 mL), not after deterioration — especially under age 60; and any cerebellar infarct or hemorrhage with mass effect or hydrocephalus.
    • Supratentorial ICH with coma, shift, falling GCS, or hydrocephalus (for EVD); any cerebellar ICH ≥15 mL, or smaller with deterioration/compression/hydrocephalus.

    Disposition¶

    • Admit: neurocritical care ICU for any monitored patient, any patient on osmotherapy or a Step 2–3 therapy, and any patient with herniation signs — with neurosurgery engaged. If the facility cannot provide monitoring/decompression, transfer early; monitor placement should accompany the treatment pathway, never become a prerequisite for rescue treatment.
    • Escalation conversations: the Step-3 tier trades death for survival with disability — document pre-injury function and the patient's values before decompression or barbiturate coma whenever time allows.
    • Weaning: ICP controlled ≥24 h → wean in reverse order of escalation, one step at a time; HTS sodium falls ≤8–10 mEq/L per 24 h; hyperventilation normalizes over hours; barbiturates taper.

    Military medicine — duty, retention and deployment

    • Profile / function: Resuscitate and transfer to appropriate neurologic/critical care capability. Do not postpone treatment for an administrative readiness decision.
    • Retention / MEB–MAR2: A temporary acute restriction does not establish permanent unfitness. After recovery, use the diagnosed cause and residual function to determine profile, retention and rehabilitation needs. Use the retention framework; permanent P3/4 does not by itself select DES versus MAR2.
    • Deployment / medications: Active coma, delirium or intracranial hypertension is incompatible with routine operational duty. Reassess only after stabilization; document the underlying disease and all continuing drugs. See the disease AND drug screen for the current CENTCOM rules and waiver packet.
    • Special duty: flight, diving and other special assignments have separate standards and waiver authorities; a general duty profile does not clear them.
    • LOD / EPTS: document onset, duty status, exposures and aggravation objectively. The designated authority determines LOD/EPTS; neither in-service diagnosis nor a genetic finding settles it automatically.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    • Hypertonic saline is the default osmotic agent now — by guideline, not just habit. The NCS 2020 cerebral edema guideline prefers HTS over mannitol in TBI and ICH (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence); SIBICC stays neutral between the agents but standardizes the tiers and the shared Na <155 / osmolality <320 limits. No trial shows an outcome difference between the agents — the preference rests on ICP-lowering efficacy and on saline's volume/CPP profile. Treat the ICP and the exam, not the sodium number.
    • The 22 / 60–70 pair is BTF-anchored and operationalized by ACS 2024: treat ICP >22 mmHg (Level IIB), CPP 60–70 (IIB), never force CPP >70 (III), with age-banded SBP floors (≥100 at 50–69 y; ≥110 at 15–49 or >70 y, III). ACS frames 20–25 mmHg sustained >5 min as the practical trigger and ≥60 as the CPP floor adjusted for autoregulation.
    • Herniation is a clinical diagnosis that outruns the monitor. Pupillary dilation is only ~28% sensitive for elevated ICP, papilledema may be absent acutely, and compartmental displacement can precede any rise at a frontal monitor — the exam and the CT cisterns (OR ~9.6 when effaced) carry the early signal.
    • Decompression is two different operations in the evidence. Early bifrontal DC for modest ICP elevation (DECRA) worsened function; late last-tier DC for refractory hypertension (RESCUEicp) cut 24-month mortality from 54.0% to 33.5% at the price of more survivors with severe disability — with continued improvement out to 24 months. In malignant MCA stroke the calculus is better (pooled OR 0.19 for death) but only early (<48 h) and mainly under 60.
    • Monitoring has boundaries: routine ICP monitoring is not supported in large hemispheric infarction (ESO) or acute liver failure (SCCM 2023), while EVD drainage for hydrocephalus in ICH/IVH and SAH is a Class 1 treatment, not a monitoring choice.
