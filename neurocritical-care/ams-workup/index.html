    Altered mental status (AMS) — workup & management¶

    Last reviewed: November 2026

    One-liner: AMS in the adult inpatient or ED — the on-call neuro / admitting-team page: stabilize and reverse the reversible in the first hour, sort delirium from dementia from primary psychiatric illness at the bedside, image by the 2024 ACR criteria, then run the tiered workup for unexplained AMS and treat the cause, not the agitation.

    🚩 Red flags — image now, protect the airway, don't anchor on 'delirium'

    Pattern Worry Do now
    GCS ≤8, lost airway reflexes, hypoventilation Airway loss, aspiration, herniation Airway now; NCCT head emergently; see Coma
    Focal deficit, gaze deviation, aphasia, new seizure Stroke, hemorrhage, NCSE NCCT now (ACR Variant 1); EEG/cEEG early — a normal CT does not exclude stroke or NCSE
    Fever + meningismus, immunocompromise, encephalitis phenotype Meningitis / encephalitis LP + empiric acyclovir ± bacterial antibiotics now — do not wait for MRI or EEG
    Severe hyponatremia with somnolence or seizures Cerebral edema / herniation 3% saline bolus (Step 1 below); ICU
    Hypoglycemia, opioid toxidrome (miosis + bradypnea) Reversible, immediately lethal POC glucose, D50, naloxone — before imaging
    AMS persisting despite treating the presumed cause Missed structural / epileptic / infectious cause NCCT "Usually Appropriate" (ACR Variant 4) + MRI + EEG + LP — do not re-label it "toxic-metabolic"

    Diagnosis / classification¶

    AMS is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. The first classification decision is delirium vs dementia vs primary psychiatric illness — and the discriminator is acute, fluctuating inattention, not confusion alone.

    DSM-5-TR delirium — all five criteria must be met¶

    1. A — Disturbance in attention and awareness (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain, and shift attention).
    2. B — Acute onset (hours to a few days), a change from baseline, and fluctuation during the day.
    3. C — An additional cognitive disturbance (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability, or perception).
    4. D — Not better explained by a preexisting, established, or evolving neurocognitive disorder, and not occurring in the context of a severely reduced level of arousal (coma).
    5. E — Evidence that the disturbance is a direct physiological consequence of another medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, a medication, toxin exposure, or multiple causes.

    Specify: cause type, acute vs persistent course, and activity level — hyperactive, hypoactive, or mixed.

    Bedside tools — CAM and 4AT¶

    CAM (Confusion Assessment Method) — positive = feature 1 + feature 2 + either 3 or 4:

    Feature What to test
    1. Acute onset and/or fluctuating course Collateral: is this new vs baseline? Does it wax and wane?
    2. Inattention Months of the year backward (failing before 7 months is abnormal); CASABLANCA letter test
    3. Disorganized thinking Rambling, illogical, unpredictable conversation
    4. Altered level of consciousness Anything other than alert — use RASS for arousal

    CAM performance: original validation ~94–100% sensitivity, 90–95% specificity (trained assessors); pooled meta-analysis sensitivity 82% (95% CI 69–91%), specificity 99% (87–100%). A positive CAM is compelling; a negative CAM does not exclude delirium — especially with a brief exam, fluctuating illness, or hypoactive delirium.

    4AT (~2 minutes, no special training; score 0–12):

    Item 0 +1 +2 +4
    Alertness Normal — — Clearly abnormal (drowsy or agitated)
    AMT4 (age, DOB, place, current year) 0 errors 1 error ≥2 errors or untestable —
    Attention (months backward) ≥7 correct <7 or refuses Untestable —
    Acute change / fluctuating course (within 2 weeks, still present in last 24 h) No — — Yes

    ≥4 → delirium likely. 1–3 → possible cognitive impairment (e.g. dementia), warrants follow-up. 0 → delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely — but without a full history of recent change/fluctuation, delirium cannot be excluded. 4AT performance: pooled sensitivity 88% (80–93%), specificity 88% (82–92%) across 17 studies (3,702 assessments of older adults); in hospitalized older adults, 4AT 76%/89% vs CAM 80%/98%. Reassess serially — delirium fluctuates.

    Delirium vs dementia vs primary psychiatric illness¶

    Feature Delirium Dementia (major NCD) Primary psychiatric illness
    Onset Acute (hours–days), from a known baseline Insidious (months–years) Subacute; prior psychiatric history
    Course Fluctuates through the day Steady, progressive decline Episodic or persistent, not fluctuating hour-to-hour
    Attention Cardinally impaired — cannot sustain attention Preserved until late disease Intact (except in severe mania/psychosis)
    Arousal / consciousness Altered (hyper- or hypoalert) Normal until late Clear sensorium, intact orientation
    Memory / thought Impaired, disorganized Impaired, but attention intact early Thought disorder / mood symptoms, memory largely intact
    Hallucinations Often visual, complex Uncommon early (except DLB) Often auditory; modality is not diagnostic
    Sleep–wake Fragmented/reversed (~75%); disturbed in 97–99% Often preserved early Disturbed, but pattern differs
    Trigger Acute medical/toxic cause required (DSM criterion E) None — neurodegenerative None — primary psychiatric disorder

    Three questions that prioritize the bedside: (1) Is this an acute change from baseline? (2) Can the patient sustain attention? (3) Does cognition or arousal fluctuate? If all three point toward delirium, apparent hallucinations, paranoia, depression, or agitation — or underlying dementia — should not divert the evaluation toward a purely psychiatric or chronic cognitive diagnosis.

    Subtypes and traps¶

    • Hypoactive delirium is the easiest to miss and the most common form in older adults — and it carries higher mortality. A quiet patient who "just seems sleepy," depressed, fatigued, or poorly cooperative can have severe delirium. Hypoactive delirium mimics depression: in one study, a third of acute-care psychiatry consults for depression were actually hypoactive delirium.
    • Hyperactive delirium (increased arousal, hypervigilance, agitation, pulling lines/tubes, wandering, loud speech, hallucinations/delusions) mimics mania or psychosis.
    • Mixed alternates between hyperactive and hypoactive states, sometimes over hours (agitated overnight, markedly somnolent the next morning).
    • Delirium superimposed on dementia (DSD) is common — ~50% of hospitalized patients with dementia. Dementia does not exclude delirium; do not diagnose a new neurocognitive disorder while the patient is delirious — manage the delirium first.
    • Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the key exception: cognitive fluctuation + visual hallucinations mimic delirium. The discriminating question is whether there is a new acute change from an established baseline.
    • New-onset "psychiatric" symptoms in an older patient, or anyone with abnormal vitals, disorientation, or visual hallucinations, should be presumed medical until proven otherwise.
    • EEG supports the distinction: diffuse slowing in delirium, usually normal in primary psychiatric illness. Collateral history is the single most important step — a missing baseline is a leading reason delirium goes undiagnosed.

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Stabilize and reverse the reversible in the first hour — ABCs, POC glucose, thiamine, naloxone if the toxidrome fits. Do not wait for imaging to treat hypoglycemia or opioid toxicity.
    2. Classify at the bedside — CAM or 4AT + collateral baseline + RASS. Acute, fluctuating inattention = delirium until proven otherwise.
    3. Image by the 2024 ACR criteria (table below) — GCS 9–14 alone is not an intubation indication, and there is no validated decision rule for skipping imaging; serial reassessment is the qualifier for deferral.
    4. If CT and first-line labs leave the cause unexplained, run the tiered workup in parallel within ~24 h — EEG/cEEG, MRI, LP, targeted second-line labs.
    5. Treat the cause; manage agitation only as rescue — nonpharmacologic bundle first, antipsychotic only for dangerous agitation, benzodiazepines only for withdrawal/seizures.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. 0–10 min: ABCs, vitals, pulse ox, POC glucose. If glucose low → D50 (Step 1). If opioid toxidrome → naloxone (Step 1). Thiamine with dextrose, never delaying it. GCS, pupils, focal exam, meningismus, toxidrome screen.
    2. 10–30 min: CAM/4AT + collateral (baseline cognition, meds, alcohol, recent changes). First-line labs (below). Noncontrast head CT by ACR variant. Stop/avoid deliriogenic drugs (benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, opioids, sedative-hypnotics).
    3. 30–60 min: If a cause is found → treat it (Step 1) and reassess. If CT is unrevealing and AMS persists → Tier 1 in parallel: EEG/cEEG, MRI, LP if indicated, Tier 2 labs. If encephalitis/meningitis is plausible → LP + empiric acyclovir ± antibiotics now.
    4. Hours 6–24: cEEG ≥24 h if comatose or fluctuating; MRI within 24–48 h once stable. Nonpharmacologic bundle running from admission. Antipsychotic only if dangerous agitation persists after de-escalation and cause treatment.
    5. Day 1–3: CSF autoimmune/specialty results, expanded toxicology, Tier 3 by pattern. Reassess daily with CAM/4AT; stop inpatient antipsychotics before discharge; document baseline cognition and arrange cognitive follow-up.

    Workup¶

    First-hour labs — no single mandatory AMS panel; phenotype-driven¶

    Order the core set, then add by phenotype: CBC, CMP (incl. Ca/Mg), glucose, BUN/creatinine, LFTs, ABG/VBG if hypercapnia suspected, UA/culture, ECG, CXR if indicated, toxicology. Add ammonia only when hepatic encephalopathy is plausible (cirrhosis, valproate, acute liver failure); CK for hyperthermia, prolonged seizure, or severe agitation. Caution: pyuria/bacteriuria alone does not explain AMS — do not anchor on a positive UA.

    Imaging — 2024 ACR Appropriateness Criteria® (Soares et al., JACR 2024)¶

    Variant Scenario Noncontrast CT MRI Contrast CT / MRI
    1 Suspected intracranial pathology / focal deficit Usually Appropriate (first-line) May Be Appropriate Usually Not Appropriate
    2 Known intracranial pathology Usually Appropriate Usually Appropriate —
    3 Suspected medical / toxic-metabolic, nonfocal, low likelihood of intracranial pathology May Be Appropriate (Disagreement) May Be Appropriate (Disagreement) Usually Not Appropriate
    4 AMS persisting despite treating the presumed cause Usually Appropriate May Be Appropriate —

    Context: CT yield is low but not zero — ~11% of atraumatic AMS (79,201 patients) and 9.8% of AMS CT orders show an acute finding. In febrile older adults, lower GCS, lateralizing signs, higher SBP, and lower temperature predicted abnormal imaging. In elderly new-onset delirium without focal findings, CT yield is very low — but a normal CT never closes the case: CT-occult stroke, NCSE, and encephalitis are the three major treatable neurologic diagnoses a normal CT misses.

    Tiered workup after unexplained AMS (CT + first-line labs unrevealing)¶

    Run Tier 1 in parallel within the first 24 hours, ordered by danger — not one test after another.

    Tier 0 — recheck in the first hours (before you call it unexplained)

    • Recheck POC glucose, blood gas (hypercapnia), sodium/calcium trends.
    • Full medication review — sedatives, anticholinergics, cefepime, opioids, valproate, lithium, alcohol, supplements.
    • Re-examine: focal signs, meningismus, fluctuation, subtle twitching, nystagmus or gaze deviation, toxidrome signs.

    Tier 1 — EEG / cEEG, MRI, LP (parallel, usually within 24 h)

    • EEG → cEEG: unexplained or fluctuating AMS; still impaired >30 min after a clinical seizure or not clearly improving by 10 min; periodic discharges (GPDs, LPDs, BIPDs) or LRDA on routine EEG; awake EEG; paralyzed or heavily sedated patients. Urgent, same day; cEEG ≥24 h if comatose (a 20–30 min routine study misses many seizures). A conventional 30–60 min EEG identifies only 45–58% of patients who ultimately have NCSE; 80–95% are detected within 24–48 h of cEEG. ACNS recommends ≥24 h for most indications, ≥48 h if comatose, sedated, or with periodic discharges. NCSE occurs in 8–30% of unexplained AMS, most with no visible clinical seizure.
      • Finding NCSE → benzodiazepines + second-line antiseizure drug. Triphasic / diffuse slowing supports a toxic-metabolic cause. Extreme delta brush suggests anti-NMDAR encephalitis. A normal routine awake EEG does not establish a psychiatric cause or exclude intermittent seizures or encephalitis; integrate the examination, sampling duration, MRI and CSF findings.
    • MRI brain (DWI, FLAIR, SWI, ± gadolinium): normal CT with persistent unexplained AMS, focal deficit, immunosuppression, cancer, endocarditis, suspected encephalitis. Within ~24–48 h once stable — sooner if posterior-circulation stroke (add MRA; MRV for venous thrombosis). For suspected encephalitis, ideally within 24 h and certainly within 48 h (EAN/ESCMID 2026). Picks up small/posterior infarcts, HSV or autoimmune encephalitis (mesial temporal FLAIR), subtle SAH, PRES, septic emboli, Wernicke, osmotic demyelination, CJD (cortical ribboning on DWI), diffuse axonal injury. In one ICU cohort MRI changed management in 76% of patients; yield is very low in plain delirium without focal deficit or falls.
    • LP: fever, meningismus, immunosuppression, suspected encephalitis or SAH, younger patients, or delirium that is persistent, unexpected, or unexplained. Right away if infection is suspected, after imaging excludes mass effect — and do not let the LP delay acyclovir/antibiotics. Opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose (with paired serum glucose), Gram stain/culture, HSV/VZV PCR or meningitis-encephalitis panel, cytology, xanthochromia, oligoclonal bands. Send paired serum/CSF autoimmune panels when the phenotype supports encephalitis; freeze extra CSF when feasible. Contraindications to immediate LP: significant mass effect/brain shift, severe coagulopathy/anticoagulation, local infection, clinical instability; evaluate for an intracranial process first if severe depressed consciousness, new seizures, focal abnormalities, papilledema, or severe immunocompromise. If the first LP is very early (within 3 days of symptom onset) and nondiagnostic while encephalitis remains likely, repeat in 2–3 days.

    Tier 2 — targeted second-line labs (send with Tier 1 when indicated)

    • Ammonia — free-flowing sample on ice, run within 30 min. A normal level makes hepatic encephalopathy unlikely; a high level does not prove it in cirrhosis (levels correlate poorly with severity). Hyperammonemia without cirrhosis → valproate, urea-cycle disorders, portosystemic shunts, urease-producing UTI. The 2026 ACG guideline recommends against routine ammonia testing to diagnose or guide treatment of hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis.
    • TSH + free T4 — screens for myxedema coma or thyroid storm. Thyroid antibodies are nonspecific and do not establish autoimmune encephalitis/SREAT; a steroid response is also nonspecific. Investigate a compatible phenotype and exclude mimics.
    • Cortisol ± cosyntropin — low threshold if hypotensive, hyponatremic, hypoglycemic, or steroid-exposed. Adrenal crisis → hydrocortisone 100 mg IV right away, no wait for results.
    • Toxicology — a standard urine drug screen misses synthetic cannabinoids, fentanyl on some panels, GHB, and many sedatives. Add serum acetaminophen, salicylate, ethanol, osmolal gap (toxic alcohols), carboxyhemoglobin, and levels of lithium, valproate, phenytoin, carbamazepine, digoxin; expanded LC-MS when the screen is negative but suspicion is high.
    • Other high-yield labs — thiamine (give empirically before results), B12, CK, lactate, calcium/magnesium/phosphate, ESR/CRP, HIV, RPR, blood cultures, lipase. Check the exam for serotonin syndrome or neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

    Tier 3 — low pretest probability, specific trigger only

    • Heavy metals (24-h urine or whole-blood lead, mercury, arsenic, thallium) — only with exposure history, neuropathy, GI symptoms, alopecia, anemia with basophilic stippling, or a subacute course. Not for acute screening. For lead, venous blood lead is the preferred test; for recent arsenic, 24-h urinary speciated arsenic; mercury testing is exposure-specific (urine for elemental/inorganic, blood for recent/methylmercury).
    • Autoimmune / paraneoplastic workup — serum + CSF antibodies, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET, pelvic ultrasound or MRI for ovarian teratoma. Use when CSF is inflammatory, MRI or EEG suggests encephalitis, or there is subacute memory loss, psychiatric change, faciobrachial dystonic seizures, or LGI1-related hyponatremia. Begin empiric immunotherapy early once infection is reasonably excluded.
    • CJD testing (CSF RT-QuIC, 14-3-3) — rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus or DWI cortical ribboning.
    • Lorazepam challenge — when catatonia is suspected: record baseline catatonic signs, give 1–2 mg IV with respiratory/sedation monitoring, and reassess at about 5 minutes with a standardized scale (e.g., BFCRS). Start at the lower end in frailty or respiratory risk. A response supports catatonia but is not specific; nonresponse does not exclude it. BAP consensus.

    Practical sequence¶

    Window Do
    Hours 0–6 Stabilize; give thiamine; review medications; recheck metabolic labs; send Tier 2 labs. If encephalitis possible → LP and start acyclovir now.
    Hours 6–24 EEG (→ cEEG if fluctuating, comatose, or routine study shows periodic/rhythmic patterns); obtain MRI.
    Day 1–3 CSF autoimmune and specialty results, expanded toxicology, Tier 3 tests based on pattern.

    Management¶

    The rule: manage delirium by finding and reversing the cause, not by sedating the patient. No drug is FDA-approved for delirium. Nonpharmacologic multicomponent care is the cornerstone. Antipsychotics are rescue drugs for selected behavioral symptoms — they do not shorten delirium or treat its cause.

    Step 1 — life-threatening, reversible causes in the first minutes

    • Hypoglycemia — check POC glucose at once. If low: dextrose 50% (D50) 25 g IV (label range 10–25 g IV = 20–50 mL D50), repeat as clinically required; or glucagon 1 mg IM if no IV. If able to swallow safely, 15 g rapid glucose PO, recheck after 15 min, repeat if still <70 mg/dL. Never delay glucose to give thiamine — the two can be given together. Look for recurrent hypoglycemia (insulin/sulfonylurea, renal/hepatic failure, poor intake, sepsis, adrenal disease) rather than assuming one bolus is definitive.
    • Opioid toxicity — suspect with miosis, bradypnea, recent dose escalation, or new renal failure. Naloxone 0.04–0.4 mg IV, titrated to respiratory effort and airway reflexes rather than to full arousal (limits withdrawal and pain crisis). Intranasal naloxone is commonly 4 mg/spray, repeatable every 2–3 min. Long-acting opioids or renal accumulation may need a naloxone infusion; observe for renarcotization.
    • Thiamine / Wernicke — suspect in alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, bariatric surgery, hyperemesis, or cancer. The classic triad is often incomplete — treat on suspicion, don't wait for levels or MRI. Thiamine 500 mg IV in 100 mL saline over 30 min, three times daily for 2–3 days; if responding, continue 250 mg IV/IM daily for 3–5 days, then 100 mg PO daily. Alternative: 200 mg IV three times daily, acceptable especially when the cause is not alcohol. Do not use 100–250 mg/day — it may not restore thiamine status, and undertreatment risks Korsakoff syndrome. For high-risk alcohol-associated deficiency without signs of Wernicke, use a specific prophylaxis protocol (e.g., 200–300 mg IM/IV daily for 3–5 days, then oral), rather than treatment-level doses by default. Also check and correct magnesium.
    • Hyponatremia, severe symptoms (somnolence, seizures) — 3% saline 100 mL IV over 10 min; repeat up to twice if severe symptoms persist (three boluses total), aiming for a 4–6 mEq/L rise. Check sodium after each bolus, then every 4–6 h. Correction limits: low risk ≤10 mEq/L in 24 h and ≤18 mEq/L in 48 h; high risk ≤8 mEq/L in any 24 h (high risk = Na <105, alcohol use disorder, hypokalemia, malnutrition, liver disease). Overcorrection → re-lower with D5W + desmopressin. (Recent observational data link slower correction to higher mortality, but conservative limits remain the consensus standard.)
    • Hyperammonemia / hepatic encephalopathy — find and treat precipitants: infection, GI bleeding, dehydration, psychoactive drugs, hyponatremia. Lactulose 60 mL PO, then 20 mL every 1–2 h until stooling begins, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day (acute overt HE: 10–20 g / 15–30 mL every 2 h until 2 soft bowel movements, then 2–4 doses/day). Rifaximin 550 mg PO twice daily — standard for preventing recurrence; adding it acutely may improve reversal and mortality. A high-volume PEG-electrolyte bowel-preparation regimen is an alternative in selected patients under the HE protocol; do not give PEG for suspected ileus or bowel obstruction, and protect the airway when needed. Do not titrate to serum ammonia — ammonia correlates poorly with severity in cirrhosis, and the 2026 ACG guideline recommends against using it to diagnose overt HE or guide inpatient treatment. Cirrhosis vs acute liver failure differ: in ALF or a urea-cycle disorder, marked hyperammonemia has different implications — the 2026 ACG guideline notes a possible role for early renal replacement therapy when ammonia is >100 μmol/L in ALF; there are insufficient data to dialyze cirrhotic patients solely to reduce ammonia. High-grade encephalopathy in ALF belongs in an ICU/transplant-capable setting.
    • → If the cause is found and treated, reassess with CAM/4AT — do not stop there if AMS persists; delirium is frequently multifactorial.

    Step 2 — systematic cause search + nonpharmacologic bundle (start on admission)

    • Medication review: stop or minimize benzodiazepines, sedative-hypnotics, anticholinergics, opioids, and review dopaminergic drugs. Do not abruptly stop levodopa or other essential Parkinson therapy: withdrawal can cause severe rigidity/hyperpyrexia. Include alcohol and supplements.
    • Fix the other contributors: hypoxemia, pain, urinary retention, constipation, severe anemia.
    • Nonpharmacologic bundle (the active treatment, not comfort care): multicomponent programs such as HELP cut delirium incidence by ~40–50% (Cochrane RR 0.57) and may shorten duration and length of stay. Orientation: clocks, calendars, frequent reorientation, family presence, a companion rather than a drug. Senses and sleep: glasses and hearing aids, adequate daytime light, sleep protocol — avoid nighttime vitals, noise, and sedative-hypnotics. Mobility and devices: early mobilization; remove catheters, lines, and other tethers; avoid physical restraints. Basic needs: hydration, nutrition with feeding help, bowel and bladder monitoring, pain control. ICU: the ABCDEF bundle. Restraints only when injury is imminent, with frequent monitoring and reassessment; use sitters/family/de-escalation first.
    • → If agitation is dangerous despite de-escalation, cause treatment, and the bundle → Step 3.

    Step 3 — rescue antipsychotic for dangerous agitation only (and the withdrawal exception)

    • Indication: only for distress or danger to self or others, or agitation that blocks essential care, after verbal/nonverbal de-escalation has failed and contributing factors are addressed. NICE limits use to short courses, usually ≤1 week. The published APA 2025 guideline discourages routine antipsychotics for prevention or resolution; SCCM’s 2025 focused update makes no recommendation for or against antipsychotics for ICU delirium treatment because evidence is uncertain — trials have not shown shorter delirium duration, ventilation duration, ICU stay, or mortality. Antipsychotics sedate rather than treat delirium and can convert hyperactive delirium to hypoactive delirium, which carries a worse prognosis.
    • Haloperidol — older or frail adults: 0.25–0.5 mg PO/IM, repeated every 4 h as needed (0.5–1 mg IM is also used). Younger adults or severe agitation: 0.5–2 mg PO or short-acting IM (up to 2–5 mg IM per dose). Reassess before each repeat; allow ~30–45 min for the dose to work, then use the same or a smaller incremental dose rather than stacking. Usual ceiling 20 mg/day IM. Goal is calm and safely redirectable, not obtunded. PRN, low-dose, time-limited — never standing. Stop as soon as the target behavior resolves.
    • IV haloperidol is off-label with a higher risk of QTc prolongation and torsades — use ECG monitoring.
    • Atypical alternatives (comparable efficacy in trials): quetiapine 12.5–25 mg, olanzapine 2.5–5 mg (PO/IM/ODT), risperidone 0.25–0.5 mg. No convincing evidence they cure delirium or consistently outperform haloperidol. If there is no dangerous agitation or distress — particularly in hypoactive delirium — there is generally no indication for an antipsychotic.
    • Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia: avoid haloperidol (contraindicated — severe neuroleptic sensitivity/parkinsonism). Use low-dose quetiapine.
    • Safety checks: baseline ECG; keep K >4 and Mg >2; stop if QTc >500 ms; avoid combining with other QT-prolonging drugs. Other risks: aspiration (~4-fold risk of aspiration pneumonia), falls, higher mortality. Watch for akathisia, which can mimic worsening delirium.
    • Mechanically ventilated ICU patients: dexmedetomidine has a distinct role — SCCM recommends it for delirious agitation precluding weaning/extubation, and the published APA guideline favors it over other sedating agents when sedation is needed during mechanical ventilation.
    • Benzodiazepines are the exception, not the rule: see the Avoid box below.

    Avoid

    • Benzodiazepines in ordinary medical/surgical delirium. The American Geriatrics Society advises against them as first-line treatment for agitated delirium (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). They increase the incidence of delirium (OR ~3), lengthen it, and predict transition to delirium in the ICU; risk rises with dose and half-life. In one comparative trial the lorazepam arm was stopped early for adverse effects, and a Cochrane review found no adequate trials supporting benzodiazepines for delirium not related to alcohol withdrawal. They cause additional inattention/amnesia, oversedation, falls, aspiration, respiratory depression, and sometimes paradoxical disinhibition.
    • Benzodiazepine exceptions: alcohol or sedative-hypnotic withdrawal, seizures/status epilepticus, and catatonia. Selected refractory agitation near the end of life may warrant benzodiazepines within a palliative-care plan; this is not a blanket first-line recommendation for all terminal delirium. Randomized rescue-treatment evidence. In alcohol withdrawal, benzodiazepines treat the underlying GABA/glutamate withdrawal physiology and reduce withdrawal seizures and delirium; symptom-triggered benzodiazepines are first-line, and antipsychotics alone are inadequate because they lower the seizure threshold. Haloperidol must not replace adequate benzodiazepine therapy in delirium tremens — if an antipsychotic is needed for refractory dangerous behavior during DT, it is an adjunct, not monotherapy.
    • Do not label persistent unexplained AMS "toxic–metabolic delirium" just because the CT is normal. CT-occult stroke, NCSE, and encephalitis are the three major treatable diagnoses that MRI, EEG, and LP respectively prevent missing.
    • Do not use serum ammonia to diagnose or grade hepatic encephalopathy in cirrhosis, and do not continue inpatient antipsychotics by default at discharge — they are often continued inadvertently.
    • Do not diagnose a new dementia while the patient is delirious, and do not divert unexplained delirium to a primary psychiatric disposition until medical/toxic/neurologic causes have been adequately addressed.

    TRICARE — verified rules and the current form

    Emergency stabilization and inpatient treatment must proceed through the clinical/facility pathway. Outpatient drugs, prolonged home infusions and follow-up services may need separate benefit authorization. The official list flags Xifaxan 550 mg for PA; exact indication and quantity rules matter. Do not extrapolate this to every rifaximin strength or to all ICU drugs.

    Disposition¶

    Situation Disposition
    New, persistent, or unexplained delirium; cause uncertain; active treatment or serial testing required; cannot safely maintain hydration/medications/mobility; no reliable supervision Admit — medical syndrome, not a psychiatric disposition
    Airway or ventilatory compromise, hemodynamic instability/shock, severe sepsis, recurrent opioid respiratory depression, status epilepticus, high-grade HE/acute liver failure, severe symptomatic hyponatremia on closely monitored hypertonic therapy, severe alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal or DTs, rapidly worsening neurologic findings, agitation requiring continuous titratable IV sedation or very high nursing intensity Monitored / ICU level — DTs specifically require an appropriately equipped acute hospital unit with close vital-sign and respiratory monitoring
    Cause simple and reversible (drug adverse effect, uncomplicated infection); evaluation can be timely; a reliable caregiver can report worsening Outpatient or hospital-at-home can suit select patients — hospitalization itself worsens delirium, especially with dementia or frailty
    Trigger identified and adequately treated (or safely manageable outside hospital); cognition/attention returned sufficiently toward baseline without dangerous fluctuation; physiologic parameters stable; reliable supervision and prompt follow-up exist Discharge — but first reassess any antipsychotic started for delirium rather than letting it become an unintended chronic medication; document baseline cognition, reconcile medications, arrange cognitive follow-up (delirium predicts long-term cognitive decline), consider geriatrics consultation. Delirium often persists for weeks after the cause is treated.

    Military medicine — duty, retention and deployment

    • Profile / function: Resuscitate and transfer to appropriate neurologic/critical care capability. Do not postpone treatment for an administrative readiness decision.
    • Retention / MEB–MAR2: A temporary acute restriction does not establish permanent unfitness. After recovery, use the diagnosed cause and residual function to determine profile, retention and rehabilitation needs. Use the retention framework; permanent P3/4 does not by itself select DES versus MAR2.
    • Deployment / medications: Active coma, delirium or intracranial hypertension is incompatible with routine operational duty. Reassess only after stabilization; document the underlying disease and all continuing drugs. See the disease AND drug screen for the current CENTCOM rules and waiver packet.
    • Special duty: flight, diving and other special assignments have separate standards and waiver authorities; a general duty profile does not clear them.
    • LOD / EPTS: document onset, duty status, exposures and aggravation objectively. The designated authority determines LOD/EPTS; neither in-service diagnosis nor a genetic finding settles it automatically.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    What this page covers: the first-hour approach to AMS, bedside classification with CAM/4AT, imaging by the 2024 ACR criteria, the tiered workup for unexplained AMS, and cause-first management with rescue-only antipsychotics.

    What the evidence says:

    • The 2024 ACR Appropriateness Criteria (Soares et al., JACR 2024) give a variant-based imaging framework for AMS/coma/delirium/psychosis: noncontrast CT is "Usually Appropriate" first-line when intracranial pathology is suspected or when AMS persists despite treating the presumed cause, but only "May Be Appropriate (Disagreement)" for nonfocal suspected medical/toxic-metabolic AMS — and there is no validated decision rule for skipping imaging.
    • CAM and 4AT are the bedside discriminators, and they perform well: pooled CAM sensitivity 82% / specificity 99%; pooled 4AT sensitivity 88% / specificity 88%. The clinical framework emphasizes that acute, fluctuating inattention is the decisive feature, and that a negative CAM does not exclude delirium.
    • EEG is not optional in unexplained AMS. NCSE occurs in 8–30% of unexplained AMS, most with no visible clinical seizure; a 30–60 min EEG detects only 45–58% of cases, while 80–95% are detected within 24–48 h of continuous EEG. Clinical practice emphasizes on cEEG ≥24 h when comatose.
    • The 2026 ACG hepatic encephalopathy guideline changed practice: do not use serum ammonia to diagnose overt HE or guide inpatient treatment in cirrhosis — treat the clinical syndrome and precipitants. The same guideline notes a possible role for early renal replacement therapy when ammonia is >100 μmol/L in acute liver failure, a distinction from cirrhosis.
    • Delirium management has shifted from "give Haldol" to cause-first care. Clinical practice emphasizes: no drug is FDA-approved for delirium; antipsychotics are rescue-only for dangerous agitation (haloperidol 0.25–0.5 mg in frail older adults, 0.5–2 mg in other adults, ceiling 20 mg/day IM); benzodiazepines worsen and prolong delirium (OR ~3) except in alcohol/sedative withdrawal, seizures, or end-of-life care; and multicomponent nonpharmacologic care (HELP: ~40–50% incidence reduction, Cochrane RR 0.57) is the cornerstone.
    • Hyponatremia correction limits are settled and consistent with the cited guidance: 3% saline 100 mL bolus for severe symptoms, 4–6 mEq/L rescue rise, ≤10 mEq/L/24 h (≤18/48 h) low risk, ≤8 mEq/L/24 h high risk — with D5W + desmopressin for overcorrection.
    • Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy needs prompt high-dose parenteral thiamine. Published protocols differ in dose/duration; this page uses a defined regimen with clinical reassessment. Treat suspicion promptly, replace magnesium when deficient and never delay correction of hypoglycemia.

    What remains uncertain:

    • The APA delirium guideline was published in 2025. The 2025 SCCM PADIS focused update adds context-specific recommendations, including dexmedetomidine over propofol when light sedation/delirium reduction is the priority; this is not a mandate for every ventilated patient. APA guideline; SCCM focused update.

    • Rifaximin 550 mg has an official pharmacy PA flag; obtain the indication-specific form and quantity rules from the TRICARE guide.

    • Acute delirium has no single universal retention outcome; determine cause and residual function after recovery.
    • See the neuroinfection page for empiric antimicrobial doses; adjust for organ function and local resistance.
