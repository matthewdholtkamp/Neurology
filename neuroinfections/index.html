    Neuroinfections¶

    Last reviewed: October 2026

    One-liner: fever plus a neurologic syndrome is a CNS infection until proven otherwise — start antimicrobials before the LP, and when the picture is subacute encephalopathy, run the infectious and autoimmune workups in parallel.

    Educational clinical decision support — not medical advice. Verify every dose against current references and local protocols before ordering.

    🚩 Red flags — treat first, prove later

    Pattern Worry Do now
    Fever + neck stiffness + altered mentation Acute bacterial meningitis Blood cultures, dexamethasone + antibiotics with the first dose — never delay for LP or CT
    Febrile encephalopathy + aphasia / focal deficit / seizures (temporal lobe) HSV encephalitis IV acyclovir immediately — do not wait for LP, MRI, or PCR
    Headache + fever + focal deficit or seizure Brain abscess Blood cultures, empiric abx, urgent neurosurgical + ID assessment
    HIV + subacute headache, often with raised ICP Cryptococcal meningitis LP with opening pressure; induction antifungals + serial therapeutic LPs
    Subacute meningitis + cranial neuropathies / hydrocephalus / low CSF glucose TB meningitis Treatment emergency — empiric HRZE after specimens; add dexamethasone
    Subacute memory loss / psychiatric change + seizures or movement disorder Autoimmune encephalitis MRI/EEG/LP + paired serum/CSF antibody panels; start immunotherapy at the probable stage — do not wait for antibodies

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Resuscitate and draw blood cultures ×2 — then start empiric antimicrobials immediately. Antimicrobials are never held for LP or imaging.
    2. Classify the syndrome: acute meningitic (hours–days) → bacterial; febrile encephalopathy → HSV; focal lesion with fever → abscess; HIV + raised ICP → cryptococcal; subacute/chronic meningitis → TB/fungal; subacute encephalopathy → autoimmune.
    3. Image when indicated, then LP when safe — and measure the opening pressure at every LP.
    4. Route to the syndrome orderset below; tailor once CSF, serology, and cultures return.
    5. Treat the complications in parallel: raised ICP, seizures, hydrocephalus, hyponatremia.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. Blood cultures → empiric antimicrobials + adjunctive steroids (where indicated) — now.
    2. CT head first only if indicated (immunocompromise, focal deficit, papilledema, seizure, depressed consciousness); otherwise straight to LP. Measure opening pressure.
    3. Send the syndrome-specific tests (CSF profile, PCR, cryptococcal antigen, Xpert Ultra, VDRL, antibody panels) — but do not wait for results to treat.
    4. Control pressure and seizures while antimicrobials work; get neurosurgery involved early for abscess, hydrocephalus, or uncontrolled ICP.
    5. Narrow and set the duration once the organism (or antibody) is known; arrange follow-up imaging, repeat LPs, and secondary prophylaxis where indicated.

    Acute bacterial meningitis (adults)¶

    Community-acquired. Dexamethasone and antibiotics ride in on the first dose together — steroids given later lose their benefit.

    Step 1 — empiric antibiotics + dexamethasone (before the LP if needed)

    • Blood cultures ×2 — draw first, but never delay antibiotics for them.
    • Dexamethasone 10 mg IV q6h — give before or with the first antibiotic dose, up to 4 days; stop it if CSF is not consistent with bacterial meningitis (NICE: give within 12 h of presentation if given at all).
    • Vancomycin (weight-based per local protocol — dose not captured in this run's research) + ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h — adults 18–50.
    • Add ampicillin 2 g IV q4h (Listeria cover) if age >50, pregnant, or immunocompromised.
    • Alternative cephalosporin: cefotaxime 2 g IV q4–6h.
    • CT head first if immunocompromised, focal deficit, papilledema, seizure, or depressed consciousness — then LP. Never delay antibiotics for CT or LP.

    18–50, immunocompetent Vancomycin + ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h
    >50, pregnant, or immunocompromised Above + ampicillin 2 g IV q4h
    Healthcare-associated / post-neurosurgical Per IDSA 2017 — vancomycin + antipseudomonal β-lactam; tailor fast

    TRICARE — meningitis prescribing

    IV ceftriaxone, vancomycin, ampicillin, and dexamethasone are inpatient ward stock — typically no prior authorization in the inpatient setting. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — narrow by organism; set the duration

    • Narrow to organism-directed therapy once cultures/susceptibilities return; keep source control (devices, sinuses, ears) moving in parallel.
    • Durations are guideline-specific, not interchangeable: NICE — 10 days if clinically recovered; WHO — 7 days conditional. Healthcare-associated ventriculitis/meningitis (IDSA 2017): 10–14 days for S. aureus or gram-negative bacilli (some experts: 10–14 days after the last positive culture), 21 days for gram-negative infection; with repeatedly positive CSF cultures, continue therapy.

    HSV encephalitis (adults)¶

    Febrile encephalopathy with confusion, personality/behavioral change, impaired consciousness, aphasia or another focal deficit, or new seizures — start acyclovir on suspicion. A normal early CSF cell count does not exclude it.

    Step 1 — empiric IV acyclovir (do not wait for LP, MRI, or PCR)

    • Acyclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h — start immediately; infuse each dose over ≥1 hour (never rapid/bolus push); maintain hydration; monitor renal function; adjust for renal impairment; dose by ideal body weight in obesity.
    • Duration 14–21 days: 14 days for an immunocompetent adult with a good response; 21 days if immunocompromised. (The U.S. label still lists 10 days — contemporary encephalitis guidance is 14–21.)
    • Response or complications may warrant specialist-guided extension.

    TRICARE — encephalitis prescribing

    IV acyclovir is inpatient ward stock — typically no prior authorization in the inpatient setting; outpatient oral acyclovir/valacyclovir are standard formulary generics. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — tailor by PCR; know when to re-tap

    • HSV PCR negative but suspicion high: continue acyclovir and repeat CSF HSV PCR in 3–7 days — an early negative (first days of illness) does not rule out HSV encephalitis.
    • HSV PCR positive and improving: consider repeat CSF PCR near end of therapy only if the response is inadequate or the course is complicated; if PCR stays positive, continue IV treatment and reassess with infectious diseases.
    • Routine end-of-treatment LP is not required (U.S. IDSA approach; UK guidance differs and favors an end-of-course LP with continued treatment while PCR stays positive).
    • Two negative PCRs with persistent suspicion: reassess CSF timing/quality, MRI, and EEG — then broaden to alternative infectious and autoimmune diagnoses with specialists.

    Brain abscess (adults)¶

    Urgent neurosurgical + ID assessment; blood cultures; abscess material for aerobic/anaerobic culture when feasible. Ceftriaxone is the backbone; metronidazole is never skipped; vancomycin and cefepime are scenario-specific additions.

    Step 1 — empiric antibiotics by presumed source

    • Sinusitis source: ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h + metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h. Add vancomycin if S. aureus/MRSA is plausible; assess the sinus focus for source control.
    • Otitis/mastoiditis source: ceftriaxone + metronidazole as above. If Pseudomonas is a credible concern (especially extension from otitis externa): cefepime 2 g IV q8h in place of ceftriaxone, retaining metronidazole; adjust for renal function.
    • Hematogenous source: ceftriaxone + metronidazole until the distant source is established; add vancomycin with bacteremia, endocarditis, or another staphylococcal source. Seek and treat the source — confirmed endocarditis gets organism-directed therapy, not automatic continuation of the full empiric regimen.
    • Metronidazole's role: anaerobic coverage with good abscess penetration; it does not replace the cephalosporin's aerobic coverage. Vancomycin is an addition for suspected staphylococcal infection, not a metronidazole substitute.
    • Do not delay treatment for sepsis, neurologic deterioration, impending herniation, or imminent rupture. (In a stable patient, ESCMID conditionally allows withholding antibiotics only if aspiration/excision happens promptly, preferably within 24 h, to improve diagnostic yield.)

    TRICARE — brain abscess prescribing

    IV ceftriaxone, cefepime, metronidazole, and vancomycin are inpatient ward stock — typically no prior authorization in the inpatient setting. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — duration, aspiration, excision

    • Duration: aspirated or medically managed bacterial abscess — 6–8 weeks of IV therapy, individualized to pathogen, drainage, course, and interval imaging. After complete excision, ~4 weeks; shorter courses are expert opinion only, not a rule.
    • Aspiration is preferred for most presumed bacterial abscesses: diagnostic pus, reduces the collection, reaches deep lesions with less tissue disruption (repeat aspiration may be needed). Get neurosurgical source control as soon as feasible.
    • Excision is considered for: superficial lesion in non-eloquent tissue, selected posterior-fossa lesions, difficult organisms (Nocardia, fungi), or a lesion not controlled by aspiration. No single size cutoff decides — mass effect, location, accessibility, diagnostic uncertainty, and response guide the choice.
    • Evidence is insufficient to recommend routine early oral step-down.

    Cryptococcal meningitis (HIV-positive adults)¶

    Three-phase therapy — induction, consolidation, maintenance — plus active pressure management with serial therapeutic LPs. Acetazolamide, mannitol, and routine steroids are not substitutes for CSF drainage.

    Step 1 — induction antifungals

    • Resource-rich setting (close inpatient monitoring): liposomal amphotericin B 3–4 mg/kg IV daily + flucytosine 25 mg/kg PO four times daily — for 2 weeks. Monitor renal function, blood counts, drug levels when available.
    • Resource-limited setting (AMBITION regimen): liposomal amphotericin B 10 mg/kg IV once on day 1 + flucytosine 25 mg/kg PO four times daily + fluconazole 1,200 mg PO daily for 14 days. The single amphotericin dose is not stand-alone — it depends on both oral drugs.
    • Adding flucytosine to amphotericin B gives faster fungal clearance and improves survival. Liposomal amphotericin B is preferred where resources allow (less nephrotoxicity); amphotericin B deoxycholate 0.7–1.0 mg/kg/day remains the mainstay where cost is limiting.
    • If the patient stays unstable or CSF culture stays positive, continue induction/intensified treatment rather than stepping down.

    TRICARE — cryptococcal meningitis prescribing

    IV liposomal amphotericin B and flucytosine are inpatient ward stock — typically no prior authorization in the inpatient setting; oral fluconazole is a standard formulary generic. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — manage the pressure with serial therapeutic LPs

    • Measure opening pressure at the diagnostic LP and at every subsequent LP. An opening pressure ≥25 cm H₂O is associated with more complications — but do not use 25 cm H₂O as a reason to withhold drainage from a symptomatic patient.
    • Treat headache or neurologic signs of raised pressure promptly: drain enough CSF to reduce pressure by at least half or to <20 cm H₂O.
    • Repeat therapeutic LPs at least daily while symptoms or raised pressure persist; consider an early repeat LP even after a lower initial reading — pressure can rise later.
    • No drainage destination is asserted here when repeated LPs fail or cannot be tolerated (source transcription was incomplete) — escalate to neurosurgery/ID for CSF-diversion planning.

    Step 3 — consolidation, maintenance, and ART timing

    • Consolidation: fluconazole 800 mg PO daily for at least 8 weeks; in a stable patient, NIH allows 400 mg daily once CSF cultures are sterile and ART has begun. Get a repeat LP with CSF fungal culture during/at the end of induction — a persistently positive cryptococcal antigen alone does not prove treatment failure.
    • Maintenance: fluconazole 200 mg PO daily for at least 1 year from the start of antifungal therapy. Stop only when the patient is asymptomatic + CD4 ≥100 cells/mm³ + suppressed HIV RNA on effective ART; restart if CD4 falls below 100.
    • ART timing — defer, don't rush: for a patient not already on ART, do not initiate immediately; NIH recommends deferring ART until 4–6 weeks after antifungals begin, individualized to clinical improvement, pressure control, and preferably CSF culture sterility. Early ART increased mortality (COAT trial) — the opposite of most OIs.

    TB meningitis (adults)¶

    A treatment emergency: start antituberculous therapy promptly after specimens are drawn — do not wait for smear, molecular, or culture confirmation.

    Finding Typical TBM pattern
    CSF Lymphocytic pleocytosis, high protein, low glucose
    Opening pressure Measure it; no validated TBM-specific cutoff — follow exam and imaging, not pressure alone
    Direct tests Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra is the preferred rapid initial test; send AFB smear (usually insensitive), mycobacterial culture (confirms + susceptibility)
    Yield tip ≥6 mL of CSF for TB testing improves yield; if CSF is scarce, prioritize the molecular test
    Negative tests Negative smear, Ultra/Xpert, or culture does NOT rule out TBM

    Step 1 — empiric anti-TB therapy + dexamethasone (a treatment emergency)

    • Drug-susceptible TBM: isoniazid + rifampin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol (HRZE) daily for 2 months, then isoniazid + rifampin (HR) for 7–10 months. Total 9–12 months; many experts use 12 months (2HRZE/10HR). Do not substitute shorter pulmonary-TB regimens. (Individual anti-TB drug mg doses were not captured in this run's research — dose per ATS/CDC/IDSA 2016 and local protocol.)
    • Adjunctive dexamethasone — start with TB treatment, taper over 6–8 weeks. Published severity-dependent schedules: Grade II–III: IV 0.3 mg/kg/day in week 1, IV 0.2 mg/kg/day in week 2, then oral 0.1 mg/kg/day in week 3, then oral 3, 2, and 1 mg/day in weeks 4–6; Grade I: IV 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1 mg/kg/day in successive weeks 1–4, then oral 4, 3, 2, and 1 mg/day in weeks 5–8. (Week-2/3 unit continuity inferred from the published schedule pattern — confirm against the British Infection Society guideline.) The survival benefit is clearest in people without HIV; in HIV-positive adults the benefit was not established, so steroid use there is an individualized discussion.
    • Seek respiratory/extraneural specimens; repeat LP only if safe when suspicion stays high — and never withhold empiric treatment while searching.

    TRICARE — TB meningitis prescribing

    First-line anti-TB drugs and dexamethasone are typically covered on TRICARE, but TB treatment is dispensed through public-health/TB-control channels in most settings — check the local TB program. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — pressure, hydrocephalus, and escalation

    • Do not perform serial LPs reflexively — there is no validated TBM-specific opening-pressure cutoff or mandatory serial-LP protocol. Follow the neuro exam and imaging.
    • If raised pressure is symptomatic and LP is safe, repeat therapeutic LPs may be considered with specialist input.
    • Urgent neurosurgical/neurocritical-care assessment for obstructive hydrocephalus, mass lesion, or herniation risk. Image for hydrocephalus, declining consciousness, or refractory intracranial hypertension — options include temporary ventricular drainage and, when needed, definitive CSF diversion.

    Neurosyphilis (adults)¶

    A clinical and laboratory diagnosis — never a serum titer or a single CSF result alone. In an adult with compatible neurologic findings (meningitis, cranial neuropathy, stroke, cognitive change): serum serology and LP.

    Test What it tells you
    Serum RPR or VDRL (quantitative) Baseline titer for follow-up
    Serum treponemal test (TP-PA or immunoassay) Confirms syphilis exposure; may stay reactive after treated disease — does not by itself establish active CNS disease
    CSF-VDRL Reactive = diagnostic (without substantial blood contamination) in a patient with neuro signs; highly specific but insensitive — a negative does NOT exclude neurosyphilis
    CSF-RPR Not an interchangeable, FDA-cleared substitute for CSF-VDRL
    CSF FTA-ABS / TP-PA Positive is less specific than CSF-VDRL; a negative makes neurosyphilis highly unlikely, especially with nonspecific symptoms
    CSF pleocytosis >5 WBC/mm³ is nonspecific; in HIV, >20 WBC/mm³ may improve specificity — not a stand-alone diagnostic rule

    Step 1 — high-dose IV penicillin for symptomatic neurosyphilis

    • Aqueous crystalline penicillin G 18–24 million units/day IV — as 3–4 million units every 4 hours or by continuous infusion — for 10–14 days. IM benzathine penicillin G alone does not replace this regimen.
    • Ocular and otic syphilis get the same regimen even when CSF is normal; confirmed isolated ocular or auditory disease with an otherwise normal neuro exam does not change the treatment. Arrange prompt ophthalmology or otolaryngology assessment respectively before treatment when possible.
    • Adjunctive corticosteroids: sometimes used for ocular/otic syphilis, but benefit has not been proven — not a routine substitute for, and never a reason to delay, penicillin. Any use for a specific inflammatory complication is individualized with the relevant specialist.

    TRICARE — neurosyphilis prescribing

    IV aqueous penicillin G is inpatient ward stock — typically no prior authorization in the inpatient setting. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Neurocysticercosis (adults)¶

    Diagnose and treat by lesion location, number, stage, and inflammation/obstruction. Seizures are the most common parenchymal presentation; late-onset epilepsy is a hallmark.

    Step 1 — classify with imaging + serology

    • MRI + noncontrast CT: CT detects small calcifications best; MRI (with 3D volumetric sequences) delineates viable cysts, scolex, edema, ventricular/subarachnoid disease.
    • Absolute criteria: a visible scolex within a cyst on CT/MRI; histologic parasite identification; direct visualization of a subretinal cysticercus.
    • Serum T. solium EITB/immunoblot is the preferred confirmatory assay (more sensitive in serum for parenchymal disease, in CSF for extraparenchymal). Negative does NOT exclude a single lesion or calcified-only disease; antibody positivity does not prove parasites are viable. Antigen assays (serum/CSF, specialized lab) support viable cysticerci and help monitor subarachnoid disease.
    • Funduscopic exam before anthelminthics to exclude intraocular cysticerci.

    Step 2 — anthelminthics + steroids (guideline regimens, not a patient-specific prescription)

    • 1–2 viable parenchymal cysts, no elevated ICP: albendazole 15 mg/kg/day PO in two divided doses with food, max 1,200 mg/day, for 10–14 days.
    • >2 viable parenchymal cysts (no elevated ICP): albendazole 15 mg/kg/day + praziquantel 50 mg/kg/day PO (praziquantel generally in three daily doses) for 10–14 days — dual therapy beats monotherapy for cyst resolution. (Duration placement inferred from the guideline table layout — re-confirm against IDSA/ASTMH 2018.) Praziquantel monotherapy is less effective and not preferred.
    • Single enhancing lesion: albendazole 15 mg/kg/day with meals for 1–2 weeks is suggested (weaker evidence).
    • Subarachnoid disease: anthelminthics plus high-dose anti-inflammatory therapy, often far longer than parenchymal courses (can exceed a year) — specialist individualization.
    • Steroids start BEFORE the cysticidal drugs whenever antiparasitic therapy is planned. No universal dose: published parenchymal trial regimens include dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg/day and 0.2 mg/kg/day; subarachnoid disease may use dexamethasone 6 mg/day in divided doses for 10 days as a possible regimen (dosing not standardized). Screen for Strongyloides and latent TB when prolonged steroids are likely; monitor CBC and liver enzymes on albendazole courses >14 days.
    • Killing cysticerci can transiently worsen inflammation and neurologic symptoms — control seizures, edema, ICP, and hydrocephalus first.

    TRICARE — neurocysticercosis prescribing

    Albendazole and praziquantel are specialty outpatient agents — may carry tier or prior authorization requirements. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 3 — when to avoid or defer anthelminthics

    • Diffuse cerebral edema / cysticercal encephalitis: do NOT start cysticidal therapy — it can precipitate fatal herniation. Steroids + ICP control instead.
    • Untreated hydrocephalus: contraindication — CSF diversion or surgery first.
    • Intraocular cysticerci: avoid anthelminthics — surgical removal, not antiparasitic drugs; treatment-induced inflammation can threaten vision.
    • Calcified-only lesions: no viable parasite — do NOT give anthelminthics; treat symptoms (including seizures).
    • Removable intraventricular cysts: favor neurosurgical removal; avoid preoperative anthelminthics (can inflame/disrupt the cyst).
    • Spinal NCC is not a blanket contraindication — medical or surgical per cord dysfunction, cyst location, arachnoiditis, and operative feasibility; evaluate cord compromise before starting anthelminthics.
    • Antiparasitic treatment is never an emergency — seizures and ICP come first.

    Autoimmune encephalitis — workup and treatment (adults)¶

    Suspect AE with subacute (<3 months) working-memory loss, altered behavior/psychiatric symptoms, new seizures, movement disorder, speech dysfunction, catatonia, dysautonomia, or central hypoventilation. Faciobrachial dystonic seizures point toward LGI1. Normal MRI does not exclude AE. Status epilepticus, hypoventilation, or severe dysautonomia warrants monitored inpatient care and early neuroimmunology involvement.

    Phenotype often points to the antibody:

    Phenotype clue Think
    Days-onset viral-type prodrome → early psychiatric features → seizures, movement disorders (orofacial dyskinesias, chorea/dystonia), dysautonomia/hypoventilation NMDA-R
    >40 y, weeks-onset faciobrachial dystonic seizures + amnesia, disorientation, hyponatremia LGI1 (most common adult subtype)
    Males >60 y, limbic features, neuromyotonia, Morvan syndrome CASPR2
    Limbic encephalitis with prominent early seizures (strong SCLC association) GABA-B-R
    Refractory seizures/status with multifocal MRI lesions GABA-A-R
    Limbic encephalitis AMPA-R
    Insidious, often paraneoplastic, poorer immunotherapy response Intracellular/onconeural: Hu, Yo, Ri, Ma2, CV2/CRMP5, amphiphysin, GAD65

    Diagnostic criteria (Graus 2016 — syndrome-specific, not one universal scale)¶

    Classification Key requirements
    Possible AE <3 months of working-memory deficit, altered mental status, or psychiatric symptoms; ≥1 of: new focal CNS finding, otherwise-unexplained seizure, CSF pleocytosis (>5 WBC/mm³), or MRI suggestive of encephalitis; reasonable exclusion of alternatives
    Probable antibody-negative AE Same subacute core; no better-defined AE syndrome; no well-characterized antibodies in serum and CSF; ≥2 of: suggestive MRI, inflammatory CSF (pleocytosis, CSF-specific oligoclonal bands, elevated IgG index), or inflammatory brain biopsy; exclusion of alternatives
    Probable anti-NMDAR encephalitis Rapid onset of ≥4 of 6 groups — behavior/cognition, speech, seizures, movement, consciousness, autonomic dysfunction/hypoventilation — plus abnormal EEG or inflammatory CSF; exclusion of alternatives (3 groups with a systemic teratoma can also qualify)
    Definite AE Syndrome-specific: definite anti-NMDAR requires a compatible symptom group + IgG anti-GluN1 in CSF with alternatives excluded; definite autoimmune limbic encephalitis can be diagnosed without an antibody — subacute limbic symptoms + bilateral medial-temporal T2/FLAIR changes + temporal EEG abnormality or CSF pleocytosis + exclusion of alternatives

    A negative panel alone does not establish "probable seronegative AE"; conversely, absence of an antibody does not exclude clinically well-supported disease.

    Antibody panels — paired serum AND CSF¶

    • Cell-surface/synaptic: NMDA-R (GluN1), LGI1, CASPR2, AMPA-R, GABA-A-R, GABA-B-R, DPPX, mGluR5, GlyR, dopamine-2 receptor.
    • Astrocytic/demyelinating: AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG, GFAP-IgG — phenotype-directed (optic neuritis, myelitis, ADEM-like, cortical/brainstem lesions), not routine AE-panel substitutes.
    • Intracellular/onconeural (paraneoplastic): GAD65, Hu (ANNA-1), Yo (PCA-1), Ri (ANNA-2), Ma2, CV2/CRMP5, amphiphysin, SOX1, Tr, KLHL11.
    • CSF is more sensitive/specific for NMDA-R; LGI1 sensitivity is often higher in serum — test both. Commercial kits carry a high false-positive rate, especially serum-only (~1% of control sera); confirm with a second method in a reference lab when the picture is unclear.
    • Support with CSF analysis (oligoclonal bands, IgG index, antibody-specific indices) and routine infectious exclusion (HSV, VZV, enterovirus PCR).

    Step 1 — first-line immunotherapy (start at the probable stage — do not wait for antibodies)

    • IV methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days — a first-line option after appropriate infectious evaluation; often followed by an oral taper with PJP and osteoporosis prophylaxis.
    • IVIG 2 g/kg total IV over 2–5 days (commonly 0.4 g/kg/day × 5) — combine with steroids in severe disease; preferred when steroids are poorly tolerated.
    • Plasma exchange (PLEX): 5 exchanges on alternate days over ~10 days (up to 5–10 in published protocols) — alternative to IVIG alongside steroids in severe disease; do it before monoclonal B-cell therapies (PLEX depletes them).
    • For severe AE, combination first-line therapy (steroids + IVIG or PLEX) is recommended in the Canadian adult consensus.

    TRICARE — autoimmune encephalitis prescribing

    IV methylprednisolone and PLEX are inpatient procedures/ward stock — typically no prior authorization; IVIG and rituximab are specialty agents that may carry tier or prior authorization requirements. Verify current status at the TRICARE Formulary Search — DoD P&T revises tiers.

    Step 2 — second-line immunotherapy (escalate when no response after ~2–4 weeks of optimized first-line)

    • Rituximab — preferred second-line (chosen by ~80% of experts; favored for neuronal cell-surface antibody disease): 1,000 mg IV × 2 doses 14 days apart, OR 375 mg/m² IV weekly for 4 weeks.
    • Cyclophosphamide — favored for suspected T-cell–mediated/classical paraneoplastic disease, with specialist/oncology input; the optimal regimen is not established. Published regimens: 600–1,000 mg/m² IV monthly for 3–6 months, or 15 mg/kg IV every 3 weeks for 4–6 months. Fertility concerns apply; monitor toxicity.
    • Reassess objective response and the diagnosis before second-line treatment.
    • Refractory/third-line options (plasma-cell or cytokine directed): bortezomib, tocilizumab, daratumumab; emerging agents (inebilizumab, ofatumumab, efgartigimod, CAR-T).

    Step 3 — tumor screening (at the initial evaluation — do not wait for antibody results)

    • NMDA-R: ovarian teratoma in women (malignancy in up to ~40–47%) — pelvic ultrasound/MRI, with follow-up imaging; tumor removal accelerates recovery.
    • Modality approach: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis; if negative, whole-body FDG-PET; mammography ± breast MRI; testicular ultrasound in men (especially Ma2); PET does not replace dedicated pelvic imaging for a possible teratoma.
    • High-risk/onconeural antibodies (Hu, CV2/CRMP5, Ma2, amphiphysin, GABA-B-R/SCLC): if initial screening is negative, repeat surveillance every 3–6 months (some recommend 6–12 months) for 3–5 years; intensify with older age, smoking, or cancer history.
    • The 2021 paraneoplastic criteria specify every 3–6 months for at least 2 years for a high-risk phenotype, every 4–6 months for 2 years with a high-risk antibody.

    Disposition¶

    • Admit: altered mentation, seizures, focal deficit, elevated opening pressure, respiratory compromise, suspected HSV/TBM, or any patient needing IV antimicrobials, serial LPs, or immunotherapy — this is overwhelmingly an inpatient disease family.
    • ICU: status epilepticus, refractory ICP, hydrocephalus, severe dysautonomia or hypoventilation (AE), posterior-fossa abscess, or TBM with declining consciousness.
    • Follow-up: repeat LPs (cryptococcal pressure/culture, TBM safety), interval imaging (abscess, NCC), serologic follow-up (neurosyphilis titers, cryptococcal consolidation), tumor surveillance (AE/paraneoplastic), and seizure/rehab planning.

    Military medicine — neuroinfections

    Framework — verify against current AR 40-501, DoDI 6130.03, DoDI 1332.18 before counseling; aeromedical/special-duty policy is service-specific.

    • Deployability: active CNS infection and its prolonged antimicrobial/immunotherapy course are deployment-limiting until resolved; residual deficits (seizures, cognitive impairment, hydrocephalus with shunt) can be deployment-limiting. Consider environment: fluconazole maintenance, anti-TB therapy, and IVIG/rituximab are not maintainable downrange; albendazole/praziquantel courses are short but require follow-up imaging.
    • Profile (DA Form 3349): temporary profile during acute illness and recovery; permanent profile for residual deficits (seizures, focal weakness, cognitive impairment, shunt). Functional limits: no driving/heights/field duty during seizure treatment, ICP issues, or immunotherapy toxicity monitoring.
    • EPTS / LOD: document onset circumstances; line-of-duty determination matters for benefits and ongoing care. Pre-service infections with residual deficits fall under EPTS rules — document the baseline.
    • Retention (AR 40-501 / DoDI 6130.03): recurrent seizures, uncontrolled infection, permanent neurologic deficit, or shunt dependence triggers retention-standard review — cite the applicable chapter/paragraph before counseling.
    • MEB / IDES: refer when the condition or its residuals fail retention standards or prevent duty performance; documentation that carries a board: admission records, serial neuro exams, imaging, antibody/serology results, seizure logs, and treatment course.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    • Steroids ride with the first antibiotic dose in bacterial meningitis (de Gans/van de Beek) — and stop when CSF says "not bacterial." NICE and WHO durations differ (10 vs 7 days); they are not interchangeable.
    • Treat HSV encephalitis for 14–21 days, not the 10-day label, and re-tap in 3–7 days when PCR is negative but suspicion stays high.
    • Brain abscess: ceftriaxone + metronidazole is the backbone in every scenario; the source picks the third agent (cefepime for credible Pseudomonas, vancomycin for staph). Six to eight weeks IV; aspiration over excision for most.
    • Cryptococcal meningitis: the AMBITION single–high-dose liposomal amphotericin B regimen changed resource-limited induction; pressure is managed with serial therapeutic LPs, not acetazolamide; and ART is deferred 4–6 weeks — the opposite of most OIs.
    • TB meningitis is a treatment emergency — Xpert Ultra plus empiric HRZE, with a 6–8 week dexamethasone taper whose survival benefit is clearest without HIV.
    • Neurocysticercosis: albendazole + praziquantel beats monotherapy for >2 viable cysts; steroids start before the cysticidal drugs; never treat through uncontrolled ICP or hydrocephalus.
    • Autoimmune encephalitis: start immunotherapy at the probable stage on paired serum+CSF panels; a negative panel neither proves seronegative AE nor excludes well-supported disease; screen for tumor at presentation, not after the antibodies return.
