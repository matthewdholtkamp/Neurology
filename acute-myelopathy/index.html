    Acute myelopathy¶

    Last reviewed: November 2026

    One-liner: acute spinal cord dysfunction — a syndrome, not a diagnosis. The first job is to exclude a compressive, vascular, or infectious cause that needs a surgeon or antibiotics now; only then does it become an inflammatory-myelitis problem. For the on-call provider seeing new weakness, a sensory level, or sphincter dysfunction: image the cord first, and do not give empiric steroids for "transverse myelitis" before the MRI.

    🚩 Red flags — escalate immediately

    Pattern Worry Do now
    Rapidly progressive weakness, new sphincter dysfunction Compressive lesion needing surgical decompression Emergent MRI whole spine; neurosurgery/spine surgery now
    Fever, IV drug use, bacteremia, recent spinal instrumentation, CRP often >100 mg/L, ESR >60 mm/h Spinal epidural abscess Blood cultures, then empiric antibiotics immediately; emergent decompression if deficit
    Known cancer with new back pain (pain precedes myelopathy by weeks in most patients) Malignant spinal cord compression Dexamethasone on suspicion, even before imaging; surgery and/or radiotherapy
    Anticoagulation, coagulopathy, or recent procedure/surgery Epidural hematoma Reverse coagulopathy; urgent decompression
    Apoplectic onset with tearing back or chest pain Aortic dissection or spinal cord infarction Aorta imaging (CTA if dissection possible); support perfusion
    Ascending level in a cervical lesion, respiratory compromise Rising cervical edema, ventilatory failure ICU; monitor vital capacity; airway plan
    Worsening after steroids Dural AV fistula, or lymphoma steroids may obscure Stop and rethink; spinal angiography; do not re-dose blindly

    Diagnosis / classification¶

    Tempo is the first classifier — and it beats any single test:

    Tempo Think first
    Hyperacute — nadir in minutes to hours Spinal cord infarction, epidural hematoma, aortic dissection
    Acute/subacute — nadir in hours to days, under 3 weeks Inflammatory myelitis (MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, sarcoid, SLE/Sjögren, Behçet), infectious myelopathy, epidural abscess
    Progressive beyond 3 weeks Tumor, compression, dural AV fistula, metabolic causes, sarcoid — transverse myelitis is unlikely here

    Anatomic compartment on MRI is the second classifier: an extradural or intradural extramedullary process deforming the cord is compressive until proven otherwise; an intramedullary lesion is inflammatory, infectious, vascular, or neoplastic. Categories overlap — an epidural abscess is both infectious and compressive.

    Syndromes (localize before you image): complete cord syndrome, anterior cord syndrome (dissociated sensory loss — pain/temperature lost, vibration/proprioception spared), Brown-Séquard syndrome (ipsilateral weakness + proprioception loss, contralateral pain/temperature loss), central cord syndrome, and conus/cauda equina syndrome. Early cord injury is often flaccid and areflexic — neither absent hyperreflexia nor an initially indistinct sensory level excludes myelopathy, and flaccidity alone is not Guillain–Barré syndrome.

    Pitfalls that mislead: enhancement, CSF pleocytosis, and raised protein do not reliably separate myelitis from mimics — spondylotic myelopathy can show pleocytosis in 12.5% of cases and enhancement in up to 80%. Spinal cord infarction makes up 14–16% of cases labeled "transverse myelitis," and an early MRI can be normal in infarction.

    Rapid approach¶

    1. Stabilize and localize. Protect ventilation (cervical lesions) and perfusion — avoid hypotension, especially if infarction is possible. Record a reproducible baseline: exact onset and time to worst deficit, strength in all four limbs, tone, reflexes, plantars, sensory level by pinprick/light touch plus vibration/proprioception, perineal sensation, voluntary anal contraction, and bladder/bowel symptoms. Re-examine serially, including before and after transport or intervention.
    2. Image emergently — assume compression until proven otherwise. MRI of the spine without and with IV contrast, centered on the clinical level; image the whole spine if cancer is suspected, because compression is often multilevel. If MRI is impossible, use CT myelography. Notify neurology early and involve neurosurgery/spine surgery immediately when compression, hematoma, instability, or rapidly progressive deficits are suspected — those discussions proceed while the workup continues. If MRI or spinal surgery is unavailable, arrange urgent transfer.
    3. Labs before MRI (do not let them delay it): CBC, ESR/CRP, blood cultures if any infection risk, coagulation studies, glucose, electrolytes. B12/copper come later for subacute cases.
    4. Treat the cause, in cause order: compression → surgery/steroids/antibiotics per Step 1 below; infarction → perfusion + aorta; fistula → angiography, never steroids; inflammatory → IVMP, then PLEX early where indicated.
    5. Support and dispose: bladder scan/catheter for retention, respiratory monitoring (vital capacity) in cervical lesions, DVT prophylaxis once bleeding risk allows, spinal cord unit, and rehabilitation once stable.

    The prescriptive flow (what I want done, in order)¶

    1. ABCs + neuro baseline + sensory level (above). Admit; ICU for high cervical lesions or rising edema.
    2. Emergent MRI whole spine (with contrast) — plus brain MRI with gadolinium once compression is excluded, and orbits if there are visual symptoms.
    3. If compressive, infectious, or vascular — treat that first (Step 1). Obtain urgent MRI to exclude compression/hematoma and assess infection/vascular mimics before empiric myelitis steroids. MRI alone cannot exclude early infarction or every DAVF; pursue DWI/repeat imaging/angiography when indicated without creating an unnecessary delay in a convincing inflammatory emergency. Malignant compression is the exception: steroids start on suspicion.
    4. If inflammatory — IVMP 1,000 mg IV daily × 3–5 days (Step 2), with disease-specific escalation and taper (table below). In NMOSD, do not wait: add PLEX early, ideally within 2 days.
    5. Second-tier workup in parallel once compression is excluded (CSF, antibodies, systemic screen — see Workup).
    6. Supportive care + disposition + rehabilitation (see Disposition). Re-image and reconsider the diagnosis if the course breaks the tempo rules — progression beyond 3 weeks, or enhancement that persists, points away from demyelinating myelitis, toward sarcoidosis in particular.

    Workup¶

    Imaging: MRI spine without and with IV contrast (whole spine if cancer suspected); MRI brain with gadolinium; orbits if visual symptoms. Spinal angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for dural AV fistula. CTA of the aorta if dissection is possible. CT myelography only if MRI is impossible. For infarction: DWI — restricted diffusion appears before T2 change, in an anterior or watershed distribution, with no early enhancement; early enhancement argues against infarction. Associated vertebral body infarction or an adjacent arterial dissection/occlusion supports infarction.

    CSF (after imaging excludes compression): cell count and differential, protein, glucose, paired oligoclonal bands/IgG index, cytology. Add Gram stain and culture plus viral PCR (HSV, VZV, enterovirus) when infection is possible.

    Serum antibodies — cell-based assays, drawn before steroids/PLEX where feasible: AQP4-IgG by live cell-based assay (sensitivity >80%, specificity >99%; ELISA can give false-positive low titers — confirm on a cell-based assay if the picture is atypical; false negatives occur during immunosuppression or after PLEX, so retest off treatment or during a relapse; CSF AQP4 testing adds little) and MOG-IgG (live assays more specific than fixed; low-positive titers require phenotype/MRI support and exclusion of a better diagnosis; titers fluctuate and can be negative outside attacks in up to 57% of patients). Coexistence of both antibodies is rare.

    Systemic screen: ANA, ENA (SSA/SSB), dsDNA, antiphospholipid antibodies, ACE, chest CT or PET for sarcoidosis, B12/copper (a normal B12 does not exclude deficiency — check MMA and homocysteine; nitrous oxide inactivates B12), HIV, syphilis; paraneoplastic panel if suspected. For infarction: echocardiography, lipids, diabetes workup.

    Inflammatory myelitis — MS vs NMOSD vs MOGAD¶

    The three diseases that dominate inflammatory myelitis, distinguished by lesion length and position, CSF oligoclonal bands, and cell-based antibody assays. Full disease pages: NMOSD, MOGAD, MS.

    2015 NMOSD criteria (Wingerchuk et al.): AQP4-IgG positive — one core clinical characteristic plus exclusion of other diagnoses is enough; a single attack can establish the diagnosis. AQP4-IgG negative or unknown — two or more different core characteristics (dissemination in space; dissemination in time is not required), at least one of which must be optic neuritis, myelitis with LETM, or area postrema syndrome with a dorsal medulla lesion, plus the additional MRI requirements, and exclusion of alternatives. Up to 40% of patients who were AQP4-negative turn out MOG-IgG positive and are now classified as MOGAD.

    2023 international MOGAD panel criteria — all three required: (1) one core clinical event — optic neuritis, myelitis, ADEM, cerebral monofocal or polyfocal deficits, brainstem/cerebellar deficits, or cortical encephalitis; (2) positive MOG-IgG on a cell-based assay — a clear positive (fixed-assay titer ≥1:100, or live-assay positive at its standard cutoff) needs no further support, while a low-positive, titer-unavailable, or CSF-only result requires at least one supporting clinical or MRI feature; (3) exclusion of better explanations, especially MS. For MS, apply the McDonald criteria once a typical clinically isolated syndrome is established and alternatives are unlikely.

    Feature MS AQP4+ NMOSD MOGAD
    Cord lesion length Under 1 segment, rarely more than 3; often several LETM (≥3 segments) LETM common; short lesions also occur
    Axial / location Peripheral (dorsal/lateral), wedge-shaped; cervical Central, cord swelling, bright spotty lesions; cervicothoracic Gray matter only (H-sign); conus/caudal cord
    Enhancement / evolution Focal or ring enhancement Prominent, sometimes ring; lesions persist and cause atrophy Often none; lesions often resolve
    Brain MRI Periventricular, juxtacortical, Dawson fingers, inferior temporal lesions Near ventricles and aqueduct, hypothalamus, dorsal medulla ADEM-like fluffy lesions, deep gray matter, cerebellar peduncles, leptomeningeal enhancement
    CSF Oligoclonal bands 90–95% Oligoclonal bands 20–30%; pleocytosis ~35%, sometimes neutrophils/eosinophils Oligoclonal bands 10–20%; pleocytosis ~35%
    Demographics / course Relapsing, can progress Female:male ~9:1; severe attacks, incomplete recovery Female:male ~1:1; ~50% monophasic; better recovery

    Other inflammatory causes:

    Cause Cord MRI Distinguishing clues
    Neurosarcoidosis LETM (short lesions less often); long-lasting subpial/leptomeningeal enhancement, "trident sign" (dorsal subpial + central canal enhancement, axial) Subacute or progressive course beyond 3 weeks; low CSF glucose, raised CSF ACE, sometimes normal CSF; systemic disease
    SLE LETM with swelling, can span whole cord and medulla; gray-matter vs white-matter myelitis forms Antiphospholipid-related myelopathy (thoracic, patchy, little enhancement); check AQP4 — overlap occurs
    Sjögren syndrome LETM, mostly cervical, can extend to medulla; confluent, enhancing Frequently overlaps with AQP4+ NMOSD; sicca/parotid/systemic disease, neuropathy; anti-SSA positivity alone does not prove Sjögren caused the lesion; no validated pathognomonic Sjögren MRI signature
    Behçet disease Cervical or thoracic LETM (short lesions less often); "bagel sign" (T2 center darker than a bright rim); enhancement uncommon Recurrent oral/genital ulcers, uveitis/retinal vasculitis, characteristic skin disease; CSF neutrophilic, lymphocytic, or mixed pleocytosis, OCBs usually absent; HLA-B51 is neither diagnostic nor exclusionary

    Management¶

    Step 1 — treat the surgical, infectious, or vascular cause first (before any myelitis therapy)

    • Malignant spinal cord compression with neurological symptoms/signs — dexamethasone 16 mg PO (or equivalent parenteral dose) promptly, then 16 mg/day while awaiting surgery/radiotherapy (NICE NG234). Start on suspicion, even before imaging; taper after definitive treatment. Stop if compression/metastasis is excluded. Monitor glucose and provide gastric protection. Steroids are a bridge to surgery and/or radiotherapy. In unexplained epidural lesions, possible lymphoma is a reason for caution — hold steroids if lymphoma is possible and the patient is stable enough to wait for tissue, because chemosensitive tumors are managed differently.
    • Spinal epidural abscess — blood cultures first, then vancomycin + ceftriaxone immediately. Use cefepime instead of ceftriaxone for people who inject drugs, surgical-site infection, sepsis, or substantial neurologic deficits. Dosing (verify renal function; pharmacy to dose): vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV q8–12h by actual body weight in normal renal function; cefepime 2 g IV q8h when CrCl >60 mL/min, reduced or interval-extended in renal impairment. Urgent decompression for new/progressive deficits or significant compression — consult immediately. Do not wait 48–72 hours; neurological status and delay to source control affect recovery. Withholding antibiotics until image-guided biopsy is reasonable only when the patient is neurologically intact, not septic, has no cord compression on MRI, and can be closely monitored.
    • Epidural hematoma — reverse the coagulopathy and decompress urgently.
    • Disc herniation / spondylosis (the commonest extrinsic compression, mostly cervical) — decompression per spine surgery; contributing factors include malalignment, instability, and a congenitally narrow canal.
    • Spinal cord infarction — support perfusion and find the cause. No established universal reperfusion/antithrombotic regimen. Use neurology/vascular assessment and the suspected mechanism; an observational aspirin-plus-LMWH regimen is not a standard prescription. Evaluate the aorta (CTA if dissection possible). Avoid hypotension.
    • Dural AV fistula — do NOT give steroids (rapid deterioration in about half of patients). DSA is the gold standard; treatment is embolization or surgical disconnection. Surgery is permanent; embolization is not possible if the feeder also supplies the anterior spinal artery.

    → Urgent imaging and clinical assessment support inflammatory myelitis, with compression excluded and infection/vascular mimics addressed → Step 2.

    Step 2 — inflammatory myelitis: IVMP, then disease-specific escalation and taper

    • IVMP — methylprednisolone 1,000 mg IV daily for 3–5 days (children: 30 mg/kg/day, maximum 1 g). Equivalent high-dose oral steroids may work about as well as IV.
    • AQP4+ NMOSD — IVMP plus early or upfront PLEX for myelitis; myelitis responded better to PLEX than to IVMP in one study. Start PLEX first-line or within days, ideally within 2 days. Then prednisone 1 mg/kg/day, tapered to 10–15 mg/day over 2–3 weeks, with low-dose maintenance for 3–6 months to prevent early relapse until long-term immunotherapy takes effect. Untreated NMOSD attacks leave only 21.6% fully recovered — do not wait.
    • MOGAD — IVMP; add early PLEX for severe or inadequately responding attacks. Rescue with PLEX or IVIG (IVIG 2 g/kg total over 2–5 days); apheresis within 4.5 days improved recovery, and early escalation was linked to fewer relapses in children. Slow taper over up to 6 months is common — relapses cluster below about 10 mg prednisone and within 2 months of stopping. MOGAD usually responds well to steroids.
    • MS — IVMP alone in about 93% of cases; usually no taper. PLEX for severe attacks that do not respond to steroids. MS attacks are usually milder than NMOSD attacks.
    • PLEX regimen — about 5 exchanges, daily or every other day; up to 10 have been used (pediatric reviews describe 5–7 exchanges over 2 weeks). Timing matters most: earlier is better, and in steroid-refractory cases PLEX gave moderate to marked improvement in 42% vs 6% with sham.
    • IVIG — 2 g/kg total over 2–5 days, as rescue when steroids fail and as an alternative to PLEX mainly in MOGAD and children. Its role in adult NMOSD or MS myelitis is limited.

    Step 3 — mimics and refractory disease

    • B12 / copper deficiency myelopathy — replace urgently; it halts or improves with replacement, and delayed treatment leaves residual deficits. Check MMA and homocysteine when B12 is normal, and copper, ceruloplasmin, and zinc (zinc excess, e.g. denture cream, drives copper deficiency). B12 deficiency shows dorsal-column sensory ataxia and spasticity, often with neuropathy, and symmetric posterior or lateral column T2 signal, usually thoracic, rarely enhancing. Copper deficiency looks identical. Replacement dosing was not pinned in this research round — use local protocol and verify before prescribing.
    • Infectious myelopathy — HSV, VZV, CMV, EBV, West Nile virus, Borrelia, TB, syphilis, histoplasma, HIV, HTLV-1. Clues: fever, immunosuppression, CSF pleocytosis, positive serology/PCR. Treat the organism; do not anchor on steroids.
    • Sarcoid / SLE / Sjögren / Behçet — disease-specific workup and rheumatology; see the table above. Worsening beyond 3 weeks or persistently enhancing lesions should re-open the sarcoid question.
    • Other mimics to exclude: intramedullary neoplasm or lymphoma, paraneoplastic myelopathy (tract-specific lesions; Hu, CRMP5, amphiphysin antibodies), delayed radiation myelopathy, hematomyelia, hereditary myeloneuropathies (chronic presentations).
    • Refractory inflammatory myelitis — if appropriately delivered therapies repeatedly fail, reconsider the diagnosis before adding more immunotherapy.

    Avoid

    • Empiric steroids for "transverse myelitis" before MRI — compression, abscess, hematoma, and ischemia must be excluded first (malignant compression on suspicion is the exception). Steroids can blur the diagnosis and, in DAVF, cause rapid, lasting worsening.
    • MS disease-modifying drugs in NMOSD — interferon-β, natalizumab, fingolimod and other S1P modulators, alemtuzumab, and dimethyl fumarate have been reported to trigger severe attacks. Do not start an MS DMT until AQP4-IgG is back. Glatiramer acetate is a different case: it is ineffective at preventing NMOSD attacks rather than a reported exacerbation trigger.
    • Anchoring on enhancement or CSF pleocytosis to call something myelitis — see Pitfalls above.

    TRICARE — verified rules and the current form

    Urgent MRI, surgery and inpatient rescue treatment should not wait for routine outpatient PA. Continuing immune treatment, outpatient PLEX/infusions and rehabilitation require their own benefit/site review. Use the cause-specific diagnosis and evidence; “myelopathy” alone does not establish every drug indication.

    Disposition¶

    Admit with neurosurgical access; ICU for high cervical lesions or rising edema. Neuro checks must include respiratory function (vital capacity) in cervical lesions. Transfer early if surgery is unavailable. After stabilization, a dedicated spinal cord unit plans care for respiration, mobility, voiding and bowel, spasticity, autonomic dysfunction, and pressure-injury prevention.

    • Bladder and bowel: check the post-void residual or place an indwelling catheter for retention in the acute phase; move to intermittent catheterization (or suprapubic catheter when needed) plus a scheduled laxative and bowel program. (Specific PVR thresholds and timing of catheter removal were not addressed in the sources used here.)

    • DVT prophylaxis: use mechanical prophylaxis while bleeding risk prohibits anticoagulant prophylaxis, then start LMWH when the spine/surgical team judges it safe. Adjust for renal function, weight, procedures, and bleeding. Select duration with the spinal cord rehabilitation team according to mobility and VTE risk; do not prescribe an extended course from an incompletely captured research regimen.

    • Rehabilitation: start mobilization and rehabilitation once the patient is medically and surgically stable, ideally during the acute admission in a spinal cord unit, then move to secondary rehabilitation. Specific timing trials for non-traumatic myelopathy are limited.

    Cause Prognosis
    Epidural abscess Emergency spine/ID assessment and prompt source control; do not use a 48–72h window as permission to wait
    Metastatic compression Prompt treatment prevents paralysis; function before treatment drives outcome
    Spinal cord infarction Outcome varies with initial severity and cause; substantial late recovery is possible. Avoid applying a single cohort’s outcome percentage to every patient
    Dural AV fistula Treatable; strength and gait improve more than pain or sphincter function; delay causes permanent deficit
    B12 / copper deficiency Halts or improves with replacement; delayed treatment leaves residual deficits

    Military medicine — duty, retention and deployment

    • Profile / function: A new cord syndrome requires urgent care/transfer. Subsequently document mobility, hand function, bladder/bowel, sensation and respiratory support.
    • Retention / MEB–MAR2: Retention depends on cause, residual function and recovery; inflammatory demyelination, trauma, tumor and permanent paralysis invoke different standards. Use the retention framework; permanent P3/4 does not by itself select DES versus MAR2.
    • Deployment / medications: Assess recurrence, need for assistance, autonomic risk and access to rehabilitation/specialty care. Immunotherapy and anticoagulation create separate medication issues. See the disease AND drug screen for the current CENTCOM rules and waiver packet.
    • Special duty: flight, diving and other special assignments have separate standards and waiver authorities; a general duty profile does not clear them.
    • LOD / EPTS: document onset, duty status, exposures and aggravation objectively. The designated authority determines LOD/EPTS; neither in-service diagnosis nor a genetic finding settles it automatically.

    📚 Discover — evidence brief¶

    Written from primary guidelines and trials; source articles are a reading list, not copied.

    • Compression first, always. The 2026 ACR Appropriateness Criteria update keeps extrinsic cord compression as the first thing to exclude in acute-onset myelopathy, with MRI of the spine without and with IV contrast as the key study — and the whole spine when cancer is suspected, because multilevel compression is common.
    • Tempo + compartment beats any single test. Hyperacute onset points vascular; hours-to-days under 3 weeks points inflammatory; beyond 3 weeks it is almost never transverse myelitis. Enhancement and CSF pleocytosis do not separate myelitis from spondylotic mimics.
    • NMOSD is the time-critical inflammatory myelitis. Untreated attacks leave only 21.6% fully recovered, and PLEX belongs first-line or within days — ideally within 2 — not as a salvage after weeks of steroids. MOGAD is the mirror image: steroid-responsive, slow taper over up to 6 months, relapses clustering below ~10 mg prednisone.
    • Steroids are dangerous in the wrong myelopathy. Empiric steroids before MRI risk masking lymphoma and worsening a dural AV fistula (rapid deterioration in about half); malignant cord compression is the one setting where steroids start on suspicion.
    • Antibody assay quality decides the diagnosis. Live cell-based AQP4-IgG (sensitivity

      80%, specificity >99%) and MOG-IgG with the ≥1:100 clear-positive threshold — ELISA low titers and off-attack MOG titers mislead in both directions.

    • Supportive care is outcome care. Bladder/bowel programs, LMWH within 72 hours when safe, a spinal cord unit, and rehabilitation starting once stable — with prognosis set by cause and by function before treatment, especially in metastatic compression and infarction (severity at nadir strongly influences recovery; a single cohort percentage is not a universal prognosis).
